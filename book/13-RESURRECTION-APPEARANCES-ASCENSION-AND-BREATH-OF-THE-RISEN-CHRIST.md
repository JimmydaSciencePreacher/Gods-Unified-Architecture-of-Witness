# Chapter 13 — Resurrection, Appearances, Ascension, and the Breath of the Risen Christ

## The Resurrection is not simply another sign

The preceding chapter ended with the death and burial of Jesus Christ. The Gospel testimony now moves beyond the tomb to the announcement that He is risen, the encounters with the risen Jesus, His Ascension, and the promised coming of the Holy Ghost.

The Resurrection receives a central place in the New Testament proclamation. It is not merely one more extraordinary work in a catalogue. Scripture distinguishes Jesus' raising of others from His own Resurrection, joins His death and burial to the proclamation that He rose, and identifies Him as the firstfruits of them that slept.

The governing order of GUA-W remains:

**Author → Word → Creation → Witness**

God alone is Original Cause. GUA-W is the map of the One Creation; it is not God and is not Jesus Christ. Scripture identifies Jesus as the Word made flesh, the Son, and the One through whom all things were made (John 1:1–3, 14; Hebrews 1:1–3; Colossians 1:15–17). He is not a component or stage within the architecture.

The witnesses receive, encounter, and testify to what Scripture records. Their testimony is essential to the written account, but they are not the source of the Resurrection.

## Jesus declared His death and Resurrection beforehand

The Resurrection is not introduced in the Gospels as an explanation invented only after the tomb was found without His body. Jesus repeatedly tells His disciples that He will suffer, be killed, and rise again.

Matthew records these declarations in Matthew 16:21; 17:22–23; and 20:18–19. Mark records them in Mark 8:31; 9:31; and 10:33–34. Luke records the same central prediction in Luke 9:22; 18:31–33; and 24:6–7 recalls the words spoken in Galilee. John records Jesus' words concerning the temple of His body in John 2:19–22 and His authority to lay down His life and take it again in John 10:17–18.

The disciples do not fully understand these statements at the time they are spoken. John 2:22 explicitly says that after Jesus was risen from the dead, His disciples remembered that He had said this and believed the Scripture and the word which Jesus had said.

The prior declarations matter because the Gospel writers present the Resurrection in relation to Jesus' own words. The later witness does not create the prediction; it recalls and testifies to what Jesus had already said.

## The empty tomb: what the witnesses found

The four Gospel accounts record the discovery that Jesus' body was no longer in the place where He had been laid. Their accounts are distinct and should be read individually before being compared.

### Matthew 28:1–10

Matthew names Mary Magdalene and the other Mary. He records an earthquake, an angel descending from heaven, the rolling back of the stone, and the angel's declaration: “He is not here: for he is risen, as he said.” The women are invited to see the place where the Lord lay and are sent to tell the disciples. Jesus then meets them; they hold Him by the feet and worship Him.

Matthew 28:11–15 separately records the guards' report to the chief priests and elders and the bribery allegation that the disciples stole the body while the guards slept. This competing explanation is part of Matthew's account and must be evaluated as such; it is not independently narrated by the other three Gospels.

### Mark 16:1–8

Mark names Mary Magdalene, Mary the mother of James, and Salome. They arrive with spices and see that the stone has already been rolled away. In the sepulchre they encounter a young man in a long white garment, who declares that Jesus of Nazareth, which was crucified, is risen and is not there. He directs them to see the place where they laid Him and to tell the disciples and Peter that Jesus goes before them into Galilee.

Mark 16:1–8 ends with the women's fear and astonishment. The KJV includes Mark 16:9–20, but the longer ending has a recognized textual-critical question because it is absent from some important early Greek manuscripts and present in other ancient witnesses. The received KJV text is retained while the manuscript question remains visible. Claims relying uniquely on Mark 16:9–20 should be distinguished from those independently recorded elsewhere.

### Luke 24:1–12

Luke names Mary Magdalene, Joanna, Mary the mother of James, and other women. They find the stone rolled away and do not find the body of the Lord Jesus. Two men in shining garments remind them of Jesus' prior words concerning His suffering, death, and rising on the third day. The women report to the apostles, whose initial response is disbelief. Peter runs to the sepulchre and sees the linen clothes laid by themselves.

### John 20:1–18

John begins with Mary Magdalene coming while it is yet dark. Seeing the stone taken away, she runs to Simon Peter and the other disciple. They run to the sepulchre and inspect it. John records the linen clothes and the napkin that had been about Jesus' head, lying separately. He says the other disciple saw and believed, and then adds that they did not yet know the Scripture that Jesus must rise again from the dead.

Mary remains at the sepulchre weeping. She sees two angels and then Jesus, whom she initially supposes to be the gardener. When He calls her by name, she recognizes Him. Jesus sends her to tell His brethren, and she reports, “I have seen the Lord.”

### What the phrase “empty tomb” means

“Empty tomb” is shorthand for the absence of Jesus' body from the place where He had been laid. It does not mean that John reports no objects remaining: he specifically describes the linen clothes and the separate napkin. The phrase should not be made to establish a physical mechanism that the text does not describe.

The Gospel accounts name and group the women differently, give differing details about the messengers, and emphasize different responses and observations. A detail present in one account and omitted in another is not automatically a contradiction. Equally, it is not sufficient to say that every difference is automatically harmonized. The accounts must first be allowed to say what each actually says.

The empty tomb is an important part of the testimony, but it is not the whole Resurrection claim. The Gospel accounts proceed to encounters with the risen Jesus, and the apostolic proclamation joins the empty tomb's context to the appearances.

## The risen Jesus is encountered

The Gospel writers do not present the risen Jesus merely as a memory, a continuing influence, or an idea sustained by His followers. They record encounters in which He speaks, is recognized, shows the marks associated with His Crucifixion, and is physically encountered.

Matthew 28:8–10 records Jesus meeting the women, who hold Him by the feet and worship Him. Matthew 28:16–20 records the eleven meeting Him in Galilee, worshipping Him, and receiving His commission; Matthew also preserves the detail that some doubted.

Luke 24:13–35 records the encounter on the road to Emmaus. Jesus walks with two disciples, expounds the Scriptures concerning Himself, and is recognized in the breaking of bread. Luke 24:36–43 records Jesus standing among the gathered disciples, inviting them to behold His hands and feet, declaring that He has flesh and bones, and eating before them.

John 20:11–18 records Mary's encounter and recognition when Jesus calls her by name. John 20:19–23 records Jesus appearing to the disciples behind shut doors, showing them His hands and side, speaking peace, sending them, and breathing upon them. John 20:24–29 records Thomas' absence from the first appearance and his later encounter, ending in the confession, “My Lord and my God.” John 21:1–14 records the appearance by the sea of Tiberias and the miraculous catch of fishes.

Acts 1:3 summarizes that Jesus showed Himself alive after His passion by many infallible proofs, being seen of the disciples over forty days and speaking of the things pertaining to the kingdom of God.

The appearances are not a single continuous transcript of every event during the forty days. The Gospel writers provide selected encounters. The named witnesses, settings, recognition, responses, and sequence should be compared without pretending that every chronological detail is supplied.

### The bodily testimony

Luke 24:36–43 is particularly explicit. Jesus invites the disciples to behold His hands and feet, says “handle me, and see; for a spirit hath not flesh and bones, as ye see me have,” and eats before them. John records the wounds in His hands and side and Thomas' response to the invitation to inspect them.

These passages establish the Gospel writers' presentation of the risen Jesus as the same Jesus who was crucified, now risen and encountered. They do not provide a complete modern anatomical description or a scientific account of the properties of the risen body. Paul's discussion of the “spiritual body” in 1 Corinthians 15:35–58 must be read in its own argument; the phrase should not be silently translated into “immaterial” or treated as a denial of the Gospel accounts' bodily testimony.

## Paul's received proclamation

Paul's summary in 1 Corinthians 15:3–8 joins four central elements:

1. Christ died for our sins according to the scriptures.
2. He was buried.
3. He rose again the third day according to the scriptures.
4. He was seen by Cephas, then by the twelve, then by above five hundred brethren at once, then by James, then by all the apostles, and last of all by Paul.

Paul does not leave the Resurrection as a minor consequence of Jesus' ministry. In 1 Corinthians 15:12–19 he argues that if Christ is not risen, apostolic preaching and faith are vain. He then declares, “But now is Christ risen from the dead, and become the firstfruits of them that slept” (v. 20).

The chapter develops the relationship between Adam and Christ, death and resurrection, the natural and spiritual body, corruption and incorruption, and the final defeat of death. Christ's Resurrection is the firstfruits; it is not merely another instance of a person being restored to mortal life.

Paul's list of witnesses is a distinct apostolic testimony. It should not be forced into an exact chronological schedule when the text does not provide one. Nor should the number of witnesses be inflated by counting overlapping Gospel reports as though every mention necessarily described a separate appearance.

## Why Christ's Resurrection is distinct from the raising of others

The Gospels record Jesus raising others, including the daughter of Jairus, the widow's son at Nain, and Lazarus. Those accounts are not identical to the Resurrection of Jesus.

The people Jesus raised during His ministry returned to mortal life within the narratives. Scripture presents Christ as risen, no more to return to corruption or death (Romans 6:9). Paul calls Him the firstfruits of them that slept (1 Corinthians 15:20–23); Colossians 1:18 calls Him the firstborn from the dead. Revelation 1:17–18 records the risen Christ declaring that He is alive for evermore and has the keys of hell and of death.

John 10:17–18 records Jesus' own statement that He lays down His life and has power to take it again. Acts 2:24, 32 and 13:30–37 proclaim that God raised Him from the dead. Galatians 1:1 speaks of God the Father who raised Him. Romans 8:11 speaks of the Spirit in relation to the raising and life of Christ.

These statements belong together in canonical examination. They are not competing accounts from which one must be selected, nor do they authorize an invented mechanism. The full Scriptural testimony concerning the Father, Son, and Spirit must be preserved.

## The Ascension is a distinct event

The Resurrection, the appearances, and the Ascension are related but distinct.

### Luke 24:50–53

Luke records Jesus leading the disciples out as far as Bethany, lifting His hands, and blessing them. While He blesses them, He is parted from them and carried up into heaven. The disciples worship Him and return to Jerusalem with great joy, continually praising and blessing God in the temple.

Luke's closing account does not state the number of days between the Resurrection and the Ascension in this passage.

### Acts 1:1–11

Acts supplies further detail. Jesus showed Himself alive over forty days and spoke of the kingdom of God. He commanded the disciples to wait in Jerusalem for the promise of the Father. They asked whether He would at that time restore the kingdom to Israel. Jesus directed them to the Father's authority over times and seasons and promised that they would receive power when the Holy Ghost came upon them, becoming witnesses in Jerusalem, Judaea, Samaria, and unto the uttermost part of the earth.

While the disciples beheld, Jesus was taken up, and a cloud received Him out of their sight. Two men in white apparel announced that this same Jesus would so come in like manner as they had seen Him go into heaven.

Acts 1:3 and 1:9–11, read in their immediate narrative order, place the forty-day appearances and instruction before the narrated Ascension. Luke 24's closing passage is more compressed and does not independently state the interval. The relation between the two presentations and the exact chronology remain subjects for comparative interpretation. The alternative scholarly proposals are recorded in the dedicated study rather than concealed or treated as settled by assertion.

The Ascension must not be merged with the Resurrection or with any one of the appearances. It is the distinct departure narrated by Luke and Acts. The wider canonical testimony concerning Christ's exaltation and present position includes Psalm 110:1; Acts 2:32–36; Ephesians 1:19–23; Philippians 2:9–11; Hebrews 1:3; and 1 Peter 3:22.

## The Breath of the Risen Christ

John 20:19–23 requires particular attention because John records the risen Jesus breathing upon His disciples and saying, “Receive ye the Holy Ghost.”

This event belongs to the Resurrection-day appearance. It must be read in its own sequence and alongside the other Scriptural testimony concerning breath, life, the Spirit, and the promised empowerment of the disciples.

### Genesis 2:7 — God breathes life into the formed man

Genesis 2:7 states that the LORD God formed man of the dust of the ground, breathed into his nostrils the breath of life, and man became a living soul. The Hebrew narrative identifies God as the One who forms and breathes; man becomes a living being.

The KJV phrase “living soul” translates *nepeš ḥayyāh*, wording also used of living creatures elsewhere in Genesis. The verse should not be made to claim that animals are not living beings or to establish an unsupported account of a detachable substance. Its distinctive statement is God's forming of the man and His breathing into him the breath of life.

### Ezekiel 37:1–14 — breath and the restoration of Israel

Ezekiel's vision describes dry bones brought together, covered with sinews, flesh, and skin, yet without breath. The prophet is commanded to speak to the breath; breath comes into them, and they live. The LORD interprets the vision as concerning the whole house of Israel and promises to put His Spirit in them.

The Hebrew *rûaḥ* can mean wind, breath, or spirit. Its precise force depends on the context. Ezekiel deliberately uses the imagery across the vision, but the passage is not simply a description of ordinary respiration. It is the LORD's promise of restoration to His people.

Genesis 2:7 and Ezekiel 37 are related witnesses to God's life-giving and restoring agency. They are not the same event.

### John 20:22 — Jesus breathes and commands reception

John records the following order:

1. Jesus appears to the disciples and speaks peace.
2. He shows them His hands and His side.
3. He sends them as the Father sent Him.
4. He breathes upon them.
5. He says, “Receive ye the Holy Ghost.”
6. He speaks concerning the remission and retention of sins.

The Greek verb translated “breathed” is *enephysēsen* (ἐνεφύσησεν), from *emphysaō*. It is the same distinctive verb used in the Septuagint translation of Genesis 2:7 for God's breathing into Adam. The correspondence is stronger than a general shared image: John uses the same Greek breathing verb in a setting where the risen Christ breathes and commands the reception of the Holy Ghost.

This is a significant Scriptural correspondence. It supports a new-creation resonance in John's scene. It does not make the disciples new Adams in the same sense as Christ, nor does it make the two events identical. Genesis 2:7 records God's giving life to the formed man; John 20:22 records the risen Christ's action toward His commissioned disciples.

### John 7 and John 14–16 — the promised Spirit

John 7:37–39 connects Jesus' promise of living water with the Spirit whom believers should receive, and says that the Spirit was not yet given because Jesus was not yet glorified. John 14–16 records Jesus' teaching about the Comforter: the Father gives and sends the Spirit; the Spirit testifies of Christ, teaches, brings Jesus' words to remembrance, guides into truth, and glorifies Him.

These chapters form the promise-context for John 20:22. They do not allow the investigator to ignore the explicit action and words in John 20, nor do they permit the later account in Acts to be omitted.

### John 20:22 and Acts 2 — the exact relation remains open

Acts 1:4–8 records Jesus commanding the disciples to wait for the Father's promise and promising power when the Holy Ghost comes upon them. Acts 2 records the sound from heaven as of a rushing mighty wind, the appearance of cloven tongues like as of fire, the disciples being filled with the Holy Ghost, and their speaking with other tongues as the Spirit gave them utterance.

The dedicated Investigation III.2 preserves several interpretive proposals rather than reducing the question to a simple actual-versus-symbolic binary:

- **Actual reception on Resurrection day, followed by distinct empowerment:** Jesus' breathing and command are understood as an actual reception of the Holy Ghost, while Pentecost is understood as a subsequent endowment or empowerment for public witness.
- **Enacted sign or anticipation:** Jesus' action is understood as a prophetic or enacted sign pointing toward the promised outpouring.
- **Partial or staged reception:** John 20:22 is understood as one aspect or stage of the Spirit's work, with Acts 2 recording a further or fuller manifestation.
- **Distinct narrative and theological presentations:** John's account and Luke–Acts are understood as presenting related dimensions of the promised Spirit and the disciples' commission, without assuming that their descriptions are interchangeable.

These are proposals to examine, not conclusions adopted wholesale. The study separately considers the extent and timing of reception, the function of the gift, the relation among the Gospel and Acts narratives, and the scope of the recipients. It also compares John 20:19–23, Luke 24:33–49, Acts 1:12–15, and Acts 2:1–4 without assuming that every gathering has an identical membership or a precisely established headcount.

John's wording expressly joins Jesus' breathing with the command, “Receive ye the Holy Ghost.” Luke and Acts expressly record the future promise, the Ascension context, the outpouring, and its public witness. The exact theological and chronological relationship remains unresolved in the dedicated investigation. The lexical and canonical connections are evidence to weigh; they do not by themselves settle the question. Neither passage should be erased to protect a preferred reading of the other.

John 20:23 follows the sending and reception language. The precise ecclesial and sacramental implications of the authority to remit and retain sins have been interpreted differently across Christian traditions. The immediate context and wider canonical teaching concerning repentance and remission in Christ's name remain central to its examination.

## The Spirit and the witnesses

Acts 1:8 identifies the coming of the Holy Ghost with power and the disciples' witness to Jesus. Acts 2 records the outpouring and the public proclamation of the risen Christ. Peter's sermon declares that God raised Jesus, that the apostles are witnesses, and that the exalted Christ has received of the Father the promise of the Holy Ghost and shed forth what the hearers see and hear (Acts 2:32–33).

The distinction between source and witness remains clear. The disciples are commissioned and empowered to testify. They do not originate the Resurrection, the Spirit, or the message. The Spirit's testimony glorifies Christ, in keeping with John 15:26 and 16:13–14.

The breath in John 20 and the wind-like sound in Acts 2 should not be collapsed into one physical event. John records Jesus breathing upon the disciples; Acts records a sound from heaven as of a rushing mighty wind and the visible appearance like fire. The texts use related breath/Spirit language in different narrated settings. Their exact relationship is the subject of the open question already identified.

## The Resurrection under the Triple Constraint

**Biblical compliance** requires reading the four Gospel accounts individually, preserving their wording and distinctive details, and then comparing them. It requires keeping the empty tomb distinct from the appearances, the appearances distinct from the Ascension, and John 20:22 distinct from Acts 2 while examining their canonical relationship.

**Scientific coherence** requires care about what historical, textual-critical, medical, and physical methods can establish. Manuscript questions such as Mark 16:9–20 and Luke 24:51–52 must be acknowledged. Historical evaluation of the witnesses and early proclamation must be distinguished from the theological claim. The texts do not supply a modern physical mechanism for the Resurrection or the risen body's properties; such mechanisms must not be invented.

**GUA-W consistency** preserves God as Original Cause, the unique identity of Jesus Christ, and the derivative role of witnesses. The architecture is not Jesus, and Jesus is not a component within it. The disciples receive and testify; they do not originate what they witness. The completed architecture is not altered by a new analogy or by a proposed mechanism.

## What the current record establishes—and what remains open

The central Scriptural record establishes that Jesus predicted His death and Resurrection; the Gospel writers report that His body was absent from the place where He had been laid; the risen Jesus appeared to named witnesses and groups of disciples; and the apostolic proclamation identifies Him as risen and as the firstfruits of them that slept.

Luke and Acts record the Ascension, with Acts supplying the forty-day period and additional details. John records the risen Christ breathing upon His disciples and commanding them to receive the Holy Ghost. Acts records the later outpouring at Pentecost and the Spirit-empowered witness.

The dedicated studies preserve open questions, including:

- The precise harmonization and chronology of the Gospel accounts and the appearances.
- The textual-critical status and history of Mark 16:9–20 and Luke 24:51–52.
- The historical assessment of the empty-tomb and appearance testimony.
- The exact chronology and interpretation of Luke 24 in relation to Acts 1.
- The precise theological relationship between John 20:22 and Acts 2.
- The full interpretive implications of John 20:23.
- The limits of historical and scientific claims about the Resurrection and the risen body.

These are bounded questions. They do not make the explicit Scriptural testimony disappear, and they do not make the completed GUA-W incomplete. The status of an investigation describes the state of its examination, not a deficiency in its Source.

## The witnesses testify to the risen Christ

The chapter has moved through the testimony in its distinct parts: the tomb, the appearances, the Ascension, and the breath of the risen Christ. Each account contributes its own details. The differences remain visible; the relationships are examined without inventing a single mechanism or chronology beyond the evidence.

The central proclamation remains the one Paul records: Christ died, was buried, rose again the third day according to the Scriptures, and was seen.

The witnesses testify to the One who lives. Their testimony does not originate His life or authority.

**The Author gives. The Word is made flesh. Christ is risen. The witnesses receive and testify.**

**One Author. One originating Word. One Creation. Many witnesses.**
