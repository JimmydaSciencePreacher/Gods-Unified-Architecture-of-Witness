# Chapter 12 — The Passion and Death of Christ

## The death must be read as the Gospel writers record it

The works and signs examined in the preceding chapter direct attention to the identity and authority of Jesus Christ. The Passion narratives now bring the reader to the Crucifixion, the death of Jesus, and His burial.

The governing order of GUA-W remains:

**Author → Word → Creation → Witness**

Jesus Christ is not merely one witness among others, nor is He a component contained within GUA-W. The Gospel testimony identifies Him as the Word made flesh, the Son, and the One through whom all things were made (John 1:1–3, 14; Hebrews 1:1–3; Colossians 1:15–17). GUA-W is a map of the created order; it is not God and it is not Christ.

The Passion accounts are therefore read with two distinctions in view. First, the events themselves must not be confused with the later testimony about them. Second, the four Gospel narratives must be compared without forcing their distinct details into a single account that erases what each writer chose to record.

The primary texts for the death and burial are Matthew 27:32–66, Mark 15:21–47, Luke 23:26–56, and John 19:16–42 in the King James Version. The darkness, the torn Temple veil, and Matthew's report of the earthquake and opened graves are related signs, but each has its own textual questions and limits.

## The Crucifixion

All four Gospels record Jesus being crucified under Roman authority at the place called Golgotha, rendered Calvary in Luke, with two others crucified alongside Him (Matthew 27:32–38; Mark 15:21–27; Luke 23:26–33; John 19:16–18).

The accounts share the central event while preserving differences in their details.

Matthew, Mark, and Luke record Simon of Cyrene being compelled to carry the cross. John 19:17 says Jesus went forth bearing His cross. These statements are not to be silently substituted for one another. The accounts may describe different portions of the journey or emphasize different aspects, but the precise sequence should not be asserted beyond what the texts establish.

All four Gospels record the charge or title associated with Jesus as King of the Jews. Their wording and presentation differ. John 19:19–22 gives the written title and records the objection raised by the chief priests. The soldiers divide His garments; John gives the additional detail of the seamless coat and the casting of lots (John 19:23–24).

The Gospel writers also record mockery and the presence of spectators. Matthew and Mark say both crucified men reviled Jesus, while Luke records one rebuking the other and addressing Jesus (Luke 23:39–43). John records two others crucified with Him without narrating that exchange. The differences belong to the separate accounts and should remain visible in comparison.

## The sayings from the cross

The four Gospels do not give one identical list of Jesus' words from the cross.

Matthew 27:46–50 and Mark 15:34–37 record Jesus' cry, “Eli, Eli, lama sabachthani?” and His final loud cry before He gives up the ghost.

Luke 23:34, 43, 46 records sayings concerning forgiveness, the promise to the repentant man crucified beside Him, and His final words: “Father, into thy hands I commend my spirit.”

John 19:26–30 records Jesus speaking to His mother and the beloved disciple, saying “I thirst,” and declaring, “It is finished,” before bowing His head and giving up the ghost.

The sayings are preserved according to each Gospel's account. A complete harmony may be explored, but it must not be presented as though any one Gospel supplies every saying or the exact minute-by-minute order of all of them.

John's “It is finished” belongs first to the immediate Passion narrative. Its relationship to Genesis 2:1–5 and Revelation 21:6 is considered within the established canonical comparison of completion declarations. The three passages remain distinct in wording, context, and event.

## The death of Jesus

The Gospel testimony is explicit that Jesus died.

Matthew 27:50 records that He “yielded up the ghost.” Mark 15:37 says He “gave up the ghost.” Luke 23:46 records His final words and says that He gave up the ghost. John 19:30 likewise records that He bowed His head and gave up the ghost.

Mark supplies a further detail. Joseph of Arimathaea goes to Pilate and asks for Jesus' body. Pilate is surprised that Jesus is already dead, summons the centurion, and asks whether He has been any while dead. After receiving the centurion's confirmation, Pilate grants the body to Joseph (Mark 15:42–45).

John records that the soldiers came to Jesus, saw that He was already dead, and did not break His legs. One soldier pierced His side with a spear, and John reports that blood and water came forth (John 19:31–37). John connects the unbroken bones with Scripture and also cites the statement, “They shall look on him whom they pierced” (John 19:36–37; compare Exodus 12:46; Numbers 9:12; Psalm 34:20; Zechariah 12:10).

These details must be kept distinct. Mark records Pilate's inquiry to the centurion. John records the soldiers' observation and the piercing. John reports what came from Jesus' side but does not provide a modern clinical diagnosis or laboratory analysis. Later medical interpretations are not additional statements from the Gospel text.

The four accounts do not describe Jesus as merely unconscious or as escaping death before the burial. Each records His death. Historical and medical investigation can examine the setting and the limits of what can be known, but a proposed physiological mechanism should not be presented as though the Gospel writers supplied it.

## The darkness during the Crucifixion

Matthew 27:45, Mark 15:33, and Luke 23:44–45 report darkness from the sixth hour to the ninth hour—approximately noon until 3 p.m. Luke also says that the sun was darkened. John records the Crucifixion and death but does not mention the darkness.

The KJV wording differs: Matthew says “all the land,” Mark says “the whole land,” and Luke says “all the earth.” The Greek term *gē* can refer to earth, land, or a region according to context. The English wording alone does not settle whether the darkness was local, regional, or global.

The Synoptic accounts do not identify a physical cause. An ordinary solar eclipse is not a satisfactory explanation under the Passover chronology: Passover occurs at the full-moon portion of the lunar month, whereas a solar eclipse requires the new-moon alignment. The reported interval of three hours also exceeds the duration of an ordinary total solar eclipse at any one location.

Dust, cloud, or another atmospheric process has sometimes been proposed, but no event-specific evidence in the current investigation establishes one of these as the cause. The physical cause and geographic extent remain unresolved.

The darkness has important Scriptural parallels. Amos 8:9–10 speaks of the sun going down at noon and the earth being darkened in a clear day. Joel 2:10, 31; Isaiah 13:10; Ezekiel 32:7–8; and Exodus 10:21–23 provide wider biblical language connecting darkness with divine judgment and the day of the LORD. These are canonical relationships; the precise literary dependence of each Gospel account should be examined rather than assumed.

The textual finding is clear within its stated scope: the three Synoptic Gospels report the darkness and its time markers. The exact physical cause and geographic reach are not specified.

## The veil of the Temple was rent

Matthew 27:51 and Mark 15:38 say that the veil of the Temple was rent in two from the top to the bottom. Luke 23:45 says that the veil was rent in the midst. John does not mention the event.

There is a difference in narrative placement. Matthew and Mark place the tearing after Jesus gives up the ghost. Luke places the notice of the torn veil before Jesus' final saying and death in the written sequence. Luke does not provide an exact clock-time for the tearing. The accounts should be compared in their own order rather than silently rearranged.

The Gospel phrase identifies the Temple veil but does not explicitly name it as the inner veil or the outer entrance curtain. The inner veil is a serious and often preferred identification when the Old Testament sanctuary pattern is considered, but the precise curtain remains a qualified conclusion rather than an explicit statement in the Gospel wording.

Exodus 26:31–35 describes the veil separating the Holy Place from the Most Holy Place. Leviticus 16:2, 12–17 describes the restricted and regulated entry of the high priest within the veil on the Day of Atonement. The veil therefore belongs to the sanctuary's ordered boundary and access.

Hebrews 6:19–20, 9:1–12, and 10:19–22 illuminate the access to God made through Christ's priestly work. Hebrews 10:20 speaks of the new and living way through the veil, “that is to say, his flesh.” This is an explicit theological argument within Hebrews. Its relationship to the physical tearing reported in the Synoptic Gospels is significant, but the passages should not be collapsed into one another.

The Gospel writers report the tearing; the sanctuary texts explain the veil's role; Hebrews develops the Christological meaning of access through Jesus. Each contributes its own testimony.

## The earthquake, rent rocks, and opened graves

Matthew 27:51–54 alone among the canonical Gospels records the earthquake, the rending of rocks, the opening of graves, and the raising of many saints.

Matthew reports that the earth quaked and the rocks rent. He then says that the graves were opened and many bodies of sleeping saints arose. The next verse says they came out of the graves after Jesus' resurrection, entered the holy city, and appeared unto many (Matthew 27:52–53).

These are distinguishable statements. An opened grave is not identical to a person being raised. Being raised is not identical to coming out of a grave. Coming out is not identical to appearing to people. Matthew reports each element.

The timing requires care. Matthew places the earthquake and rent rocks in the sequence at Jesus' death. He explicitly says that the saints came out of the graves after Jesus' resurrection. The exact timing of their being raised in relation to the opening of the graves and the Resurrection is debated. The text does not name the saints, give their number, describe their later destiny, or explain the precise condition of their bodies.

The wider canon calls Christ “the firstfruits of them that slept” (1 Corinthians 15:20), “the firstborn from the dead” (Colossians 1:18), and the first begotten of the dead (Revelation 1:5). These passages are essential context for interpreting Matthew's account. They do not authorize an invented explanation of the saints' later fate or bodily condition.

Matthew 28:2 reports another earthquake at the tomb. That is a separate narrated event and should not be confused with the earthquake in Matthew 27:51.

Earthquakes, fractured rock, and rock-cut tombs are physically intelligible phenomena. General geological knowledge and archaeological evidence about ancient tombs provide context, not event-specific verification of Matthew's report. No measured magnitude, epicentre, geological mechanism, or independent contemporary record of these particular appearances is established in the investigation.

## Joseph, Nicodemus, and the burial

All four Gospels record Joseph of Arimathaea's role in receiving and burying Jesus' body. The accounts describe him with different details.

Matthew 27:57–61 calls Joseph a rich man and a disciple of Jesus. He asks Pilate for the body, wraps it in clean linen, lays it in his own new tomb hewn out of the rock, rolls a great stone to the door, and departs. Matthew also records Mary Magdalene and the other Mary sitting over against the sepulchre.

Mark 15:42–47 describes Joseph as an honourable counsellor waiting for the kingdom of God. Mark records his bold request to Pilate, Pilate's inquiry to the centurion, Joseph's purchase of fine linen, and the placing of Jesus in a rock-hewn sepulchre. Mary Magdalene and Mary the mother of Joses observe where He is laid.

Luke 23:50–56 describes Joseph as a good and just counsellor who had not consented to the counsel and deed of the others. Jesus is laid in a sepulchre hewn in stone, “wherein never man before was laid.” The women who came with Jesus from Galilee see how His body was laid, return, prepare spices and ointments, and rest on the sabbath according to the commandment.

John 19:38–42 describes Joseph as a disciple secretly for fear of the Jews. Nicodemus joins him, bringing a mixture of myrrh and aloes, about an hundred pound weight. They wrap Jesus' body with the spices in linen clothes, according to the manner of the Jews to bury. John identifies a garden near the place of crucifixion and a new sepulchre in which no man had yet been laid.

The accounts agree on Joseph's involvement and the burial of Jesus' body, while preserving distinct information. Nicodemus is named by John alone. The garden and the quantity of spices are John's details. Luke records that Joseph dissented from the council's action and that the tomb had never before held a body. Matthew alone records the guard and seal placed at the tomb (Matthew 27:62–66).

The women are important witnesses to the burial place. The accounts preserve their observation in differing detail. Luke explicitly says they saw how His body was laid. This observation becomes relevant when the women return to the tomb in the Resurrection accounts.

## Historical and medical evidence: what it can and cannot establish

The Roman practice of crucifixion and the treatment of executed bodies are relevant historical questions. Roman treatment was not necessarily identical in every case; the evidence indicates variation according to circumstance and official discretion. First-century Jewish burial archaeology establishes that a crucified man could receive burial in the region, but an archaeological find of another individual is not evidence about Jesus' own remains.

Historical context can assess whether the Gospel burial account is plausible within known practices. It cannot independently identify the body of Jesus or verify every detail of the Gospel narratives.

Medical literature has proposed several physiological explanations for death by crucifixion. Such proposals are not equivalent to a diagnosis of Jesus. The surviving Gospel accounts do not provide clinical measurements, and the exact medical mechanism remains unresolved.

The same evidentiary discipline applies to the darkness, the veil, and the earthquake. A physically conceivable explanation is not thereby the established explanation of this particular event. A lack of surviving event-specific geological or meteorological evidence is not, by itself, a disproof of the Gospel report.

## The Passion testimony under the Triple Constraint

**Biblical compliance** requires that each Gospel account be read in its own wording and context. The differences in the sayings from the cross, Simon's role, the two crucified men, the darkness, the veil's narrative placement, the confirmation of death, and the burial details remain visible. The distinct events are not collapsed.

**Scientific coherence** requires accurate treatment of historical, archaeological, medical, astronomical, and geological evidence. General background evidence is not represented as event-specific verification. Proposed mechanisms remain hypotheses unless the evidence establishes them.

**GUA-W consistency** preserves God as Original Cause, the unique identity of Jesus Christ, and the distinction between the event and its witnesses. The centurion's confirmation, the soldiers' actions, Joseph's request, Nicodemus' preparation, and the women's observation are real parts of the narrated history. They do not become the originating cause of Jesus' death or the authority behind His work.

The Passion record is not made stronger by claiming more than the sources establish. Its testimony is examined carefully because the claims are central, not because the details may be freely supplied.

## What is established, and what remains open

The Gospel accounts establish the Crucifixion and death of Jesus, the release of His body, and His burial in a tomb. Mark records Pilate's inquiry to the centurion; John records the soldiers' observation and the piercing. All four Gospels record Joseph's role in the burial.

The Synoptics report darkness from the sixth to the ninth hour. Matthew, Mark, and Luke report the torn Temple veil with different narrative placement and wording. Matthew alone records the earthquake, rent rocks, opened graves, and saints who came out after Jesus' resurrection.

Important questions remain open: the exact physical cause and geographic extent of the darkness; the precise identity of the Temple curtain; the scale and mechanism of Matthew's earthquake; the timing and later destiny of the saints; the precise medical mechanism of Jesus' death; and the extent to which historical evidence can independently corroborate particular details.

These unresolved questions are specific. They do not erase the explicit testimony already recorded. Nor do they make the central Resurrection investigation a substitute for examining the death and burial that precede it.

## The burial is the boundary before the Resurrection testimony

Paul's summary in 1 Corinthians 15:3–5 places Christ's death, burial, Resurrection, and appearances together in the received proclamation. Acts 13:28–30 likewise recounts the condemnation, the laying of Jesus in a sepulchre, and God's raising Him from the dead.

The death and burial are therefore not incidental background. They belong to the apostolic proclamation. The body is released, prepared, and laid in a tomb. The women observe where He is laid. The next chapter turns to what the Gospel witnesses report when they return.

**The text records the death. The body is released. The body is prepared and buried. The women observe where He is laid. The Resurrection investigation proceeds from that recorded burial—not from an assumed or omitted one.**

**One Author. One originating Word. One Creation. Many witnesses.**
