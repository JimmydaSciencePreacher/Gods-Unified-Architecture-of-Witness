# Chapter 14 — The Human Will: Creaturely Capacity and Creator Authority

## The question beneath the question

The human will is often discussed as though one question settles every other question: Is man free to choose?

Scripture gives a fuller account. Human beings perceive, reason, evaluate, desire, choose, act, respond, and bear responsibility. Yet those real creaturely capacities do not make mankind the source of life, the author of Creation, or the ultimate authority that establishes good and evil.

The question must therefore be stated more precisely:

**What capacities has God given to mankind, what responsibilities has He entrusted to mankind, and what authority remains God's alone?**

This chapter examines that distinction through Genesis 1–3, Job 38–41, Isaiah 40–48, Colossians 2:6–15, and Romans 1:18–32. The wider question of the human will in salvation is carried forward into the next chapter, where the passage-by-passage investigations are brought together.

The governing order remains:

**Author → Word → Creation → Witness**

God alone is Original Cause. Mankind is created, receives life, and acts within the order God gives. Human activity is real; it is not original authorship.

## Genesis 1–2: the human being receives life, identity, work, and command

Genesis begins with God's action. The heavens and the earth are created by Him (Genesis 1:1). Mankind is made in God's image and likeness (1:26–27), blessed, and given dominion and responsibility within the created order (1:28–30).

Genesis 2 gives a more particular account of man's formation. The LORD God forms man of the dust of the ground, breathes into his nostrils the breath of life, and man becomes a living soul (2:7). The man's life is received; he is not self-originating.

God places the man in the garden to dress it and keep it (2:15). He gives a command concerning the trees of the garden, including the prohibition concerning the tree of the knowledge of good and evil and its stated consequence (2:16–17).

These passages establish several realities together:

- Man is a creature whose life comes from God.
- Man has real capacities to perceive, name, work, and act.
- Man receives a place and responsibility within Creation.
- Man receives God's command and is accountable in relation to it.
- The created human will operates within an order it did not originate.

Genesis 2:19–20 records Adam observing and naming the living creatures brought to him. His naming is a genuine human act of recognition and classification. It does not create the creatures or establish their existence. The text thus distinguishes human participation from divine authorship within the opening account itself.

Delegated dominion is real dominion, but it is delegated. The authority to tend and keep the garden does not confer authority to rewrite the command by which the man is to live.

## Genesis 3: perception, evaluation, desire, and action

Genesis 3 records the serpent challenging the Word God had spoken. The woman hears the rival account, considers the tree, sees that it is good for food, pleasant to the eyes, and desired to make one wise, takes its fruit, and eats. She gives also to her husband, and he eats (Genesis 3:1–6).

The narrative does not portray human beings as lacking perception, evaluation, desire, or action. Those capacities are plainly present. The issue is the relation of those capacities to God's prior Word.

The serpent's question challenges what God said. The woman's evaluation occurs within that challenge. The action follows the rival account rather than faithful reception of the command already given.

Genesis 3:22 says that the man has become as one of “us, to know good and evil.” The verse must be read with the entire narrative. It records a changed condition of knowledge; it does not say that mankind has become the original authority who establishes what good and evil are. God continues to speak, judge, provide, and determine the consequences of the transgression (3:9–24).

The distinction is therefore not between a human being who can evaluate and one who cannot. It is between creaturely evaluation and the Creator's authority. The ability to judge or desire does not itself make the creature the source of the standard by which judgment is true.

## The meanings that must not be confused

The phrase *free will* is used in more than one way. Without defining the claim, agreement or disagreement can conceal different questions.

**Choice** is selecting, deciding, or responding among alternatives.

**Responsibility** is moral or covenantal accountability for an action or omission.

**Ability** is the capacity to perform the particular action being discussed. The domain and conditions of that capacity must be named.

**Willingness** is desire, intention, readiness, or volition concerning an action.

**Delegated responsibility** is work or authority entrusted to a creature within an order established by another.

**Ultimate authority** is the Creator's authority as the source of Creation, its purpose, and the governing standard of good and evil.

These categories are related, but they are not interchangeable. Choice does not by itself prove ability in every domain. Responsibility does not by itself settle every question about capacity. Willingness does not by itself establish independent originating power. A command does not, by itself, settle every question about the hearer's ability. And the presence of a human response does not identify that response as the originating cause of God's purpose.

Logical distinction is not automatically chronological sequence. Defining choice, ability, willingness, divine initiative, regeneration, faith, and salvation as separate categories does not establish the temporal order in which any of them occur. Those questions require their own Scriptural examination.

## What the KJV phrase “freewill” actually records

The joined English expression **freewill** occurs in the KJV in contexts that principally concern voluntary offerings. Ezra 7:13 is a notable different use: it describes those who are “minded of their own freewill to go up” to Jerusalem.

These passages are important, but the English phrase must be read in context. The freewill offerings in Leviticus, Numbers, Deuteronomy, Chronicles, Ezra, and Psalm 119 concern gifts or offerings voluntarily brought. Ezra 7:13 concerns a voluntary decision to go up to Jerusalem. These occurrences do not, by themselves, define a comprehensive philosophical doctrine of autonomous human will, nor do they identify the originating cause of spiritual birth, faith, or salvation.

The underlying Hebrew and Aramaic forms are not one identical grammatical construction in every occurrence. The Hebrew *nedavah* word-family is associated with voluntariness and voluntary offerings; Ezra contains Aramaic usage requiring its own treatment. A concordance count or dictionary gloss cannot replace the contextual reading of each verse.

Leviticus 1:3 also uses the wording “of his own voluntary will,” while Exodus 35:5 speaks of a willing heart. These are related subjects, but they are not occurrences of the exact joined English word *freewill*. A careful word study keeps the exact phrase register distinct from the wider study of choosing, willing, desire, and response.

The finding is limited but important: Scripture records voluntary human action. That fact should neither be denied nor made to answer questions the passages do not address.

## Job 38–41: the creature does not become the Creator's judge

The divine speeches in Job 38–41 come after Job's suffering and the extended arguments with his friends. They are not an abstract treatise on free will, nor do they give a simple explanation of why Job suffered. They confront Job with the Creator's knowledge, power, governance, and judgment.

Job 38 asks where Job was when the foundations of the earth were laid, who set the sea's bounds, and whether Job knows or governs the storehouses of snow, the paths of light, the ordinances of heaven, or the processes of weather. The questions repeatedly distinguish the One who made and governs from the creature who does not possess that comprehensive knowledge and power.

Job 39 turns to the birth, freedom, strength, and life of wild creatures. Job does not command their existence or govern their lives. His ability to observe and describe them does not make him their source.

The direct authority question appears in Job 40:8:

> “Wilt thou also disannul my judgment? wilt thou condemn me, that thou mayest be righteous?”

The issue is not whether Job may lament, ask questions, reason, or seek justice. The book itself records those things. The challenge concerns the attempt to invalidate God's judgment and condemn God so that the creature may establish his own righteousness as the final standard.

The descriptions of Behemoth and Leviathan continue to expose the limits of Job's power. Their precise identification is debated and is not necessary to the central point: the creature's ability to observe or describe does not confer mastery over the creature, much less authority over its Maker.

Job therefore contributes a clear distinction between human knowledge and divine governance. It does not settle every question about human choice, spiritual ability, or salvation. Its contribution is specific: the creature does not possess the authority to overturn the Creator's judgment.

## Isaiah 40–48: the LORD alone is Creator

Isaiah repeatedly places the living LORD over against idols, rival claims, and the limits of creaturely power.

Isaiah 40 asks who has measured the waters, marked off the heavens, weighed the mountains, or directed the Spirit of the LORD. It calls the hearer to look upon the heavenly host and consider the One who created them and calls them by name (40:12–26). The everlasting Creator does not faint or become weary; His understanding is unsearchable (40:28).

Isaiah 42:5 identifies the LORD as the Creator of the heavens and the earth and all that comes forth from them. Isaiah 42:8 declares that He will not give His glory to another or His praise to graven images. Isaiah 43:10 calls Israel as a witness, but the witness is not thereby made the Author or source of the acts being witnessed.

Isaiah 44 exposes the absurdity of the idol-maker who uses created material and human labor to fashion an object, then treats that object as a god. The problem is not the act of making an object; it is the exchange of the Creator for the creature and the assignment of divine status to what human hands have made.

Isaiah 45:9 gives the potter-and-clay image:

> “Shall the clay say to him that fashioneth it, What makest thou?”

The image establishes the asymmetry between Maker and made. The clay does not become the authority that defines the potter's purpose. This does not erase the human commands, choices, and accountability recorded elsewhere in Scripture. It does establish that the creature is not the originating authority over the Maker.

Isaiah 46:9–11 declares that God makes known the end from the beginning and that His counsel shall stand. Isaiah 48:12–13 again identifies the LORD as the first and the last, the One whose hand laid the foundation of the earth and spread out the heavens.

Isaiah's witness is consistent: mankind may learn, investigate, make, and act within Creation, but those capacities do not make mankind the source of Creation or the authority that establishes God's purpose.

## Romans 1:18–32: the exchange of Creator and creature

Romans 1 describes a human reversal. Paul says that truth is held down in unrighteousness; what may be known of God is manifested through what God has made; people fail to glorify Him as God or give thanks; their reasoning becomes vain and their hearts darkened. The passage then describes the exchange of God's glory for images of creatures and the worship and service of the creature rather than the Creator (Romans 1:18–25).

Paul does not describe mankind as having no capacity to know, reason, desire, or act. Those capacities are present in the people under indictment. The problem is their suppression and misdirection of truth, and the exchange by which the creature is given the place of the Creator.

Romans 1:28–32 continues with the refusal to retain God in knowledge and the resulting disordered conduct. The passage is not a warrant to label every scientist, philosopher, or person who disagrees with an interpretation as consciously suppressing God. Its claims must remain attached to Paul's argument and the conduct he describes.

Romans 1 does not condemn investigation of Creation. It condemns the suppression of truth and the worshipful reversal in which the creature is served rather than the Creator. Knowledge of created things is not itself the problem; the problem is the exchange of the true God for what is made.

## Colossians 2: knowledge must remain according to Christ

Colossians 2:8 is sometimes quoted as though Paul condemns every philosophical question or every form of human reasoning. The paragraph says more precisely what is at issue.

Paul urges believers to walk in Christ, rooted and built up in Him (2:6–7). He warns against being taken captive through philosophy and vain deceit, after human tradition and the rudiments of the world, “and not after Christ” (2:8). The controlling contrast is not thought versus no thought; it is teaching according to Christ versus teaching that takes believers captive and displaces Him.

The following verses identify Christ as the One in whom all the fullness of the Godhead dwells bodily and in whom believers are complete (2:9–10). Colossians 1:15–20 has already identified Him as the image of the invisible God, the One by whom all things were created and in whom all things consist. Colossians 2:3 says that in Him are hidden all the treasures of wisdom and knowledge.

The passage does not condemn every use of reason, every philosophical question, or disciplined inquiry. Nor does it permit an unfamiliar idea to be dismissed merely by attaching the label “vain deceit.” The content and governing source of the teaching must be examined. Paul's criterion is whether it is according to Christ.

## The Creator/creature distinction across these passages

The passages considered here have different settings and purposes:

| Passage | Immediate subject | Contribution to the question |
|---|---|---|
| Genesis 1–2 | Creation, mankind's formation, work, dominion, and command | Human life and responsibility are received within God's prior order |
| Genesis 3 | The challenge to God's Word and the human response | Perception, evaluation, desire, and action are real; they do not make the creature the source of the standard |
| Job 38–41 | Suffering, divine governance, creaturely limits, and judgment | Human knowledge and argument do not make the creature the Creator's judge |
| Isaiah 40–48 | The LORD's uniqueness and the futility of idols and rival claims | The Creator's authority is not transferred to a creature or human product |
| Romans 1:18–32 | Suppression and exchange of truth | The creature is wrongly given the place of the Creator |
| Colossians 2:6–15 | The danger of teaching not according to Christ | Knowledge and thought are not condemned; Christ remains their governing center |

Their convergence is the Creator/creature distinction. Their distinct arguments must remain visible. Job's speeches about suffering are not interchangeable with Isaiah's polemic against idols; Romans' account of the exchange is not a direct chronology of the origin of every human doctrine; Colossians' warning is not a blanket prohibition on philosophy or science.

## What this establishes about the human will

The phrase *free will* can refer to very different claims.

If it means that human beings genuinely perceive, reason, evaluate, desire, choose, act, respond, and bear responsibility, Scripture affirms those realities. Genesis 2–3 records them directly. The wider canon repeatedly addresses human beings with commands, warnings, invitations, promises, and judgments.

If it means that mankind has been entrusted with real responsibility and delegated authority within Creation, Scripture also affirms that. Genesis 1:26–28 gives dominion; Genesis 2:15 gives work in the garden; Genesis 2:19–20 records Adam's naming of the creatures.

If it means that the creature is self-originating, may establish the ultimate standard of good and evil, may disannul God's judgment, or may become the final authority over Creation's source and purpose, the passages examined do not support that claim. Job 40:8 challenges the attempt to disannul God's judgment. Isaiah 45:9 rejects the clay's claim to dictate to its Maker. Romans 1:25 names the exchange in which the creature is worshipped and served rather than the Creator.

The distinction can be stated plainly:

**Capacity is not sovereignty. Choice is not authorship. Responsibility is not ultimate authority. Knowledge is not the source of the thing known.**

This conclusion must not be overstated. It does not mean that every human definition is illegitimate, every scientific model is idolatry, every mistaken interpretation is deliberate rebellion, or every act of inquiry is a rejection of God. Human beings can investigate, describe, model, discover, correct, and exercise delegated responsibility. Those activities remain creaturely.

## The relation to salvation

This chapter establishes the distinction between creaturely capacity and Creator authority. It does not, by itself, provide the full canonical account of the human will in salvation.

The next question is more specific: Does Scripture identify fallen man's independent will as the originating cause of spiritual birth, faith, or salvation?

That question cannot be answered merely by defining choice or by pointing to a command. Nor can it be answered by citing one verse about divine initiative while ignoring passages that describe human response. The passages must be examined in their own contexts and then compared with the wider canonical testimony.

The Human Will and Salvation Reference Work contains twenty passage investigations and a cross-reference completion audit. Its distinctions include choice, responsibility, ability, willingness, divine initiative, regeneration, faith, and salvation. Those categories must not be collapsed, and their analytical order must not be mistaken for a chronological sequence.

The next chapter takes up that salvation question directly, tracing the Father's purpose, the Son's saving work, the Spirit's work, and the human response through the relevant Scriptural testimony.

## The Triple Constraint

**Biblical compliance:** Genesis, Job, Isaiah, Romans, and Colossians are read in their own literary and canonical contexts. Human choice and responsibility are affirmed where the texts affirm them; ultimate authority is not assigned to the creature where Scripture reserves it to God.

**Scientific coherence:** The chapter makes no claim that these passages supply a physical mechanism or settle an empirical scientific model. Linguistic, historical, and theological claims are limited to their evidence. A description of a process is distinguished from authorship of that process and from ultimate authority over its purpose.

**GUA-W consistency:** The distinction between Author and witness remains intact. Human beings are real witnesses and agents within Creation, but they are not Original Cause. The created will operates within a reality it did not originate.

## Conclusion

The biblical question is not whether mankind can know, reason, investigate, explain, desire, or choose. Scripture shows that mankind does these things and is accountable for them.

The question is whether those creaturely capacities grant mankind the authority of the Creator.

Genesis records the human being receiving life, work, dominion, and command. Genesis 3 records the challenge to God's Word and the human response. Job confronts the creature who would disannul God's judgment. Isaiah declares the uniqueness of the Creator and the limits of the clay. Romans describes the exchange of the Creator for the creature. Colossians directs knowledge and understanding to remain according to Christ.

**The creature may investigate the work. The creature does not become the Author.**

**One Author. One originating Word. One Creation. Many witnesses.**
