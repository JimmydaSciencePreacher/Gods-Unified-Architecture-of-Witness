# Chapter 15 — God's Purpose and the Saving Work of Christ

## The question returns to the Source

The preceding chapter distinguished creaturely capacity from Creator authority. Human beings genuinely perceive, desire, choose, believe, obey, and act. Those responses are real. They do not make the creature the originating source of life or the author of God's saving purpose.

The salvation question now comes into focus:

**Does Scripture identify fallen man's independent will as the originating cause of spiritual birth, faith, or salvation—or does it identify God as the source and initiator while preserving the human response?**

The Human Will and Salvation Reference Work has examined twenty assigned passages, completed a retrospective cross-reference audit, synchronized the entry-level findings, and recorded a final canonical synthesis. This chapter presents that work as a connected account. It is not twenty isolated proof-texts, and it does not claim that every question about salvation has been settled.

The governing order remains:

**Author → Word → Creation → Witness**

God alone is Original Cause. The Father purposes and gives; the Son is sent and accomplishes the saving work; the Spirit gives life, sanctifies, renews, and seals according to the passages that name His work. The Gospel is proclaimed and heard. People repent, believe, come, receive, obey, and continue. These statements must be allowed to stand together without making the human response the source of salvation or making that response unreal.

## Return first to the Beginning

Genesis 2:1–5 records God's completed work and the created order preceding man's formation in the narrative. Genesis 2:7 attributes man's formation and life to God. Genesis 2:15 records man's appointed place and work. Genesis 2:16–17 records God's command and its boundary.

Genesis 3 then shows human willing in operation: hearing, receiving communication, perceiving, evaluating, desiring, taking, eating, hiding, answering, and bearing accountability. The human response is not fictitious. But no human action in the narrative originates Creation, gives man life, establishes God's command, or becomes the final authority over good and evil.

The beginning therefore establishes the distinction that governs the salvation investigation: the creature truly acts within an order it did not originate.

Genesis 2:1–5 does not itself mention election, the Book of Life, the names given to the Son, or a timestamp for their inscription. The wider canon must be heard on those matters. Returning to the Beginning establishes the foundation; it does not authorize placing later phrases into Genesis that Genesis does not contain.

## The Father's purpose precedes the human response

The New Testament repeatedly locates God's saving purpose before the historical manifestation of Christ and before the human response described in the Gospel.

- **Ephesians 1:3–14** speaks of the Father choosing in Christ before the foundation of the world, predestinating according to the good pleasure of His will, and purposing and working all things after the counsel of His own will. Redemption is in Christ through His blood; the Gospel is heard and believed; the Holy Spirit of promise seals those who believe.
- **1 Peter 1:19–20** describes Christ as a lamb without blemish and without spot, foreordained before the foundation of the world and manifested in these last times.
- **2 Timothy 1:9** says God saved and called according to His own purpose and grace, given in Christ Jesus before the world began.
- **Titus 1:2** places the promise of eternal life, which God that cannot lie promised, before the world began.
- **John 17:5, 24** records the Son speaking of the glory He had with the Father before the world was and of the Father's love for Him before the foundation of the world.

These passages establish that God's purpose in Christ is not a human plan formed in reaction to an unexpected event. The saving purpose is God's; Christ's mission is not an afterthought originating in human choice.

The passages do not all describe the same aspect of that purpose. Foreordination of Christ, election in Christ, grace given before the world began, and the promise of eternal life are related witnesses, not interchangeable technical terms. Their common testimony is prior divine purpose.

### The Father gives people to the Son

John 6:37–40, John 10:27–30, and John 17:1–26 must be read in their own contexts.

In John 6, Jesus says that all the Father gives Him shall come to Him; He will not cast out the one who comes; He will lose nothing of all that the Father has given Him, but raise it up at the last day. He then says no one can come unless the Father draws him, and that coming is given by the Father (6:44, 65).

In John 10, Jesus describes His sheep as hearing His voice and following Him. He knows them, gives them eternal life, and declares that they shall never perish; no one can pluck them out of His hand or the Father's hand (10:27–29). He identifies the Father as greater than all and declares, “I and my Father are one” (10:30).

In John 17, the Son speaks of the men given Him out of the world, manifests the Father's name to them, keeps them, and prays for those who will believe through their word. The prayer extends beyond the first disciples to later believers (17:20). The Son's work, keeping, sending, and the future witness of the disciples all remain within the Father's purpose.

The relationship is explicit: the Father gives; the Son receives, keeps, gives life, and raises; those who belong to Him hear, come, believe, and follow. The passages do not say that every use of “given,” “drawn,” “come,” “hear,” “believe,” and “follow” is a technical synonym. Their distinct words and settings matter.

### When were the names written?

The Book of Life passages require careful handling.

Revelation 17:8 speaks of those whose names were not written in the book of life “from the foundation of the world.” The verse must be read in its complete sentence and in the context of the beast, the inhabitants of the earth, and the contrast between those who wonder after the beast and those whose names are in the book of life. The wording makes the foundation-of-the-world phrase directly relevant to the status of the names not written.

Revelation 13:8 requires a separate grammatical observation. In the KJV, “from the foundation of the world” follows the description of the Lamb “slain,” while the verse also speaks of those whose names are not written in the book of life. The phrase's syntactic attachment is disputed: interpreters connect it either with the Lamb's being slain or with the names not written. The verse should therefore be cited with that grammatical question visible, not as though its attachment were beyond dispute.

The two verses must not be collapsed into one another. Revelation 17:8 is the more direct witness for the foundation-of-the-world qualification concerning names not written; Revelation 13:8 is relevant but syntactically debated. Revelation 21:27 identifies those who enter the holy city as those written in the Lamb's book of life. Luke 10:20, Philippians 4:3, and Hebrews 12:23 also refer to names written in heaven or the book of life.

The wider canon separately establishes God's purpose before the world began and Christ's foreordination before the foundation of the world (Ephesians 1:3–14; 2 Timothy 1:9; Titus 1:2; 1 Peter 1:19–20). John records the Father giving people to the Son (John 6:37–40; 10:27–29; 17:2, 6, 9, 24). These witnesses belong together in the larger account of divine purpose, the Son's saving mission, and the identity of those who belong to Him. They do not all state the same proposition, and they do not by themselves narrate a separate timestamp or mechanism for the inscription of every individual name. We may affirm the prior purpose Scripture explicitly reveals without inventing an inscription event or a precise moment that Scripture does not narrate.

Genesis 2:1–5 is the foundational account of completed Creation before man's formation in the narrative. It does not explicitly say, “the Father wrote these names” or narrate the giving of individuals to the Son. Those claims must be supported from the passages that actually speak of election, foreordination, giving, and the Book of Life.

## Christ's saving work is the center, not a human achievement

The Father's purpose is accomplished through the Son. The New Testament does not present salvation as a human attempt to climb toward God or as a work by which people make themselves alive.

John 1:1–14 identifies the Word as existing in the beginning, as God, as the One through whom all things were made, and as the Word made flesh. John 3:16–18 presents the Father's giving of the Son and the purpose of His coming. John 6:37–40 identifies the Son's mission to receive those given by the Father, lose nothing, and raise them at the last day.

Romans 5:6–11 says Christ died for the ungodly, while we were yet sinners and enemies; reconciliation is through His death and salvation through His life. Romans 8:31–34 places God's giving of His Son and Christ's death, resurrection, and intercession at the center of assurance. Ephesians 1:7 identifies redemption through Christ's blood and forgiveness of sins according to the riches of God's grace. Colossians 1:13–22 describes deliverance, redemption, reconciliation, and peace through the blood of Christ's cross.

The Cross and Resurrection are not merely examples that persuade a person to save himself. They are the saving work of Christ proclaimed by the Gospel. The previous chapters have examined the Passion, Resurrection, appearances, and Ascension; this chapter considers their place in the wider account of God's purpose.

Hebrews 1:1–3 identifies the Son as the brightness of God's glory and the express image of His person, upholding all things by the word of His power, and as having by Himself purged our sins before sitting at the right hand of the Majesty on high. The saving work belongs to Christ. He is not a component of GUA-W; GUA-W is a map of the created order and its witness, not Christ Himself.

## God makes alive: grace, mercy, and the Spirit's work

### Ephesians 2:1–10

Paul describes the former condition as being dead in trespasses and sins, walking according to the course of this world and the desires of the flesh (2:1–3). The people described are not portrayed as lacking all desire or activity. Their former walk is active and disordered.

The decisive transition begins in verse 4: “But God, who is rich in mercy,” for His great love, “even when we were dead in sins, hath quickened us together with Christ.” Paul attributes the life-giving action to God. The passage continues: by grace are ye saved; God raises and seats believers with Christ; salvation is by grace through faith, not of works, so that no one may boast; believers are God's workmanship, created in Christ Jesus unto good works (2:5–10).

The movement is significant: the former condition, God's mercy and life-giving action, salvation by grace through faith, the exclusion of boasting, and the resulting walk in good works. Works are not the originating source or earning ground of salvation; they are the appointed result and walk of those created in Christ.

The Greek demonstrative “this” in Ephesians 2:8 has been discussed in relation to the phrase “by grace are ye saved through faith.” The grammatical gender of the demonstrative and its relation to the preceding clause require care. The verse clearly teaches salvation by grace through faith and excludes boasting; it should not be used as though its grammar alone, without argument, unambiguously says that the noun “faith” by itself is the antecedent of “that.” The wider canon speaks more directly about the divine granting of believing.

### Titus 3:3–8

Titus gives a compact account of the contrast between the former life and God's saving action.

Paul says that “we ourselves also were sometimes foolish, disobedient, deceived,” serving diverse lusts and pleasures, living in malice and envy, hateful and hating one another (3:3). Then comes the contrast: “But after that the kindness and love of God our Saviour toward man appeared,” not by works of righteousness which we have done, but according to His mercy He saved us, “by the washing of regeneration, and renewing of the Holy Ghost” (3:4–5).

The Spirit is then described as shed on us abundantly through Jesus Christ our Saviour, that being justified by grace we should be made heirs according to the hope of eternal life (3:6–7). Verse 8 turns to the good works that believers should maintain.

The source is God's mercy, not works of righteousness performed by the recipients. Regeneration and renewal of the Holy Ghost are expressly named. The passage joins salvation, the Spirit's renewal, justification by grace, inheritance, and the continuing call to good works.

Titus 3:3–7 does not, by itself, provide a complete chronological sequence of regeneration, faith, and justification. That is a limit of this passage considered alone, not a declaration that the wider canon has no further evidence. The reference work's cross-reference audit brings Romans 10, Ephesians 1, John 1, 3 and 6, Acts 13 and 16, Philippians 1:29, 2 Thessalonians 2:13–14, James 1:18, 1 Peter 1:3, 23, and 1 John 5:1 into the combined examination. Together these passages establish God's initiating and life-giving work, the Father's drawing and granting, the Spirit's sanctifying work, the Word and Gospel as means of proclamation, and the real human acts of hearing, attending, repenting, coming, and believing. They narrow the question substantially. They do not supply one exhaustive technical timetable specifying every moment and causal relation between regeneration and the first act of faith.

### James 1:16–18 and 1 Peter 1:3, 23

James warns against being deceived and says every good gift and every perfect gift is from above, coming down from the Father of lights. He then states: “Of his own will begat he us with the word of truth” (James 1:18). God is the subject; His own will and the Word of Truth are named in relation to begetting.

First Peter 1:3 blesses the God and Father of our Lord Jesus Christ, who according to His abundant mercy has begotten believers again unto a lively hope by the resurrection of Jesus Christ from the dead. First Peter 1:23 says they are born again, not of corruptible seed but of incorruptible, by the word of God which liveth and abideth for ever.

These texts join God's will and mercy, the Word, and new birth. They do not each supply an exhaustive chronology of every aspect of conversion. They do make it impossible to describe spiritual birth as self-originated by the human will.

## Faith and repentance: real responses, with divine initiative

The conclusion must not be made by deleting the human verbs. Scripture repeatedly records people hearing, receiving, repenting, coming, believing, confessing, obeying, and persevering. These are genuine responses, not fictional appearances.

The question is whether those responses are identified as independent originating causes of spiritual birth or salvation. The twenty investigations do not find that claim established. Several passages speak directly of divine initiative in relation to those responses.

### John 1:12–13 and John 3:3–8

John 1:12 records those who receive Christ and believe on His name. Verse 13 then describes their birth as not of blood, nor of the will of the flesh, nor of the will of man, but of God. The same unit therefore preserves reception and believing while expressly identifying God—not human will—as the source of birth.

John 3 tells Nicodemus that a man must be born again, and that birth is of the Spirit. Jesus then speaks of believing in the Son and having eternal life. The passage's wind/Spirit comparison describes the Spirit's sovereign operation; it does not give a complete technical chronology of every relation between new birth and first faith. The precise referent of “water” in John 3:5 remains an interpretive question in the reference work, with Ezekiel 36 and the wider canonical context considered.

### John 6: the Father draws and gives

John 6:35–45 and 63–65 places coming to Christ in relation to the Father's giving, drawing, and teaching. Jesus says no man can come unless the Father draw him (6:44), and later says no man can come unless it is given unto him of the Father (6:65). The Spirit quickens (6:63).

The passage establishes divine initiative and the necessity of coming to Christ. It does not make coming unreal; nor does it state a stopwatch chronology for the relation between regeneration and the first act of faith.

### Acts: repentance, opened hearts, and appointed belief

Acts 11:18 records the Jerusalem believers concluding that God has also granted the Gentiles repentance unto life. Acts 13:48 says that as many as were ordained to eternal life believed. The grammatical form in that verse must be treated carefully: the clause does not expressly name an agent in the appointment construction, even though the wider context is relevant to divine initiative. Acts 16:14 records that the Lord opened Lydia's heart so that she attended to the things spoken by Paul.

These passages preserve both God's action and the human response. The Gospel is preached; people hear and attend; they repent and believe. The texts do not present the messenger or hearer as the independent source of spiritual life.

### Romans 10: the Gospel is preached and heard

Romans 10:13–17 joins calling, believing, hearing, and preaching. Paul asks how people will believe in Him of whom they have not heard, and how they will hear without a preacher. He concludes that faith comes by hearing, and hearing by the word of God.

The Gospel is not an incidental accessory. It is the public means of proclamation through which the message is heard. The preacher is a real instrument; the hearer genuinely hears and believes. Romans 10 does not make the preacher or hearer the Original Cause of salvation.

Romans 9–11 must be read as one connected argument. Romans 9 addresses God's purpose, mercy, election, and the potter/clay image; Romans 10 addresses the preached word, Israel's hearing, confession, and belief; Romans 11 continues with Israel, the Gentiles, mercy, and the warning against boasting. Romans 10 must not be omitted to protect a reading of Romans 9, and Romans 9 must not be neutralized by isolating the commands and responses of Romans 10. Paul's whole argument retains divine mercy and the real proclamation and response.

### Philippians 1:29 and 2:12–13

Philippians 1:29 says that it is given to believers not only to believe on Christ but also to suffer for His sake. The Greek verb translated “it is given” is passive; the immediate preceding verse says the matter is “of God,” which supports identifying God as the contextual giver. The infinitive translated “to believe” is active: the Philippians themselves truly believe. The text places their real act of believing within what has been graciously granted. It does not specify the complete mechanism or chronology of regeneration and first faith.

Philippians 2:12–13 commands believers to work out their salvation with fear and trembling, “for it is God which worketh in you both to will and to do of his good pleasure.” The command is real, the believers' willing and doing are real, and God's operation is explicit. The passage does not force a choice between divine operation and human obedience. Nor does its present-tense wording create a complete technical timeline for conversion.

## The twenty investigations: the combined finding

The Human Will and Salvation Reference Work contains HW-001 through HW-020. Each assigned passage has one authoritative entry; the retrospective cross-reference completion audit has been performed; and the findings have been synchronized.

The central result is clear within the stated scope:

**None of the twenty assigned passages identifies fallen man's independent will as the originating cause of spiritual birth, faith, or salvation.**

Together, the passages repeatedly identify God as the source and acting initiator of spiritual birth, begetting, life-giving, mercy, renewal, drawing, granting, choosing, calling, and preserving. The Word/Gospel and its proclamation are identified as means through which the message is heard. The Spirit's work is expressly named. Human believing, repentance, hearing, coming, obedience, and perseverance remain real.

The reference work's canonical synthesis records the following dispositions:

| Question | Combined finding |
|---|---|
| Does an independent human will originate spiritual birth in the passages examined? | **Answered: no.** The passages identify God as source; John 1:13 expressly excludes human-will origin. |
| Is God the source of begetting, regeneration, and life-giving? | **Answered: yes.** John 1 and 3, James 1, 1 Peter 1, and Titus 3 directly contribute. |
| Is believing a real human act? | **Answered: yes.** Receiving, hearing, coming, and believing are all present. |
| Is believing included in divine gracious provision? | **Answered within Philippians 1:29's scope.** The grant includes believing and suffering for Christ. |
| Are the Word, Gospel, and proclamation involved in the means by which people hear and believe? | **Answered: yes.** Romans 10, Acts 16, James 1, and 1 Peter 1 contribute distinct testimony. |
| Can divine initiative and real human responsibility coexist in the same account? | **Answered: yes.** Deuteronomy 30, Joshua 24, Philippians 2:12–13, and the wider testimony preserve both. |
| Is the complete chronology and causal relation between regeneration and the first act of faith fully specified? | **Narrowed, not exhaustively settled.** Important relations are stated; no complete technical timetable is supplied by these twenty investigations. |

The completed phase does not mean every question about salvation is finished. It does mean the twenty assigned passage investigations and their canonical completion audit have reached their stated phase conclusion. This research milestone is not a claim that all theological questions are resolved, and it is not to be confused with Christ's words, “It is finished.”

## The questions that remain bounded

The reference work preserves several specific interpretive questions:

- The exact referent of “water” in John 3:5.
- Whether “washing of regeneration” in Titus 3:5 refers exclusively to water baptism, inward cleansing, or a joined image.
- The full semantic force of “foreknowledge” in Romans 8:29 and 1 Peter 1:2.
- The full grounds and scope of election/appointment, including grammatical limits in Acts 13:48 and Romans 9:22.
- The precise scope of “this” in Ephesians 2:8 and the referent of “from the beginning” in 2 Thessalonians 2:13.
- The complete chronological and causal relation between regeneration and the first act of faith.
- An exhaustive biblical definition of every faculty and operation of the human will.

These are not all the same kind of question. Some concern grammar, some lexical or theological interpretation, and some the limits of chronology. The cross-reference audit has narrowed them. It has not converted every one into a settled answer.

A bounded unresolved question is not evidence that Scripture lacks an answer. It is a report of what the examined texts and their canonical comparisons have—and have not—established.

## The Triple Constraint

**Biblical compliance:** The chapter reads the passages in their own contexts and then compares them canonically. It preserves both divine initiative and real human response. It does not make John 1:12 alone answer what John 1:13 says, isolate Romans 9 from Romans 10–11, or turn a passage-level limitation into a whole-canon silence.

**Scientific coherence:** Linguistic claims are limited by grammar and context. A passive verb does not automatically identify an unexpressed agent; an active verb does not establish independent originating cause; a tense or aspect does not provide a complete chronology. Historical, textual, and theological claims are distinguished from claims about physical mechanisms.

**GUA-W consistency:** God remains Original Cause. The Father purposes and gives; the Son accomplishes the saving work; the Spirit's work is named in the text; the Gospel is proclaimed; people hear, repent, believe, and obey. Witnesses testify to what is given; they do not originate the Source.

## Conclusion — salvation is of the Lord

The twenty investigations do not support the claim that fallen man's independent will originates spiritual birth, faith, or salvation. They do establish that human beings genuinely respond, believe, repent, obey, and are accountable. The same testimony attributes the source and initiative of salvation to God, names Christ's saving work, identifies the Spirit's life-giving and renewing work, and gives the Word and Gospel their appointed place.

The answer is not to erase the human response. It is to recognize the response in the place Scripture gives it.

**The Father purposes and gives. The Son saves. The Spirit gives life and renews. The Gospel is proclaimed. The hearer hears and believes.**

The derivative witness does not become Original Cause.

**One Author. One originating Word. One Creation. Many witnesses.**
