# Chapter 7 — Genesis 3: The Question of Authority and the Origin of Disconnected Knowledge

*Part III — The Departure and Its Consequences*

> **The creature can perceive, evaluate, desire, choose, and act. None of those capacities makes the creature the Author, or transfers to the creature the authority of the One who gave the Word.**

## The turning point in the account

The first six chapters have carried one continuous account:

- God is the Author and Creator.
- His Word precedes the creature's response.
- The created order is differentiated without becoming divided in its origin.
- The heavens, earth, and all their host are declared finished.
- Man receives life, place, provision, appointed work, permission, and prohibition.
- Human naming and recognition are real acts, but they do not make the human being the author of what is named.

Genesis 3 now records the first human departure from the command already given.

We must be exact about what changes. Genesis 3 does not say that mankind becomes the Creator, that every human capacity disappears, or that all knowledge becomes evil. It records a rival testimony about God's Word, a human response to that testimony, disobedience, judgment, and a changed condition.

The central question is therefore not merely, “Did Adam and Eve make a choice?” The text plainly records that they acted. The more precise question is:

**What did they receive, what did they evaluate, whose word did they follow, and what authority did their action presume?**

This chapter begins with Genesis 3 itself. Later Scripture will be brought in where it directly clarifies the account. We will not begin with a modern philosophical definition of free will and force the passage to fit it.

## Genesis 3:1 — The serpent questions the Word already given

> Now the serpent was more subtil than any beast of the field which the LORD God had made. And he said unto the woman, Yea, hath God said, Ye shall not eat of every tree of the garden?  
> — Genesis 3:1, KJV

The verse identifies the serpent as a creature made by the LORD God. The serpent is not an independent originating power equal to God. The narrative places him within the created order.

The serpent then speaks to the woman. His opening question concerns what God has said.

The prior command is recorded in Genesis 2:16–17. God gave broad permission concerning the trees of the Garden, then identified one tree from which the man was not to eat. Genesis 3:1 recasts that permission in a question that foregrounds the restriction.

The difference matters. God had said, in effect, that the man could freely eat of every tree except the one prohibited. The serpent opens by asking whether God had said they could not eat of every tree.

The question is not a neutral repetition of the command. It frames the Creator's word in a way that directs attention toward deprivation rather than the generous permission that preceded the prohibition.

The Hebrew word translated “subtil” is עָרוּם (*‘ārûm*), which can carry the sense of shrewd, crafty, or prudent depending on context. Genesis 2:25 describes the man and woman as naked, עֲרוּמִּים (*‘ărummîm*). The similar sound creates a literary wordplay often noted by readers: the humans are naked; the serpent is crafty. That observation is a feature of the Hebrew wording, not a complete explanation of the serpent's identity or motives.

The verse says the serpent is a creature God made and records his speech. It does not yet give the reader the full answer to every question about the serpent's origin. Later canonical passages identify the old serpent with the Devil and Satan (Revelation 12:9; 20:2). Those later witnesses clarify the canonical identity; they do not erase the immediate narrative.

**What changes in the conversation:** the command has already been given by God; now another speaker challenges the way that command is represented.

## Genesis 3:2–3 — The woman reports the command

> And the woman said unto the serpent, We may eat of the fruit of the trees of the garden:
> But of the fruit of the tree which is in the midst of the garden, God hath said, Ye shall not eat of it, neither shall ye touch it, lest ye die.  
> — Genesis 3:2–3, KJV

The woman answers by affirming permission to eat from the trees of the Garden and identifying a prohibition concerning the tree in the midst of the Garden.

Her report includes the words “neither shall ye touch it.” Genesis 2:16–17, as recorded in the KJV, states the prohibition against eating; it does not include that phrase.

That difference must be recorded without rushing to a conclusion. The text does not explain here whether the woman is paraphrasing, repeating an instruction given elsewhere, or adding a protective boundary in her report. We must not assert a motive the passage does not state.

Her wording also says “lest ye die,” while Genesis 2:17 states, “for in the day that thou eatest thereof thou shalt surely die.” The difference in wording is visible; the precise significance should be examined in context rather than exaggerated.

A further distinction matters: Genesis 2:16–17 says the command was given to the man before the woman is described as made in Genesis 2:21–22. Genesis 3:2–3 records the woman's report. The narrative does not provide a separate account of the exact occasion on which she received the command. We may not invent that missing scene.

**Textual finding:** the woman knows and reports that a prohibition exists. Her report is not word-for-word identical to the earlier KJV wording. That observation is secure; a claim about why the difference occurs requires further evidence.

## Genesis 3:4–5 — The serpent contradicts God and offers another account

> And the serpent said unto the woman, Ye shall not surely die:
> For God doth know that in the day ye eat thereof, then your eyes shall be opened, and ye shall be as gods, knowing good and evil.  
> — Genesis 3:4–5, KJV

The serpent now moves beyond questioning. He contradicts the consequence God stated: “Ye shall not surely die.”

He then supplies an alternative explanation of God's command and motive. He says their eyes will be opened and that they will be as gods, knowing good and evil.

The progression is clear:

1. God gives the command and states the consequence (Genesis 2:16–17).
2. The serpent questions the command's wording (Genesis 3:1).
3. The woman reports the prohibition (3:2–3).
4. The serpent directly contradicts the stated consequence (3:4).
5. The serpent offers an alternative account of what God knows and what eating will accomplish (3:5).

The issue is no longer simply that a question has been asked. A rival testimony now directly opposes the Word already given.

The serpent's words invite the woman to assess God's command through the serpent's account of God's motive and the promised result. The serpent presents disobedience as a route to a supposedly desirable gain.

The text does not say that knowledge itself is evil. Nor does it say that all inquiry, observation, or learning is rebellion. Genesis 1–2 has already shown human beings receiving a commission, observing creatures, naming them, and working within Creation. The departure in Genesis 3 is not the discovery of knowledge as such. It is the reception and pursuit of a rival account in opposition to the Creator's Word.

The phrase “as gods” and the expression “knowing good and evil” must be read within the whole narrative. We must not reduce them to a slogan that all moral discernment is forbidden. Genesis 3:22 later acknowledges that the man has become “as one of us, to know good and evil,” but the same verse immediately speaks of the tree of life and the danger of eating and living for ever. The later statement does not say that God's authority has been transferred to mankind.

**The first departure relevant to this book is authoritative and relational:** the creature is invited to treat a competing testimony as the measure by which the Creator's Word is evaluated.

## Genesis 3:6 — Seeing, evaluating, desiring, taking, and eating

> And when the woman saw that the tree was good for food, and that it was pleasant to the eyes, and a tree to be desired to make one wise, she took of the fruit thereof, and did eat, and gave also unto her husband with her; and he did eat.  
> — Genesis 3:6, KJV

This verse is central to the investigation of human willing because it records several distinguishable operations.

**Seeing:** the woman sees the tree.

**Evaluation:** she regards it as good for food and pleasant to the eyes.

**Desire:** she regards it as desirable to make one wise.

**Taking:** she takes the fruit.

**Eating:** she eats.

**Giving:** she gives also to her husband.

**Eating by the man:** he eats.

These actions belong together in the narrative, but they are not synonyms. Seeing is not the same as evaluating; evaluation is not the same as desire; desire is not the same as taking; taking is not the same as eating.

The verse also says that the woman gave the fruit to her husband “with her,” and he ate. It does not provide enough detail to reconstruct every moment of the serpent's conversation or to establish exactly where Adam stood during each preceding statement. Do not fill the silence with an invented scene.

Genesis 2:16–17 had already recorded God's command. Genesis 3:6 records the human action contrary to it. The narrative does not portray the eating as a physical accident or as an act performed by someone else in their place. The actors are named, and their actions are recorded.

This is a real exercise of creaturely capacity. But it is not the transfer of authorship. The woman does not become the Creator by evaluating the tree; Adam does not become the authority over the command by eating.

### The three evaluations must remain distinct

The verse gives three descriptions:

- Good for food.
- Pleasant to the eyes.
- Desired to make one wise.

It is tempting to turn these into a complete psychological formula or to make them identical to another passage's wording. That would exceed the text.

First John 2:16 later names “the lust of the flesh, and the lust of the eyes, and the pride of life.” The resemblance invites canonical comparison, but John is addressing the world and its desires in his own argument. He does not quote Genesis 3:6 or state that the two three-part descriptions are an exact lexical equation. We may compare them as related witnesses to disordered desire, but must not collapse their contexts.

James 1:13–15 also describes temptation, desire, conception, and sin. It helps clarify that desire and the resulting act are distinguishable. James is not supplying a transcript of the serpent's conversation or a complete chronology of every event in Eden. Its contribution must remain within its own context.

**Finding:** Genesis 3:6 shows human perception, evaluation, desire, and action operating after the competing testimony has been introduced and against the command already given.

## Genesis 3:7 — The eyes are opened, and the humans recognize their nakedness

> And the eyes of them both were opened, and they knew that they were naked; and they sewed fig leaves together, and made themselves aprons.  
> — Genesis 3:7, KJV

The serpent had said their eyes would be opened. The verse now records that the eyes of both were opened and that they knew they were naked.

The outcome must be read with the whole sentence. The knowledge described here is not presented as an uncomplicated elevation into divine wisdom. Their immediate recognition concerns nakedness, and their response is to sew fig leaves together and make coverings.

The text records a change in awareness and a human attempt to respond to it. It does not say that the coverings remove the transgression or restore the prior condition.

The humans act: they recognize, sew, and cover themselves. Their activity continues, but it does not undo what has happened.

## Genesis 3:8–10 — Hiding, fear, and the Author who calls

> And they heard the voice of the LORD God walking in the garden in the cool of the day: and Adam and his wife hid themselves from the presence of the LORD God amongst the trees of the garden.
> And the LORD God called unto Adam, and said unto him, Where art thou?
> And he said, I heard thy voice in the garden, and I was afraid, because I was naked; and I hid myself.  
> — Genesis 3:8–10, KJV

The man and woman hear the voice of the LORD God and hide among the trees.

God calls to Adam: “Where art thou?”

The text does not suggest that God lacks knowledge of Adam's location. The question initiates the encounter and brings Adam to answer. We should not make God a seeker of information in the same way a human investigator is.

Adam reports that he heard God's voice, was afraid because he was naked, and hid himself.

The contrast with Genesis 2:25 is important. The prior chapter said the man and his wife were naked and were not ashamed. Now Adam identifies nakedness and fear as the reasons for hiding.

The narrative records a changed human response to God's presence. The humans have not become the Author or escaped the Author's presence. They are still within the Creation God made, and God is the One who calls.

## Genesis 3:11–13 — Questioning, confession, and displaced responsibility

> And he said, Who told thee that thou wast naked? Hast thou eaten of the tree, whereof I commanded thee that thou shouldest not eat?
> And the man said, The woman whom thou gavest to be with me, she gave me of the tree, and I did eat.
> And the LORD God said unto the woman, What is this that thou hast done? And the woman said, The serpent beguiled me, and I did eat.  
> — Genesis 3:11–13, KJV

God's question directs Adam back to the command and the act of eating.

Adam answers by referring to the woman and to God's giving of her: “The woman whom thou gavest to be with me.” He then says, “she gave me of the tree, and I did eat.”

The final words matter. Adam does not deny that he ate. His answer points to another person's action and to God's gift, but the text still records his own admission.

God then asks the woman what she has done. She answers that the serpent beguiled her and that she ate.

The account preserves more than one factor:
- The serpent's deception.
- The woman's eating.
- Adam's eating.
- Adam's response when questioned.
- The woman's response when questioned.

The text does not say that deception erased human action. It also does not say that the presence of human action makes the serpent's deception unreal. Both must remain in the account.

The narrative also records a movement toward displaced responsibility. Adam points to the woman and to God's giving; the woman points to the serpent's beguiling. Neither answer changes the stated acts of eating.

**The Author asks; the creatures answer.** The human responses do not rewrite the command or determine the judgment.

## Genesis 3:14–15 — Judgment and the divine declaration of enmity

> And the LORD God said unto the serpent, Because thou hast done this, thou art cursed above all cattle, and above every beast of the field; upon thy belly shalt thou go, and dust shalt thou eat all the days of thy life:
> And I will put enmity between thee and the woman, and between thy seed and her seed; it shall bruise thy head, and thou shalt bruise his heel.  
> — Genesis 3:14–15, KJV

God addresses the serpent. The serpent does not determine the terms of his own judgment.

Verse 15 begins with divine initiative: “I will put enmity.” The conflict is declared by God. It is not a recovery plan proposed by Adam and Eve, and it is not a future controlled by the serpent.

The verse speaks of enmity between the serpent and the woman, and between their respective seed. The Hebrew noun זֶרַע (*zera‘*) can refer to seed or offspring and may be collective. The verse's language must be read with its canonical witnesses rather than forced into an exclusively individual or exclusively collective meaning before that comparison is made.

The KJV says, “it shall bruise thy head, and thou shalt bruise his heel.” We retain the KJV wording when quoting it. The pronouns and the poetic parallelism should not be made to carry a conclusion beyond what the Hebrew and wider context establish.

### The canonical witness to the promised conflict

Later Scripture supplies important clarification:

- **Revelation 12:9** identifies the great dragon as “that old serpent,” called the Devil and Satan. This explicitly identifies the serpent in Revelation's vision; the apocalyptic imagery must still be read in its own context.
- **Romans 16:20** says the God of peace shall bruise Satan under the believers' feet shortly. God remains the acting subject.
- **Hebrews 2:14–15** identifies Christ's participation in flesh and blood and His death as the means by which He destroys the one having the power of death, that is, the devil, and delivers those held in bondage through fear of death.
- **1 John 3:8** says the Son of God was manifested to destroy the works of the devil.
- **Colossians 2:14–15** describes Christ's triumph over principalities and powers through the Cross.

These passages provide canonical witness to the conflict and victory over the devil. They do not all quote Genesis 3:15, and their distinct arguments must remain visible.

**The theological finding:** the Author declares the conflict and the victory's direction. The creature does not author the promise of redemption.

## Genesis 3:16–19 — Consequences for the woman, the man, and the ground

The next verses record God's words to the woman and the man.

Genesis 3:16 addresses the woman concerning sorrow and conception/childbearing, and her relation to her husband. The verse's wording must be preserved. It is a divine pronouncement following the transgression; it should not be converted into a blanket authorization for every form of domination or abuse. Nor should the actual wording be removed because it is difficult.

Genesis 3:17 identifies Adam's action in relation to the command:

> Because thou hast hearkened unto the voice of thy wife, and hast eaten of the tree, of which I commanded thee, saying, Thou shalt not eat of it: cursed is the ground for thy sake; in sorrow shalt thou eat of it all the days of thy life;  
> — Genesis 3:17, KJV

God explicitly connects Adam's hearkening to his wife's voice and his eating with the command God had given. The text does not describe Adam as a passive object with no action of his own.

The ground is cursed for Adam's sake. Genesis 3:18 describes thorns and thistles and the herb of the field. Genesis 3:19 describes bread eaten in the sweat of his face until he returns to the ground.

The connection to Genesis 2:15 is important. Work was appointed before the Fall: Adam was placed in the Garden to dress and keep it. Genesis 3 does not introduce work for the first time. It describes altered conditions of labor, sorrow, and the ground.

The last clause of verse 19 returns to Genesis 2:7:

> for out of it wast thou taken: for dust thou art, and unto dust shalt thou return.  
> — Genesis 3:19, KJV

The man who received life from God and was formed from dust is now told of his return to dust. The text does not present man as the source or sustainer of his own life.

### The canonical clarification of Adam's role

Romans 5:12–21 develops the relation between Adam's transgression, sin, death, and the contrast with Christ's obedience and righteousness. Paul makes Adam's act central to the entrance of sin and death into the human story, and sets Christ's work over against it.

First Timothy 2:13–14 refers to Adam's formation before Eve and says the woman was deceived and was in the transgression. This is a later canonical statement about the events; it should be read in the argument of 1 Timothy, not used to erase the Genesis account's record that Adam also ate and was directly addressed by God.

The canonical witness confirms that Genesis 3 is not an isolated moral tale. It is foundational to the later Scriptural account of Adam, sin, death, and Christ. But Romans 5 and 1 Timothy 2 contribute their own arguments; they do not replace the wording of Genesis.

## Genesis 3:20–21 — Naming and provision continue after the transgression

> And Adam called his wife's name Eve; because she was the mother of all living.
> Unto Adam also and to his wife did the LORD God make coats of skins, and clothed them.  
> — Genesis 3:20–21, KJV

Adam names his wife Eve, and the verse gives the reason: she was the mother of all living.

The act of naming continues after the transgression. It remains a human act of recognition; it does not make Adam the source of life.

The next verse attributes provision to God. The LORD God makes coats of skins and clothes them.

The wording must be allowed to stand. The text says God made garments of skins and clothed the man and woman. It does not explicitly name an animal, narrate a sacrifice, or describe a ritual mechanism in these verses. Those claims may be examined through wider canonical evidence, but they must not be inserted into Genesis 3:21 as though stated there.

Even after the transgression and judgment, the account records God's action and provision. Human beings do not become self-sufficient authors of their own restoration.

## Genesis 3:22–24 — The tree of life and guarded access

> And the LORD God said, Behold, the man is become as one of us, to know good and evil: and now, lest he put forth his hand, and take also of the tree of life, and eat, and live for ever:
> Therefore the LORD God sent him forth from the garden of Eden, to till the ground from whence he was taken.
> So he drove out the man; and he placed at the east of the garden of Eden Cherubims, and a flaming sword which turned every way, to keep the way of the tree of life.  
> — Genesis 3:22–24, KJV

The passage records God's statement concerning the man's knowledge of good and evil and His action concerning access to the tree of life.

The expression “as one of us” must be interpreted within the verse and the wider Scriptural witness. It is not a declaration that mankind has become God, nor that divine authority has been transferred to humanity.

The stated concern is that the man might take from the tree of life, eat, and live for ever. God sends him forth from the Garden and places cherubims and a flaming sword to keep the way of the tree of life.

The repeated verb translated “keep” links the passage to the earlier account of man's appointed work in Genesis 2:15, though the subjects and objects differ. In Genesis 2:15 the man is placed to keep the Garden; in Genesis 3:24 the way to the tree of life is guarded by divine appointment. The shared English word invites attention, but it does not make the two scenes identical.

The final action is God's: He drives out the man and guards the way. The man does not determine his own access to the tree of life.

The chapter ends with a boundary established and maintained by God. The human transgression has consequences, but it has not made the creature sovereign over life, judgment, or the created order.

## What changed—and what did not

Genesis 3 records a genuine departure. It does not record a transfer of authorship.

| Before the transgression | In and after Genesis 3 |
|---|---|
| God gives the command (2:16–17). | The serpent questions and contradicts the command (3:1–5). |
| Man and woman receive provision (2:9, 16). | The woman evaluates, desires, takes, and eats; Adam also eats (3:6). |
| They are naked and not ashamed (2:25). | They know they are naked, cover themselves, fear, and hide (3:7–10). |
| Man receives appointed work in the Garden (2:15). | The ground is cursed and labor is marked by sorrow and toil (3:17–19). |
| God gives the command and its boundary (2:16–17). | God identifies the transgression, judges, sends the man out, and guards access to life (3:11–24). |

Several things remain true:

- God remains the Creator.
- The humans remain creatures.
- The human capacity to perceive, evaluate, desire, speak, and act remains visible.
- Human actions remain accountable.
- God's authority over the command and its consequence is not transferred.
- God's action and provision continue after the transgression.

**The human will acts, but it does not become the Author.**

## The origin of disconnected knowledge: a carefully bounded finding

This book has already established in Chapter 3 that Theology addresses the ultimate WHY of the One Creation, while the differentiated sciences investigate HOW and their legitimate proximate WHY questions within their respective domains.

We must not now claim that Genesis 3 records the historical birth of modern science, physics, biology, chemistry, or the academic separation of disciplines. The text does not use those categories.

Genesis 1–2 already records differentiated works, appointed functions, human observation, naming, cultivation, and responsibility. Differentiation itself is not the Fall.

What Genesis 3 does record is the first human departure in which a competing testimony about the Creator and His command is received and acted upon against the Word already given.

The distinction is:

**Differentiation is not disconnection.**

The sciences can investigate distinct aspects of One Creation without becoming interchangeable and without losing the unity of the Creation they investigate. The departure in Genesis 3 is not the existence of distinct kinds of knowledge. It is the creature's departure from the Author's Word as the governing testimony.

The later canonical witness in Romans 1:18–25 describes the suppression of truth and the exchange of the Creator for the creature. Paul does not condemn the investigation of created things in that passage. He identifies the exchange in which the creature is worshipped and served rather than the Creator.

Colossians 2:6–15 likewise warns against being taken captive by teaching not according to Christ. Its immediate concern is not a blanket condemnation of philosophy, learning, or scientific inquiry. Its stated criterion is whether the teaching is according to Christ.

The bounded conclusion is therefore:

**Genesis 3 records the origin of the human departure from the Author's Word in the beginning narrative. Romans 1 later describes the Creator/creature exchange explicitly. Neither passage warrants the claim that scientific differentiation itself is a product of the Fall.**

Knowledge remains possible. Investigation remains possible. Human reasoning and discovery remain real. The question is whether the witness recognizes the Creator as the ultimate source and purpose, or treats a creaturely account as ultimate authority.

## The human will: capacity, responsibility, and authority

We can now return to the question that has been investigated across the Human Will and Salvation Reference Work.

Genesis 3 does not give a modern abstract definition of free will. It shows human willing in operation:

- Hearing and receiving communication.
- Recalling and reporting a command.
- Seeing.
- Evaluating.
- Desiring.
- Taking.
- Eating.
- Giving.
- Heeding.
- Hiding.
- Fearing.
- Answering.
- Naming.
- Acting within consequences.

These operations are real. The narrative does not present the humans as physically compelled to eat. It records their acts and their accountability.

But the passage does not say that human capacity grants independent ultimate sovereignty. The ability to evaluate does not make the human the source of the standard. The ability to act does not make the human the author of the command. The ability to name does not make the human the creator of what is named.

The Human Will and Salvation Reference Work makes additional distinctions that this chapter must not collapse:

1. **Choice** — selecting or responding among alternatives.
2. **Responsibility** — accountability for an action or omission.
3. **Ability** — capacity to perform the particular action under examination.
4. **Willingness** — desire, intention, or readiness.
5. **Divine initiative** — what God originates or performs.
6. **Regeneration / spiritual birth** — the giving or origin of spiritual life.
7. **Faith** — believing or trusting; its act, object, source, and relation to spiritual birth must be examined.
8. **Salvation** — God's saving purpose and accomplishment, with reception, application, perseverance, and final completion distinguished.

Genesis 3 directly establishes much about perception, evaluation, desire, action, and responsibility. It establishes the Creator/creature boundary. It does not, by itself, settle every later question about the source of saving faith, the relation of regeneration to faith, or the complete order of salvation.

Those questions are not abandoned. They are carried into the canonical investigations that address them directly. The Beginning supplies the foundation; later Scripture supplies further testimony. We must neither make Genesis 3 answer what it does not state nor ignore the passages that do address the remaining questions.

## The convergence of the first seven chapters

The book is one account, not seven unrelated essays.

- **Chapter 1 — One Author, One Word, One Creation:** establishes the governing order, Author → Word → Creation → Witness.
- **Chapter 2 — The Answer Before the Question:** establishes that discovery recognizes what is already present; the question does not create its answer.
- **Chapter 3 — Theology and the Differentiated Sciences:** establishes Theology's place as the Queen of the Sciences, addressing ultimate WHY while the differentiated sciences investigate HOW and proximate WHY.
- **Chapter 4 — Genesis 1: The Word Establishes Order:** follows the Creator's Word and the differentiated works of Creation.
- **Chapter 5 — Genesis 2:1–5: Completion Before the Further Account:** examines the finished heavens, earth, and host, God's rest, blessing, sanctification, and the field conditions.
- **Chapter 6 — The Garden, Humanity, Purpose, and Relationship:** follows man's formation, placement, provision, purpose, command, naming, and joining.
- **Chapter 7 — Genesis 3: The Question of Authority and the Origin of Disconnected Knowledge:** records the challenge to the Word, the human response, the transgression, and the consequences.

The movement is continuous:

**Author's Word → Created order → Creaturely reception and responsibility → Rival testimony → Human departure → Divine judgment and continuing purpose.**

The architecture has not changed. The investigation has recognized another relationship within the same account.

## What we carry into Chapter 8

Genesis 3 ends with the man sent out of the Garden and the way to the tree of life guarded.

The next chapter must not jump over that consequence. Genesis 4 begins to show human life outside the Garden, including worship, offering, the warning to Cain, the murder of Abel, and the consequences that follow.

The first question in Genesis 4 is not merely what Cain did. It is what God said to Cain before the act, how Cain responded, and what the text records about sin, desire, responsibility, and action.

We will examine Genesis 4:6–7 carefully. The warning precedes the murder. The text's own order matters.

**Chapter 8 — Genesis 4: The First Recorded Human Consequences.**

The same governing distinction remains:

**The creature is responsible for what the creature does. The creature does not become the Author of the standard by which that action is judged.**
