# Chapter 11 — The Works and Signs of Jesus

## The works bear witness to the One who performs them

The Gospel accounts do not present the works of Jesus Christ as disconnected curiosities. They place His words and deeds within the testimony concerning who He is.

The governing order of GUA-W remains:

**Author → Word → Creation → Witness**

But this chapter requires an essential distinction. Jesus Christ is not merely another witness within Creation, nor is He a component contained by GUA-W. Scripture identifies Him as the Christ, the Son of God, and the Word made flesh. The architecture is God's map of Creation; it is not God, and it is not Jesus Christ.

John opens his Gospel by declaring that the Word was in the beginning, that the Word was with God, that the Word was God, and that all things were made by Him. John then declares that the Word was made flesh and dwelt among us (John 1:1–3, 14). Hebrews 1:1–3 speaks of the Son as the One by whom God made the worlds and the One upholding all things by the word of His power. Colossians 1:15–17 and 1 Corinthians 15:45–49 add their own testimony and must be read in their contexts.

Jesus identifies Himself as “the way, the truth, and the life” (John 14:6). His works therefore cannot be examined as though the central question were merely how an unusual event occurred. The Gospel writers direct attention to the One who speaks and acts.

## The signs have a stated purpose

John provides an especially clear statement of the relationship between the recorded signs and their purpose.

At Cana, after Jesus turned water into wine, John records:

> “This beginning of miracles did Jesus in Cana of Galilee, and manifested forth his glory; and his disciples believed on him.” — John 2:11, KJV

At the close of his account, John explains that Jesus did many other signs in the presence of His disciples that were not written in that book. The signs that were written were recorded “that ye might believe that Jesus is the Christ, the Son of God; and that believing ye might have life through his name” (John 20:30–31).

The text itself therefore identifies the signs as testimony. They are not spectacle detached from identity. They direct the reader toward the person of Jesus and the stated purpose of belief and life through His name.

This statement is John's explicit explanation of his written selection. It should not be turned into a claim that every Gospel writer uses every event in precisely the same way or that every extraordinary event is called a sign in every passage.

## Scripture uses several descriptions of the works

The New Testament uses words commonly translated as *miracles*, *signs*, *wonders*, and *mighty works*. These descriptions overlap in some contexts, but they should not be treated as interchangeable labels without examining the wording and setting of each passage.

Acts 2:22 describes Jesus of Nazareth as “a man approved of God” among the people “by miracles and wonders and signs.” Hebrews 2:3–4 speaks of the great salvation first spoken by the Lord and confirmed by those who heard Him, God also bearing witness with signs, wonders, divers miracles, and gifts of the Holy Ghost.

The vocabulary itself is part of the evidence. A sign may point beyond the event to the identity or authority of the One who performs it. A wonder may describe the effect upon those who see it. A mighty work emphasizes the deed's power. The precise meaning in any passage depends on its language and context, not on an imposed universal definition.

The register of Investigation II consequently includes healings, deliverances, provision, authority over nature, raising the dead, and events surrounding Jesus' coming and identity. It keeps these categories distinguishable rather than placing every extraordinary event into one undifferentiated list.

## The works reveal authority

Across the Gospels, Jesus speaks and acts with authority. The form of the event differs, but the narratives repeatedly direct attention to His word, command, presence, or action.

In the calming of the storm, the disciples ask, “What manner of man is this, that even the winds and the sea obey him!” (Matthew 8:27). The question arises from what they have witnessed. Mark 4:35–41 and Luke 8:22–25 preserve parallel accounts, each with its own presentation.

In the healing of the paralytic, Jesus first says, “Son, thy sins be forgiven thee.” When scribes question His authority to forgive sins, He heals the man and states the purpose: “that ye may know that the Son of man hath power on earth to forgive sins” (Mark 2:1–12; compare Matthew 9:1–8 and Luke 5:17–26). The healing is not isolated from the authority claim made in the same account.

In John 5:36, Jesus says that the works the Father gave Him to finish bear witness that the Father sent Him. In John 10:25, 37–38, He again speaks of His works in relation to the testimony concerning Him and the Father. The works are not presented as an independent source of authority apart from His identity and relation to the Father.

These passages do not authorize the investigator to invent a physical mechanism behind each event. They do establish that the Gospel testimony connects the works with Jesus' authority and identity.

## The witness is not the source of the work

The event accounts also preserve the reality of human participation without assigning the work's originating authority to the participants.

At Cana, Jesus gives the instructions. The servants fill the waterpots, draw, and carry the result to the governor of the feast. The governor tastes the wine; the disciples witness the event; John records its significance (John 2:1–11). The servants' actions are real and necessary to the narrated sequence, but the sign is attributed to Jesus.

In the healing of the centurion's servant, the centurion recognizes authority and asks Jesus to speak the word. Matthew and Luke preserve the request through their distinct narrative presentations (Matthew 8:5–13; Luke 7:1–10). The messengers convey the request in Luke's account; they do not become the source of the healing.

At the tomb of Lazarus, Jesus commands that the stone be taken away, calls Lazarus forth, and then directs those present to loose him and let him go (John 11:1–44). The people participate in the actions described. They do not originate the life by which Lazarus comes forth. The narrative itself places the sign in relation to the glory of God and the belief that the Father sent Jesus.

These accounts show that obedience, service, conveyance, observation, and reporting may all be real parts of an event without becoming its originating cause. The distinction is not a denial of human action. It is a recognition of who acts with the authority attributed to Him in the text.

The three accounts are not identical mechanisms. Their settings, actions, and narrative purposes remain distinct. Their shared testimony concerns the authority of Jesus, not a formula that can be abstracted from the passages and applied as though every work follows the same sequence.

## The works are diverse; the witness is unified

The Gospel record includes works across several broad groupings.

### Provision and transformation

At Cana, water is made wine (John 2:1–11). The multitudes are fed in the accounts of the five thousand and the four thousand (Matthew 14:13–21; 15:32–39; Mark 6:30–44; 8:1–9; Luke 9:10–17; John 6:1–14). The coin in the fish's mouth is recorded in Matthew 17:24–27. These accounts differ in circumstance and detail. The feeding of the five thousand and the feeding of the four thousand are separate events, not duplicate counts of one event.

### Authority over nature

Jesus stills the storm (Matthew 8:23–27; Mark 4:35–41; Luke 8:22–25), walks upon the sea (Matthew 14:22–33; Mark 6:45–52; John 6:16–21), and is associated with two miraculous catches of fishes in distinct accounts (Luke 5:1–11; John 21:1–14). The first catch and the post-resurrection catch should not be collapsed merely because both involve an abundance of fish.

### Healing and restoration

The Gospels record the cleansing of lepers, the healing of the paralytic, the restoration of sight and hearing, the healing of the withered hand, the healing of the woman with the issue of blood, the healing of the centurion's servant, and many other works. Some accounts appear in more than one Gospel. Their parallels must be compared carefully; differences in detail should not be erased to produce an artificially uniform narrative.

The Gospel writers also provide grouped summaries of many healings. Such summaries are part of the record even when every individual healed person is not named. John 20:30–31 explicitly reminds the reader that the written selection is not an exhaustive account of every sign Jesus performed.

### Deliverance

The accounts of Jesus casting out devils and unclean spirits are also varied. They include the man in the synagogue, the Gadarene or Gerasene account, the daughter of the Syrophoenician woman, the boy afflicted by an unclean spirit, and other recorded deliverances. The narratives describe what Jesus does and how people respond. The language and details of each passage remain important; modern diagnostic categories should not be imposed upon the text as though the Gospel writers used them.

### Raising the dead

The Gospels record the raising of Jairus' daughter, the widow's son at Nain, and Lazarus (Matthew 9:18–26; Mark 5:21–43; Luke 8:40–56; Luke 7:11–17; John 11:1–44). Each account has its own setting and testimony.

These raisings must also be distinguished from the resurrection of Jesus Christ. The raising of another person and Christ's own resurrection are not interchangeable events. The latter has its own central place in the Gospel and apostolic witness and is treated in the separate Passion and Resurrection investigation.

## The works surrounding His coming and identity

The wider Investigation II register includes events surrounding the coming and identity of Jesus: the announcements concerning His birth, the testimony at His baptism, the temptation in the wilderness, the Transfiguration, and other events. Not every one of these is called a miracle by the text. Prophecy, angelic appearance, divine testimony, temptation, and physical healing are distinct kinds of narrated event.

The Transfiguration, for example, is not an ordinary healing. The Gospel accounts record Jesus' transfiguration, the appearance of Moses and Elias, and the voice from the cloud (Matthew 17:1–9; Mark 9:2–10; Luke 9:28–36). Its significance belongs to the Scriptural testimony concerning Jesus and should not be reduced to a generic miracle category.

The register also distinguishes works performed directly by Jesus from signs and wonders performed by apostles and disciples in His name after His ascension. Acts records those later works, but their agency and attribution differ. The apostles are not the originating source of the authority they invoke. Their actions belong to a later witness and ministry, not to the category of works Jesus performed during His earthly ministry.

## The Triple Constraint in the examination of the works

The works of Jesus are examined under the same three considerations that govern every material claim in GUA-W.

**Biblical compliance** requires attention to the actual Gospel text, parallel accounts, immediate context, and wider canonical witness. A parallel account should be compared rather than silently harmonized. An event should not be called a sign, healing, prophecy, or resurrection without regard to the text's own presentation.

**Scientific coherence** requires accuracy about what historical, linguistic, medical, physical, or other disciplinary evidence can establish. A unique event reported in Scripture should not be treated as though a modern experiment has reproduced it when no such experiment exists. Nor should the absence of a modern reproduction be represented as an experimental disproof of the particular report. The relevant historical and textual questions remain open to disciplined examination, and claims about physical mechanisms require evidence appropriate to those claims.

**GUA-W consistency** preserves God as Original Cause and the distinction between Author, Word, Creation, and witness. Jesus Christ's Scriptural identity is not diminished by an architectural description. A witness who sees or reports a work does not become its source. No individual miracle is assigned to an architectural stage merely because a verbal resemblance can be found.

Theological inquiry is a Science within GUA-W and is not exempt from disciplined examination. The Gospel testimony is examined through its text, language, historical setting, and canonical relationships. Physical or biological claims are examined with the evidence and methods of their own disciplines. These methods remain distinct while investigating the One Creation.

## What the current record establishes—and what remains open

The existing Investigation II record contains thirty-five dedicated event-level case studies. Its master register also records additional events, grouped Gospel summaries, parallel accounts, events surrounding Jesus' coming, and apostolic works in His name. The register is a coverage record, not a declaration that every listed event has received a completed individual examination.

The governing Investigation II study remains open. Event-by-event work, further language study, historical examination, appropriate scientific review, and full Triple Constraint dispositions remain in progress. The written case studies are not made complete merely by appearing in the register. Their conclusions must be read within their stated scope and evidence.

Several questions therefore remain properly bounded:

- How should the Gospel writers' different selections, groupings, and narrative arrangements be understood?
- Which events are explicitly described as signs, wonders, miracles, or mighty works, and which are narrated without those labels?
- What historical conclusions are warranted by the available sources, and what questions remain theological or textual?
- How should the distinct accounts of particular events be compared without forcing every difference into a single harmonized narrative?
- How do the works of Jesus relate to the signs performed by His servants after His ascension?

These open questions do not erase what the passages plainly record. The Gospel writers explicitly identify the purpose of John's written signs; Jesus Himself identifies His works as testimony; and the narratives repeatedly present His authority through what He says and does.

## The works bear witness to Christ

The signs and works are diverse. Their settings, recipients, actions, and outcomes differ. Some are recorded in multiple Gospels; others appear in one. Some are called signs; others are described through the event itself. Some involve human participation; others emphasize a command or word. Their differences are part of the testimony.

The unifying point is not a mechanism imposed upon all the accounts. It is the identity of Jesus Christ and the authority the Scriptures attribute to Him. John records the signs so that readers may believe that Jesus is the Christ, the Son of God, and have life through His name.

**The witness reports the work. The witness does not become its source. The works bear testimony to the One Scripture identifies.**

**One Author. One originating Word. One Creation. Many witnesses.**
