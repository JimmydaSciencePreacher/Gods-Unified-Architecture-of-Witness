# Cellular Recognition, Receptor Signaling, and Symbiotic Accommodation

**Status:** Evidence-led mechanistic investigation  
**Repository placement:** Biological organization, chemical information transfer, partner compatibility, and development  
**Governing order:** Author → Word → Creation → Witness  
**Central question:** How does a living cell detect molecular features from another organism, distinguish among possible responses, and translate that detection into a biological change?

## 1. The question sharpened

The preceding chemical-transfer audit separated four things:

1. the material transferred;
2. the information-bearing features of that material;
3. the receiver capable of detecting it;
4. the response that follows.

The present investigation moves one step further. It asks what happens inside the receiving organism between exposure and response.

A cell does not consciously recognize a partner. In this investigation, *recognition* means a biochemical process: molecular features interact with receptor proteins or other sensing machinery, and the receiver's internal signaling networks produce a response.

The central distinction is:

**Detection is not the same as admission. Admission is not the same as successful integration.**

A receptor can detect a molecule without the organism ultimately permitting the other organism to enter or establish a persistent association.

## 2. Strong mechanistic model: plant root endosymbiosis

Root associations between plants and arbuscular mycorrhizal fungi (AM fungi), and between legumes and nitrogen-fixing rhizobia, are among the best-characterized systems for studying molecular recognition between distinct organisms.

The 2026 focused review by Fougner-Økland, Spezzati, and Parys describes how plant roots and microbial partners exchange chemical information, how plant LysM receptor families perceive microbial carbohydrate signals, and how signal transduction contributes to accommodation of symbionts within living plant cells.

The 2025 review by López-Ráez and colleagues compares mechanisms across arbuscular mycorrhiza, rhizobium–legume symbiosis, ectomycorrhiza, and other root–microbe interactions. It emphasizes both shared signaling elements and important differences among systems.

These are not lichen experiments. They provide a better-resolved comparative model for cellular recognition, not permission to assign the same receptor mechanisms to lichens, orchids, insects, or all symbioses.

## 3. The exchange is reciprocal

In arbuscular mycorrhizal associations, the plant is not simply waiting for a fungal message.

When phosphate availability is low, plant roots increase production and release of strigolactones into the surrounding soil. These compounds stimulate AM fungal spore germination and hyphal branching, increasing the opportunity for contact.

In response, AM fungi release compounds commonly called **Myc factors**, including:

- mycorrhizal lipochitooligosaccharides (Myc-LCOs);
- short-chain chitooligosaccharides (COs), including CO4 and CO5.

The plant's root cells perceive these microbial compounds through receptor systems at the cell surface. The exchange is therefore reciprocal: plant-derived signals affect fungal behavior, and fungal-derived molecules affect plant signaling.

This does not mean that every molecule in the exchange has one exclusive function or that the partners consciously negotiate. It is a biochemical interaction shaped by the organisms' physiology and environment.

## 4. The receiver: LysM receptor systems

Many plant receptors involved in chitin-related recognition contain LysM (lysine motif) domains. Certain LysM receptor-like kinases and receptor-like proteins bind or participate in the perception of chitin-derived oligosaccharides and lipochitooligosaccharides.

In root symbiosis, receptor complexes include members of two broad groups:

- **LYK-type receptors**, generally possessing active intracellular kinase domains;
- **LYR-type receptors**, many of which have altered kinase motifs and may function as co-receptors, structural partners, or signaling components.

The 2026 review documents receptor-family diversity across land plants and emphasizes that receptor function differs among plant species and lineages. A receptor's name or membership in a family does not establish that it performs the same task in every organism.

### 4.1 Chemical structure matters

LCOs and COs are not interchangeable generic tokens. They differ in length, lipid attachment, and chemical substitutions. In rhizobial Nod factors, modifications such as sulfation, acetylation, fucosylation, and other substitutions can affect receptor binding and host compatibility.

For example, the review describes evidence that specific modifications of Nod factors produced by *Sinorhizobium meliloti* contribute to effective recognition by *Medicago* receptors; mutant bacteria producing differently modified Nod factors show strongly impaired nodulation in the tested systems.

For AM fungi, the specificity problem is more complicated. LCO-like compounds are not exclusive to beneficial AM fungi; diverse fungi, including pathogens, can produce related molecules. The review therefore discusses the possible importance of other cues, secretion dynamics, and ratios of short- and long-chain COs in distinguishing symbiotic contexts.

**Narrow result:** The receiving system does not necessarily identify a partner from one unique chemical name. It can depend on molecular structure, combinations, receptor composition, signal dynamics, and the physiological state of the host.

## 5. One chemical family, different outcomes: defense or symbiosis

Chitin-derived molecules are important because they can be associated with both microbial recognition and defense responses. Plants must interact with beneficial fungi while retaining the capacity to resist pathogens.

Related LysM receptor systems can participate in distinct outputs. Some receptor combinations promote immune responses; others contribute to symbiotic signaling. In rice, for example, different chain lengths of chitin-derived oligosaccharides can be perceived through different receptor complexes, with short-chain COs contributing to mycorrhizal signaling and longer chitin fragments associated with immune activation.

The distinction is not a universal simple rule that “short means friend, long means enemy.” Receptor identity, species, ligand modifications, signal context, and downstream signaling all matter.

This is a significant correction to a simplistic information model:

**A chemical's meaning is not contained in its name alone. Its biological effect depends on the receiver and the conditions of reception.**

## 6. From detection to internal response: calcium signaling

In well-characterized arbuscular mycorrhizal and rhizobial systems, receptor activation can initiate the Common Symbiosis Signaling Pathway (CSSP).

One important event is oscillation in calcium concentration in the nucleus and surrounding cytoplasm. This is often described as **nucleocytoplasmic calcium spiking**.

The calcium pattern is decoded by calcium- and calmodulin-dependent protein kinase (CCaMK; called DMI3 in *Medicago truncatula*). Downstream regulators, including CYCLOPS/IPD3 and transcription factors such as NSP1, NSP2, NIN, and RAM1 in relevant pathways, promote expression of genes involved in symbiosis and organ development.

This sequence is more than a molecule touching a receptor. It is a cascade in which a local detection event is converted into coordinated changes in gene expression, cellular organization, and development.

The exact components and their roles vary across host lineages and symbiosis types. The CSSP is not a universal pathway for every organismal partnership.

## 7. Admission and accommodation: the plant actively changes its own cell

Following successful recognition and downstream signaling, the plant root cell can reorganize its internal architecture to accommodate the symbiont.

In arbuscular mycorrhiza, a **pre-penetration apparatus** (PPA) forms. It involves rearrangement of the cytoskeleton, endoplasmic reticulum, vacuolar organization, and targeted membrane trafficking. Fungal hyphae then grow through the guided route into root tissue.

Within root cortical cells, the fungus forms a highly branched structure called an **arbuscule**. This is a major interface for nutrient exchange: the plant receives mineral nutrients, while the fungus receives plant-derived carbon and lipids.

In legume–rhizobium symbiosis, a different developmental route occurs. Bacterial infection threads progress through root hairs and toward developing nodule tissue. The plant forms a new organ, the nodule, where differentiated bacteria fix atmospheric nitrogen.

These pathways share some signaling elements but produce distinct structures and involve different microbial partners. They must not be collapsed into one mechanism.

## 8. Recognition is controlled, not unconditional

Successful receptor binding does not guarantee that a symbiont will be admitted.

The host plant's nutritional state, immune activity, receptor repertoire, genetic background, and downstream developmental competence all influence the outcome. The plant regulates the extent of colonization and can limit or reject interactions that fail to meet the relevant conditions.

This distinction is important because microbial signals can overlap with signals from non-beneficial organisms. Some pathogens produce molecules related to those used in symbiotic signaling. The plant must integrate multiple cues rather than simply open a universal entry route whenever a familiar molecule appears.

The 2026 review also emphasizes that ligand specificity can overlap among receptor family members and that receptor functions differ across plant species. Current research is still refining how combinations of signals and receptor complexes direct the outcome.

**Narrow result:** Recognition is one necessary part of a controlled process; it is not equivalent to unconditional acceptance.

## 9. Return to lichenization: what is known and what is not

The lichen investigation examined the interaction between a mycobiont (fungal partner) and photobiont (photosynthetic partner).

The 2009 Joneson and Lutzoni experiment with *Cladonia grayi* found that characteristic hyphal envelopment and branching occurred with compatible photobiont pairings, including the natural *Asterochloris* partner, but not with non-compatible phototrophs or glass beads. This showed that the response was not explained by object shape alone.

The 2023 Pichler et al. review describes a proposed five-phase lichenization model: pre-contact, contact, envelopment, incorporation, and differentiation. It discusses extracellular metabolites, candidate fungal lectins and algal cyclic peptides, gene-expression changes, and continued exchange of metabolites.

However, the molecular recognition machinery in lichenization is not characterized to the same level as LysM receptor signaling in plant root endosymbiosis. The lichen review explicitly describes the signaling mechanisms as poorly understood.

Therefore:

- The *Cladonia* compatibility experiment supports partner-dependent developmental response.
- Gene-expression changes support active molecular interaction.
- Candidate compounds support hypotheses about chemical signaling.
- A fully resolved receptor-to-intracellular-pathway mechanism for lichen compatibility has not yet been established by the evidence reviewed here.

**Do not transfer the plant LysM/CSSP mechanism to lichens without direct evidence.**

## 10. The orchid connection: keep the biological question specific

The bee orchid *Ophrys apifera* remains a relevant biological case, but two different interactions must be kept separate:

1. floral interactions with insect pollinators;
2. root and seed-stage associations with mycorrhizal fungi.

The chemical deception research in *Ophrys* concerns floral signals and insect sensory responses. The plant root receptor literature concerns plant–microbe interactions. These are different receiving systems, different signal environments, and different biological outcomes.

The broader orchid family contains species whose seed germination and early development depend on compatible mycorrhizal fungi. However, the exact fungal partners and nutritional relationships vary among orchid species and developmental stages. A specific claim about *O. apifera* requires species-specific evidence; the general orchid pattern is not enough.

This audit therefore does not claim that *O. apifera* uses the same LysM receptor mechanisms documented in model root symbioses. A dedicated orchid–mycorrhizal compatibility investigation is a separate next step.

## 12. Mechanistic synthesis

The best-supported root endosymbiosis cases identify several interacting processes, not one universal one-way chain. These include signal production and release, receptor or sensor interaction, intracellular signaling, gene expression, immune activity, membrane and cell-wall remodeling, accommodation, resource exchange, and persistence.

The relationships among these processes are dynamic:

- Plant nutritional and developmental states can alter root exudates, receptor activity, and the conditions under which microbial signals are received.
- Microbial signals can alter plant signaling, immune activity, and cellular organization.
- Plant responses can change microbial access, growth, and subsequent signal exposure.
- Microbial effectors and secreted compounds can modify host defense and the physical conditions of colonization.
- Accommodation and resource exchange can change the physiological state of both partners, influencing later interactions.
- Environmental conditions can affect several of these processes at once.

Some events are causally dependent in particular experiments, but that does not establish a fixed order for all symbioses. The same process can participate in feedback, parallel responses, repeated checkpoints, or different outcomes in different organisms. The terms *recognition*, *defense*, *accommodation*, and *integration* remain analytically distinct; their relationships must be established for each biological system.

This is a research map of interacting processes, not a new GUA-W runtime sequence or universal biological law.

## 17. Interconnected regulation: beneficial-partner recognition, immunity, and accommodation

### 17.1 The question

How do plant roots support beneficial microbial associations while retaining defenses against pathogens, especially when beneficial and harmful organisms can present chemically related molecular features?

The evidence does not support a simple “friend-or-enemy” switch. Nor does it establish one fixed sequence in which recognition must finish before defense, accommodation, or exchange begins. Plant–microbe relationships involve partially shared pathways, distinct receptor systems, microbial modulation of host responses, host regulation, and environmental context. These processes can interact repeatedly.

The strongest mechanistic evidence comes from model plant–microbe systems, particularly legume roots interacting with rhizobia and arbuscular mycorrhizal (AM) fungi. Findings from these models must not automatically be assigned to lichens, orchids, insects, or every plant lineage.

### 17.2 Distinct receptor systems can discriminate related chemical signals

Bozsoki and colleagues (2017) investigated how the legumes *Lotus japonicus* and *Medicago truncatula* distinguish chitin oligomers associated with fungal recognition from lipochitooligosaccharide (LCO) Nod factors associated with rhizobial symbiosis.

They identified chitin-perception receptors LjLYS6, MtLYK9, and MtLYR4, and distinguished their functions from the Nod-factor receptor systems LjNFR1/MtLYK3 and LjNFR5/MtNFP. Inactivation of the chitin-receptor genes impaired early reactive-oxygen-species responses and defense-gene induction. The mutants were more susceptible to tested fungal and bacterial pathogens, while rhizobial and AM-fungal colonization remained substantially maintained in the tested conditions.

Biochemical work also showed that the LjLYS6 ectodomain binds chitin oligomers, with at least six N-acetylglucosamine units required for optimal binding efficiency.

**Narrow result:** In these tested legumes, distinct receptor systems help separate chitin-triggered defense from Nod-factor-triggered symbiotic signaling. This is strong evidence for receptor-level discrimination in those systems. It is not evidence that every plant uses the same receptor arrangement or that all beneficial and harmful microbes carry uniquely separable signals.

### 17.3 Shared chemical families and overlapping signaling complicate the distinction

The 2025 review by Zhang, Tan, and Wang describes plant symbiosis and immunity as a continuum of partly shared and partly distinct regulatory networks. Both can involve signal perception, cellular remodeling, metabolic rearrangement, and changes in gene expression. The outcomes differ, but the underlying processes are not wholly separate.

The 2026 review by Fougner-Økland, Spezzati, and Parys likewise emphasizes that LysM receptor families perceive several related classes of GlcNAc-derived molecules, including rhizobial Nod factors, fungal LCOs, chitin oligomers, and exopolysaccharide-associated signals. Specificity can depend on chemical modification, chain length, relative abundance, receptor composition, binding dynamics, and the formation of receptor complexes.

Therefore, a chemical family name alone does not determine whether the plant response will be defensive or symbiotic. The receiving organism's receptor repertoire and physiological context matter.

**Qualification:** “Short chitin fragments mean beneficial; long fragments mean harmful” is not a universal classification rule. Signal length is one possible discriminating feature among several, and its significance varies with the plant and microbial system.

### 17.4 Beneficial partners can actively modify host defense

Recognition is not exclusively a host-side event. Microbes can release compounds that alter the host's response.

Zeng and colleagues (2020) studied the AM fungus *Rhizophagus irregularis* and identified RiSLM, a secreted LysM-domain effector. Their experiments found that RiSLM binds chitin oligomers and LCOs, protects fungal hyphae from plant chitinases, and interferes with chitin-triggered plant immune responses. Host-induced silencing of the fungal gene substantially reduced fungal infection and arbuscule abundance in the tested host system.

This provides evidence that a beneficial symbiont can use an immune-modulating mechanism resembling strategies also found among pathogenic fungi. The presence of immune suppression is therefore not, by itself, proof of pathogenicity. Its biological significance depends on the organism, the interaction, the outcome for the host, and the wider evidence.

A related 2013 study by Liang and colleagues found that rhizobial Nod factors could suppress MAMP-triggered immunity in *Arabidopsis thaliana* and other non-legumes, including through reduced levels of pattern-recognition receptors at the plasma membrane. This shows that a microbial signal can alter immune responsiveness even in a plant that does not thereby establish the full legume–rhizobium symbiosis.

**Narrow result:** Beneficial microbes can contribute to the regulation or suppression of host immune responses. Such modulation is one part of a broader interaction and does not alone establish successful symbiosis, compatibility, or benefit.

### 17.5 Recognition is coupled to cell organization and membrane trafficking

A 2026 *Nature Communications* study by Song and colleagues provides a further example of connected processes. In soybean, Nod-factor perception activates the receptor kinase GmSymRKβ, which phosphorylates the Qa-SNARE protein GmSYP111a. The phosphorylation promotes clathrin-mediated endocytosis of the receptor–SNARE complex and links signal perception to membrane trafficking required for infection-thread development. Genetic analyses in soybean and *Lotus japonicus* support a conserved requirement for GmSYP111a in nodule initiation.

This finding connects molecular recognition with the cell's membrane-trafficking machinery. Signal reception is not merely followed by a separate developmental event; receptor activity and the machinery that reorganizes cellular membranes are mechanistically coupled.

The study is specific to the tested legume systems and does not establish that all symbiotic accommodation uses this same SNARE mechanism.

### 17.6 The relationship is reciprocal and context-dependent

The plant and microbial partners influence one another. Plant nutrient status can alter root exudates and the conditions that favor microbial recruitment. Microbial signals can alter plant receptor activity and immune state. Host responses can change microbial access and colonization. Microbial effectors can modify host defenses. Successful accommodation and resource exchange can then change the physiological state of both partners, influencing subsequent signaling and growth.

Environmental conditions, host genotype, microbial strain, developmental stage, nutrient availability, and the surrounding microbial community can affect several of these relationships simultaneously.

The evidence therefore supports a dynamic network of interacting processes, including feedback and repeated regulation—not a single irreversible progression from signal to recognition to acceptance.

### 17.8 What this evidence does and does not establish

**Established within the stated systems:**

1. Plants can use distinct receptor systems to distinguish some related microbial signals.
2. Symbiotic and immune signaling share components and regulatory features in some plant lineages.
3. Beneficial microbes can modify or suppress aspects of host immune activity.
4. Receptor signaling can be directly coupled to membrane trafficking and cellular reorganization.
5. Host state and environmental conditions influence microbial interaction and persistence.

**Not established:**

1. One universal molecular test by which all plants identify every beneficial partner and pathogen.
2. A universal friend-versus-enemy classification based on one molecule or one receptor.
3. That immune suppression always indicates a beneficial association.
4. That receptor binding alone establishes admission, successful colonization, or mutual benefit.
5. That one pathway or temporal sequence explains all symbioses.
6. That mechanisms demonstrated in legumes apply to lichens or *Ophrys apifera* without species-specific evidence.

### 17.9 Interconnected model for continued investigation

The evidence is best represented as interacting domains rather than a one-way chain:

- **Host condition:** nutrient status, development, genotype, immune state, and environmental exposure.
- **Microbial condition:** identity, developmental state, secreted molecules, cell-wall composition, effectors, and resource requirements.
- **Recognition machinery:** receptor identity, ligand structure, receptor combinations, and signal dynamics.
- **Host response networks:** calcium signaling, reactive oxygen species, hormone pathways, transcription, and membrane trafficking.
- **Physical and metabolic relationship:** entry, accommodation, nutrient exchange, defense, restriction, and persistence.
- **Feedback:** changes in the host and microbial partners alter later signal production, response thresholds, colonization, and resource exchange.

These domains can influence one another in parallel, reciprocally, and repeatedly. The map does not prescribe a fixed order. Each proposed causal connection must be tested in the relevant organism and experimental context.

### 17.12 Unresolved questions and next discriminating work

1. How do different plant lineages combine receptor identity, ligand structure, and downstream signaling to distinguish related microbial molecules?
2. Which receptor combinations are necessary and sufficient for defense or symbiotic responses in each model system?
3. How do nutrient status, developmental stage, and environmental stress alter receptor expression, immune thresholds, and partner compatibility?
4. How do beneficial microbial effectors avoid disabling defenses needed to contain pathogens?
5. Under what conditions does immune suppression support a stable mutualism, and under what conditions does it increase host vulnerability?
6. How do host responses and microbial behavior feed back on one another during established colonization and resource exchange?
7. Which mechanisms are shared across AM fungi, rhizobia, lichen-forming fungi, and orchid mycorrhizal partners, and which are lineage-specific?
8. What measurements distinguish initial recognition, successful accommodation, actual resource exchange, and persistent mutual benefit?

These questions remain open. The next investigation should examine one named system and causal mechanism at a time, then compare the results without assuming a shared mechanism.
