## 3. The Bee Orchid as the shared biological witness for LIGHT and WATER

### Identity and habitat

The Bee Orchid is *Ophrys apifera*. Kew's Plants of the World Online describes habitats ranging from dry to wet calcareous soils, in full sunlight to light shade, including grassland, woodland edges, open woods, reed swamps, and stabilized coastal dunes. This makes it a useful organism for investigating light and water together, while reminding us that local populations experience different conditions.

### Light questions

1. What light conditions occur in the habitats where *O. apifera* grows?
2. How do light intensity, duration, and seasonal timing relate to vegetative growth and flowering?
3. What is known specifically about photosynthesis and photoreception in this species, as distinct from general orchid or plant findings?
4. Which responses are directly measured in *O. apifera*, and which are inferred from broader plant physiology?

### Water questions

1. How do soil moisture, drainage, and seasonal precipitation relate to the locations where *O. apifera* occurs?
2. How do calcareous soil chemistry, water availability, and nutrient availability interact?
3. What is known about water stress during vegetative growth, flowering, capsule formation, and seed development?
4. How do local climate and seasonal timing affect flowering and reproductive success?
5. What evidence distinguishes water effects from correlated influences such as temperature, light, soil chemistry, and competition?

### The interaction of light and water

Light and water cannot be investigated as isolated inputs in a living plant. Light supplies energy for photosynthesis and also provides environmental cues detected by plant photoreceptors. Water is required for cellular function, transport, and photosynthetic processes, while its availability is influenced by soil, weather, and plant water loss. The relationship must be studied with controls for temperature, soil chemistry, season, and other environmental factors.

The orchid is a specific biological witness, not a proxy for all plants or for the entire water cycle.

### Pollination correction

Although the flower resembles a bee and many *Ophrys* species use sexual deception, *O. apifera* is predominantly self-pollinating across much of its range. Its pollinia can swing toward its own stigma; air movement can assist this process. Some regional variation and occasional cross-pollination have been reported. Do not attribute the pollination behavior of other *Ophrys* species automatically to this species.

The witness is allowed to correct our expectations.

## 4. Chemical exchange at the Bee Orchid–bee boundary

The Bee Orchid investigation must include chemical movement and signaling at the flower–insect boundary. Water is itself a chemical substance (H₂O), but it must not be conflated with every chemical signal. We therefore distinguish water transfer, volatile chemical signaling, contact-mediated transfer, and pollen transfer.

### A. Orchid → bee: volatile chemical signals

Flowers release volatile organic compounds (VOCs) into the surrounding air. These molecules move from the flower through the atmosphere and can be detected by an insect's olfactory system. In the genus *Ophrys*, floral scent blends can mimic chemical components of female insect pheromones. In studied sexually deceptive species, the identity, ratios, and volatility of compounds influence attraction and close-range behavior.

Species-specific evidence exists that *O. apifera* produces floral chemicals associated with bee-pheromone mimicry, despite its predominantly autonomous self-pollination. A study of VOC emissions in *O. apifera* reported a complex floral scent profile, including compounds such as 1-butanol, butyl ether, and caryophyllene among the more abundant compounds in that study. The presence of a compound alone does not establish its behavioral function; that requires behavioral tests with the relevant bee species and natural conditions.

### B. Bee → orchid: possible contact-mediated transfer

When an insect contacts a flower, it may physically carry pollen, microorganisms, cuticular hydrocarbons, or other surface-associated substances. These possibilities should be investigated separately. For *O. apifera*, do not claim that a bee deposits a specific chemical message onto the flower unless direct evidence demonstrates it. The well-established pollen-transfer mechanism in orchid pollination is movement of pollinia between flowers by an insect; *O. apifera* is predominantly self-pollinating, so the importance of insect-mediated transfer is population- and context-dependent.

### C. Water transfer is a separate empirical question

Do not assume that the Bee Orchid exchanges liquid water with visiting bees. *Ophrys* flowers are generally non-rewarding and do not provide nectar as a food reward. Any claim that a bee obtains water from the flower, or deposits water onto it, needs species-specific evidence. Humidity, condensation, rain, dew, and water on insect or floral surfaces may be relevant environmental factors, but they are not automatically a biological communication channel.

### D. Communication model

**Flower chemical production → volatile release → transport through air → bee sensory reception → neural/behavioral response.**

A separate possible contact route is:

**Bee contact → physical deposition or removal of material → flower-surface interaction.**

The first is a demonstrated general mechanism in *Ophrys* chemical attraction; its precise behavioral importance must be tested for *O. apifera*. The second is a research question unless species-specific evidence is found. Pollen transfer is a reproductive-material pathway and should not automatically be called chemical communication.

This distinction is central to the larger investigation: a chemical can be a carrier, a signal, a resource, or a transferred material. Its role depends on the molecule, route, receiver, and demonstrated effect.


## 5. The reproductive puzzle: chemical mimicry alongside autonomous self-pollination

This is not a contradiction to be explained away; it is a biological pattern that deserves investigation.

*Ophrys apifera* retains a flower with features associated with insect attraction and chemical mimicry, yet it is predominantly capable of autonomous self-pollination. Its flexible pollinarium structures can allow pollinia to contact the stigma, with air movement playing a role. The species is not uniformly selfing in every population: mixed pollination and regional cross-pollination have been reported.

This raises several distinct hypotheses, none of which should be treated as established merely because it sounds plausible:

1. **Reproductive assurance:** autonomous self-pollination may provide reproduction when suitable pollinators are scarce or unreliable.
2. **Retained or pre-existing floral traits:** some chemical or visual traits may persist from an ancestral or earlier pollination system, even if current reproduction is often autonomous.
3. **Mixed reproductive strategy:** occasional insect-mediated pollination may still occur in some populations or conditions.
4. **Evolutionary reversal or transition:** self-pollination may have become more important in parts of the species' range, potentially under pollinator limitation.
5. **Multiple functions or no demonstrated current function:** a compound may have a role in attraction, another ecological interaction, or no currently established function. Chemical detection alone cannot decide among these possibilities.

The 2024 species account in *Curtis's Botanical Magazine* explicitly notes the puzzle of a flower specialized for insect pollination combined with predominantly autonomous self-pollination, and discusses a possible reversal to self-pollination in association with low pollinator numbers. A 2025 study discusses the geographic variation and possible reproductive assurance associated with self-pollination. These are interpretations and hypotheses to test, not a final causal verdict.

### Research tests

- Compare floral volatile profiles across populations with differing pollinator abundance and selfing rates.
- Determine whether the relevant bee species detect the measured *O. apifera* volatile blend using electrophysiological and behavioral assays.
- Measure autonomous selfing, insect-mediated pollen transfer, fruit set, seed set, and offspring genetic diversity separately.
- Test whether environmental conditions—including light, water availability, temperature, and season—alter volatile production or reproductive outcomes.
- Distinguish compounds that are merely present from compounds shown to affect a receiver.

**Key distinction:** reproductive independence from a pollinator does not prove the absence of chemical communication. It means that communication, pollination, and reproductive success must be measured as separate outcomes.

## Species-specific evidence audit — what is known about *Ophrys apifera* scent, reception, and habitat

**Question:** Which parts of the Bee Orchid chemical-reception account have been directly measured in *Ophrys apifera*, which are supported by broader *Ophrys* research, and which still require a species-specific receiver or behavior experiment?

### 1. Direct species-specific chemical measurement

Gallego and colleagues (2012) sampled volatile organic compounds (VOCs) from orchid inflorescences, including *O. apifera*, using dynamic headspace collection with sorbent cartridges and thermal-desorption gas chromatography/mass spectrometry (TD-GC/MS). The authors reported a complex volatile profile. In their *O. apifera* sample, 1-butanol, butyl ether, and caryophyllene were among the three compounds reported as most abundant.

This is direct evidence that the sampled *O. apifera* inflorescence emitted a measurable mixture of volatile compounds under the study's sampling conditions. It is chemical-composition evidence, not a behavioral experiment. The study did not demonstrate that a particular bee species detected those three compounds, that those compounds caused attraction or pseudocopulation, or that they were necessary for pollination.

The study itself emphasizes that VOC profiles can vary with conditions such as day/night, temperature, hydric stress, pollination readiness, post-pollination state, and flower age. Those are relevant variables for future work, but this particular study should not be cited as having experimentally measured all of those effects in *O. apifera*.

### 2. Species-specific evidence versus genus-level evidence

The 2024 *Curtis's Botanical Magazine* species account by Fay and colleagues reports that *O. apifera* produces chemicals described as mimicking female bee pheromones. The same account stresses that the species differs from the familiar *Ophrys* pattern: across much of its range it is predominantly self-pollinating, with the pollinia able to swing into contact with the stigma as a result of air movement.

This supports a cautious species-level statement: *O. apifera* has a floral chemical profile described in the botanical literature as pheromone-mimicking, while autonomous self-pollination is common across much of its range. It does not establish that a particular volatile measured in the 2012 study is the active pheromone mimic, nor that a particular bee species is routinely attracted and causes successful pollination in all populations.

Research on other *Ophrys* species provides strong evidence that floral odor blends can be detected by specific male bee pollinators and can trigger distinct behaviors. For example, Mant, Peakall, and Schiestl (2005) compared floral odor differentiation and pollinator responses in other *Ophrys* pollination systems, including behavioral and electrophysiological evidence involving *Colletes cunicularius*. This is important genus-level context, but it must not be transferred as a demonstrated *O. apifera*–bee behavioral mechanism without direct species-specific tests.

### 3. Habitat gives the light-and-water investigation a real ecological boundary

Kew's Plants of the World Online species information and the 2024 Kew-authored species account describe *O. apifera* across habitats that include dry to wet alkaline or calcareous soils, in full sunlight to light shade. Recorded habitats include grassland, open woodland margins, garrigue, dune slacks, and disturbed calcareous ground such as quarries and railway verges.

This habitat range matters because the orchid is not tied to one fixed light or soil-water condition. The shared light-and-water investigation should therefore avoid a single simplified description such as “the Bee Orchid requires full sun” or “the Bee Orchid requires dry soil.” The documented range is broader.

The habitat records establish occurrence across environmental conditions; they do not by themselves establish the physiological optimum, tolerance limits, or causal effects of light and water on scent production, flowering, or reproductive success.

### 4. Separate the four empirical links

The investigation should keep these questions distinct:

1. **Emission:** Which VOCs does a flower release, in what quantities, and under which measured environmental and developmental conditions?
2. **Detection:** Does a named insect species detect the emitted mixture? This requires receiver-specific evidence, such as antennal electrophysiology or another validated sensory assay.
3. **Behavior:** Does detection cause approach, landing, pseudocopulation, or another measurable behavior under controlled and natural conditions?
4. **Reproductive consequence:** Does that behavior transfer pollinia and increase outcrossing, fruit set, seed set, or offspring genetic diversity in the population?

A result at one level does not automatically establish the next. A compound's presence is not proof of insect detection; detection is not proof of attraction; attraction is not proof of pollination; and pollination is not by itself proof of increased reproductive success.

### 5. Light and water as possible conditioning variables

The 2012 VOC paper identifies day/night, temperature, hydric stress, readiness for pollination, post-pollination state, and flower age as factors that can affect floral volatile profiles in plants. Kew's species account documents *O. apifera* across a range of light exposures and soil moisture conditions.

These sources justify asking whether light and water conditions influence *O. apifera* VOC emission. They do not yet answer that question. A discriminating study would measure, on the same identified plants and flowers:

- incident light quantity and spectral composition;
- air temperature and relative humidity;
- soil moisture and substrate properties;
- flower age and developmental/pollination state;
- VOC identity and emission rate over a defined collection period;
- the response of a specified insect receiver, if a relevant receiver is identified.

The study design would need replication across plants, dates, and sites, with suitable air blanks and controls for collection conditions. If insect response is tested, chemical measurement and behavioral trials should be linked to the same characterized floral samples rather than inferred from a species-level scent list.

### 6. Bounded cross-disciplinary placement

- **Chemistry / analytical chemistry:** identifies and quantifies emitted VOCs; method and sampling conditions define what is registered.
- **Plant physiology:** examines how developmental state, light, water, and stress relate to volatile production.
- **Atmospheric physics / transport:** examines dilution, wind, temperature, humidity, and movement of volatile molecules through air.
- **Sensory physiology:** tests whether a specified insect receiver detects the compounds.
- **Behavioral ecology:** tests whether detection changes insect behavior under defined conditions.
- **Reproductive biology / population genetics:** tests whether behavior affects pollination, selfing/outcrossing, and reproductive outcomes.
- **Measurement science:** evaluates blanks, calibration, detection limits, repeatability, and uncertainty.

These disciplines examine connected parts of one biological interaction. No one result substitutes for the measurements required by another discipline.

## Orchid → atmosphere → insect: the physical journey of a floral volatile

**Question:** After a flower emits volatile organic compounds (VOCs), what happens to them before an insect can detect them? Does the mixture measured at the flower remain unchanged as it travels through air?

### 1. Emission is the beginning, not the complete interaction

A headspace sample collected at a flower characterizes compounds entering the sampled air under the collection conditions. It does not automatically describe the exact mixture, concentration, or timing that an insect encounters at another location.

After emission, volatile molecules are transported and mixed by the surrounding air. Their distribution depends on source strength, wind, turbulence, temperature, vegetation, local topography, and the chemical background. Some compounds may also be transformed or removed during transport. The resulting sensory environment is dynamic in both space and time.

### 2. Three physical processes must be distinguished

- **Advection:** bulk movement of air carries the volatile compounds with the wind. At landscape scales, this is generally the principal means by which airborne plant odors are transported over distance.
- **Molecular diffusion:** random molecular motion produces net spreading from higher to lower concentration. It matters at small scales and around local concentration gradients, but is much slower than air movement for transport over ordinary outdoor distances.
- **Turbulent mixing / turbulent dispersion:** irregular air motion stretches, folds, and mixes odor-bearing air with surrounding air. It creates a patchy plume rather than a smooth, stable cone.

Convection and thermal structure can also move air vertically. Vegetation, ground roughness, obstacles, and topography change the local airflow and therefore the plume.

These processes are related but not synonymous. “Diffusion” should not be used as a catch-all label for all odor transport.

### 3. An odor plume is intermittent

A useful model is not a uniform scent gradient. Turbulent air creates filaments or pockets of odor-bearing air separated by intervals with little or no target odor. An insect moving through the plume can encounter a sequence of odor “whiffs” and “blanks.”

The 2019 review *Insect Odorscapes* describes how wind transports odorants downwind, while turbulent vortices mix odor-bearing and cleaner air. The local concentration can fluctuate substantially around its time-averaged value. Insects can use the timing of odor encounters together with wind direction and their own movement to locate a source; they are not necessarily following a smooth concentration gradient.

This has a direct consequence for the investigation: a single average concentration measurement may miss the temporal structure that an insect's antenna actually experiences.

### 4. The mixture itself can change during transport

Cai and colleagues (2022) tested plant volatile blends in a wind tunnel. They collected volatiles at multiple downwind positions and found that:

- the total amounts collected generally decreased with distance from the source;
- the chemical profiles differed among collection points;
- profiles nearest the source were most similar to the emitted blend;
- wind speed changed the spatial distribution of collected compounds; and
- compound ratios could change even over short transport distances.

This is direct evidence that a plant volatile blend need not remain compositionally identical as it moves through air. The experiment was not performed on *Ophrys apifera*, so its result establishes a transport principle for the tested plant volatile system, not the exact transformation of the Bee Orchid's scent.

A receiver may therefore encounter a changed ratio of compounds, not merely a weaker copy of the original blend. Because insects can respond to combinations and ratios of compounds, this is biologically important.

### 5. The insect's sensory world is species-dependent

The 2019 odorscape review explains that insect species possess different sets of olfactory receptors. Individual receptor types detect only a portion of the volatile chemicals in the environment. The sensory environment available to one insect species is therefore not identical to that available to another.

The review distinguishes:
- the chemical environment present in the habitat;
- the subset of compounds detected by a particular insect's receptors;
- neural processing of receptor activity; and
- the physiological or behavioral response that follows.

An airborne compound can be present without being detected by a given insect. Detection alone does not prove attraction, and attraction alone does not establish successful pollination.

### 6. Keep the *Ophrys apifera* evidence boundary intact

The existing species-specific audit establishes that a sampled *O. apifera* inflorescence emitted measurable VOCs. It does not establish a complete, species-specific pathway from those measured compounds through atmospheric transport to detection by a named insect and a reproductive outcome.

The broader *Ophrys* literature demonstrates that specific orchid–pollinator systems can involve blends of compounds, receiver-specific responses, and behavioral assays. Those findings are valuable comparative evidence, but the pollinator and active blend in one *Ophrys* species cannot simply be assigned to *O. apifera*.

For *O. apifera*, the next direct test would need to connect the following measurements:

1. Identify the plant, flower age, and developmental/pollination state.
2. Measure the emitted VOC blend and emission rate at the flower.
3. Measure wind speed and direction, turbulence, temperature, humidity, and relevant background VOCs.
4. Collect or measure the plume at defined distances and directions from the flower, with adequate temporal resolution.
5. Determine whether a specified insect receiver detects the emitted and transported compounds, using an appropriate validated sensory assay.
6. Test behavior separately, then determine whether the behavior leads to pollinia transfer and measurable reproductive outcomes.

Air blanks, sampling controls, calibration, replication, and detection limits are necessary. If the study uses a wind tunnel, its airflow and boundary conditions must be reported; a controlled tunnel result should not automatically be treated as a complete representation of a natural habitat.

## From insect antenna to behavior: how a chemical mixture becomes distinguishable

**Question:** Once an insect encounters a transported floral odor mixture, how does its sensory system detect and distinguish the compounds, process their combined activity, and produce a measurable response?

### 1. The antenna is a sensory surface, not a single detector

In many insects, airborne odorants enter porous hair-like structures called **sensilla**, commonly located on the antennae and sometimes on other body parts. A sensillum can house one or more olfactory sensory neurons (OSNs). The neurons carry receptor proteins that respond to particular chemical features or ligands.

Insect chemosensation is not built from one universal receptor family. Depending on the insect and sensory function, volatile chemicals can be detected through olfactory receptors (ORs), ionotropic receptors (IRs), and, in some contexts, gustatory receptors (GRs). The receptor families, their distribution, and their tuning differ across insect lineages. Do not assume that the receptor inventory of a fruit fly, moth, mosquito, bee, or a potential *Ophrys* pollinator is interchangeable.

The receptor-neuron response depends on the chemical presented, its concentration, and the receptor's sensitivity. A compound's presence in air therefore does not guarantee a detectable response in every insect.

### 2. Two complementary coding strategies: combinatorial codes and labeled lines

Research describes two broad, compatible strategies for representing odors:

- **Combinatorial coding:** multiple odorants activate overlapping subsets of sensory neurons and receptors. A compound may activate more than one receptor type, and a receptor may respond to more than one compound. The combined pattern across neurons provides a way to distinguish many possible odors.
- **Labeled lines:** some sensory pathways are relatively specialized for a particular ecologically important cue or class of cues. Their activation can be strongly associated with a particular behavioral function, such as detecting a pheromone, a host cue, or a danger-associated compound.

These are not mutually exclusive architectures. The same insect may use combinatorial coding for general environmental odors and more specialized pathways for particular cues. The balance differs by species and ecological task.

Haverkamp, Hansson, and Knaden (2018) review examples from flies, moths, and mosquitoes. They emphasize that the insect's olfactory representation is shaped both by the chemical signal and by the receiver's peripheral and central coding. Their review also describes cases in which odor mixtures that are attractive when presented separately become ineffective or repellent when combined. The identity and context of the blend matter; simply adding more odor components does not guarantee a stronger signal.

### 3. The first brain processing center: the antennal lobe

Olfactory sensory neurons project to the insect brain's antennal lobe. In many studied insects, neurons expressing the same receptor type converge on a shared processing unit called a **glomerulus**. The antennal lobe also contains local interneurons and projection neurons.

- Sensory neurons provide the incoming receptor activity.
- Local interneurons connect activity within and between glomeruli, shaping the response.
- Projection neurons carry processed activity to higher brain centers.

This means the insect's central representation is not merely a passive copy of the molecules present at the antenna. It is transformed by neural circuitry.

A 2011 experimental study in a moth recorded individual antennal-lobe neurons responding to mixtures containing two to seven components. It found nonlinear mixture responses—including suppression, hypoadditivity, and synergism—in many mixture-responsive neurons. Different neurons represented the same mixture through different spatial, frequency, latency, and temporal response properties. There is no single universal arithmetic rule by which the antennal lobe simply adds each component's response.

This is a key evidence boundary: the experiment demonstrates complex mixture processing in the studied moth and tested mixtures. It does not establish the exact neural code of an insect visiting *Ophrys apifera*.

### 4. Background odor and internal context matter

An insect encounters a chemical environment, not an isolated laboratory molecule. Background odors can overlap with target compounds, mask or alter the effective mixture, or change how a blend is represented. The 2018 review describes hawkmoth experiments in which the same floral odor can be processed differently depending on the surrounding odor background. It also describes plant blends that attract when spatially separated but lose their attractive effect when combined into a novel mixture.

Behavior is also influenced by the insect's species, life stage, sex, physiological state, prior experience, and ecological task. Hunger, mating state, and reproductive state can change the relevance of a chemical cue. A sensory response should therefore not be treated as a fixed, context-free command.

### 5. Detection, neural representation, and behavior are separate measurements

The evidence must be kept in distinct levels:

1. **Chemical presence:** a compound is present in the air at the antenna.
2. **Peripheral detection:** an antennal sensory neuron or receptor responds to the compound.
3. **Neural representation:** activity across neurons and antennal-lobe circuits represents the compound or mixture.
4. **Behavioral response:** the insect changes orientation, approach, landing, feeding, mating, or another measurable action.
5. **Ecological consequence:** the action changes pollination, reproduction, host finding, or another outcome.

A result at one level does not prove the next. Antennal detection does not prove attraction. A neural response does not by itself establish the behavioral meaning of that response. An approach to a flower does not prove pollinia transfer or successful reproduction.

### 6. What would be required to establish the Bee Orchid receiver pathway?

The current species-specific evidence for *O. apifera* includes measured floral VOCs and botanical descriptions of its floral chemistry and predominantly autonomous self-pollination across much of its range. The sources examined do not establish a named insect receiver's complete sensory pathway for the measured *O. apifera* blend.

A direct investigation would need to:

- Identify candidate insect visitors and establish whether they contact or visit *O. apifera* flowers in the population under study.
- Measure the actual floral blend and its concentrations at the insect's position, including the changing plume and background odor.
- Test antennal responses to individual compounds and reconstructed blends at ecologically realistic concentrations, using an appropriate method such as electroantennography or single-sensillum recording.
- If a response is found, test whether neural activity distinguishes the complete blend from individual compounds, altered ratios, and background mixtures.
- Conduct controlled behavioral tests that separate attraction, orientation, landing, and pseudocopulatory behavior.
- Observe whether the behavior transfers pollinia and measure the reproductive outcome.
- Repeat across relevant populations, seasons, and insect species rather than generalizing from one trial.

The tests must use the actual candidate receiver. A positive response in a moth, fly, or honeybee model cannot be assigned to an *O. apifera* pollinator without direct evidence.

## Cross-organism observation: when light input is related to another organism's action

**Jimmy's side note / investigative lead:** We may be witnessing—or approaching—a case in which light received by one organism is connected, through an organized biological process, to an action expressed by a different organism. Preserve this as an important cross-organism question. Do not yet write it as an established *Ophrys apifera* causal pathway.

The phrase **“light becoming an action”** is useful as a compact observation, provided the physical and biological distinctions remain explicit. Light does not literally turn into the second organism or directly become its action. Light is electromagnetic energy. A first organism may absorb it and use photoreceptor-mediated signaling and metabolism to alter its own state or outputs. A second organism may then receive an output—such as a volatile chemical mixture—or may independently receive light through its own visual system. Its own organization processes that input and may produce behavior.

### Three distinct pathways must not be collapsed

**Pathway A — Light received by the plant**

Light → plant photoreceptor / photosynthetic processes → intracellular signaling and physiological state → possible change in development, metabolism, or volatile emission.

This is a biologically plausible class of pathway, but the exact effect of light on the VOC identity, blend ratio, or emission rate of *O. apifera* has not been demonstrated by the species-specific sources assembled in this audit. It remains a direct research question.

**Pathway B — Plant chemical output received by an insect**

Plant VOC emission → atmospheric transport and plume transformation → insect antennal reception → neural processing → possible behavior.

General mechanisms for this pathway are supported in studied plant–insect systems. For *O. apifera*, the complete chain from its measured VOCs to a named insect's sensory response, behavior, and reproductive consequence remains unestablished in the sources examined.

**Pathway C — Light received directly by the insect**

Light → insect photoreceptors / visual processing → possible orientation or behavior.

This is separate from olfactory reception. An insect may use visual information, chemical information, and other sensory inputs together, but the relative contribution and interaction must be tested for the particular species and behavior.

### The discriminating experiment

To test whether light received by *O. apifera* is causally related to an insect's later behavior through plant chemical output, a study would need to separate plant illumination from insect illumination and control the chemical pathway:

1. Expose matched plants or flowers to defined light conditions while controlling temperature, humidity, water status, flower age, and pollination state.
2. Measure the plant's VOC identity and emission rates under each condition, using blanks, calibration, replication, and time-resolved sampling.
3. Present the characterized headspace or reconstructed blend to a candidate insect under controlled conditions, with the insect's visual environment held constant.
4. Test antennal detection separately from behavioral response.
5. Include controls that distinguish a direct visual effect of light on the insect from a plant-mediated chemical effect.
6. If behavior changes, test whether it results in pollinia transfer and a measurable reproductive consequence.

A factorial design could vary plant light exposure and insect visual exposure independently. This would distinguish a plant-mediated light → chemistry → insect response from a direct light → insect visual response, and from an interaction between both pathways.

The observation brings existing relationships into one cross-organism view:

- **Reception:** each organism receives inputs through its own organized systems.
- **Activation and Differentiation:** input-specific processes change state and distinguish conditions.
- **Propagation:** the plant's chemical output can travel through the environment.
- **Integration:** each organism processes inputs within its own organization.
- **Expression:** each organism may produce its own measurable output or action.
- **Witness-state:** investigators register each link separately and do not infer an unmeasured link from the endpoints alone.

This does not add a stage, Gear, State term, or architecture component. It is an investigative recognition across organisms, not a claim that the two organisms share one sensory system or that the mechanism is already demonstrated for this orchid.
