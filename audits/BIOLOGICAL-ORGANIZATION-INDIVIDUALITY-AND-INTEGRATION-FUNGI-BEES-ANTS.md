# Biological Organization, Individuality, and Integration: Fungal Networks, Eusocial Insects, and Ant–Fungus Agriculture

**Status:** Evidence-led cross-disciplinary investigation  
**Repository placement:** Biological identity, development, and continuity; comparative biological synthesis  
**Governing order:** Author → Word → Creation → Witness  
**Central question:** Can integration increase while distinction remains?

## Purpose and scope

This investigation follows a comparison between fungal mycelial networks and the social organization of bees and ants. It separates three questions that can be confused when a living system is described as “connected,” “related,” or “integrated”:

1. **Physical connection:** Are the parts physically continuous, joined, or merely interacting across a boundary?
2. **Genetic relationship or identity:** Do the parts share a genotype, descend from a common reproductive source, contain genetically different nuclei, or belong to separate species?
3. **Functional integration:** Do the parts contribute to coordinated activity, resource movement, regulation, persistence, or reproduction at a larger scale?

A fourth case—the cultivated fungus in fungus-growing ant colonies—is included because it provides a direct example of distinct organisms forming a persistent, interdependent association.

This is a biological investigation, not a claim that fungi, bees, and ants use one mechanism. The word *integration* is used descriptively and must be specified in each case. The word *individual* is also used carefully: genetic individuality, physiological individuality, organismal individuality, and evolutionary individuality are not interchangeable.

No new GUA-W component, stage, Gear, or universal biological law is proposed.

## 1. Working definitions

- **Hypha:** A filamentous fungal growth structure. Hyphae may be septate, with cross-walls and pores, or coenocytic, with extended compartments containing multiple nuclei.
- **Mycelium:** The vegetative body or network formed by fungal hyphae. A mycelium may contain many branches and interconnected pathways.
- **Hyphal anastomosis:** Fusion between compatible hyphae, potentially establishing cytoplasmic continuity and allowing movement of materials between connected regions.
- **Genet:** A genetic individual or lineage derived from a single founding propagule or zygote, depending on the organism and study convention. A genet may occupy a large area and contain many physically connected modules.
- **Ramet:** A potentially separable physiological or modular unit within a larger clonal organism. Usage varies by field; the term must be defined in each study.
- **Heterokaryon:** A fungal cell or mycelium containing genetically distinct nuclei in a shared cytoplasm. Cytoplasmic continuity therefore does not necessarily mean that every nucleus has the same genotype.
- **Eusociality:** Commonly characterized by overlapping generations, cooperative brood care, and reproductive division of labor.
- **Superorganism:** A proposed higher-level biological individuality attributed to some highly integrated social insect colonies. It is a debated analytical concept, not a literal statement that a colony is anatomically one animal.
- **Symbiosis / mutualism:** A close association between different organisms; mutualism is a relationship in which the partners benefit under the conditions being described. The terms do not imply that conflict, exploitation, or dependency can never occur.

## 2. Case A — Fungal mycelium: physical continuity and resource movement

### 2.1 What is physically connected?

Filamentous fungi grow by extending and branching hyphae. In many fungi, hyphae form an interconnected mycelial network. Hyphal fusion can join branches and establish pathways through which cytoplasm, organelles, and dissolved materials may move. Septa and septal pores regulate continuity; the network is not simply an unrestricted open tube.

Not every visible fungal network is necessarily one continuous physiological individual. Hyphae can remain separate, fusion can be incompatible or restricted, and a substrate may contain more than one fungal genet or species. The appearance of a continuous mat is not, by itself, proof of one genetic individual.

### 2.2 What does experimental evidence establish?

Simonin et al. (2012) manipulated hyphal fusion in *Neurospora crassa*. They compared wild-type colonies with a mutant that lacked the tested fusion capacity and a mutant with substantially reduced fusion. Using labeled nutrient tracers, they found that reduced or absent fusion impaired nutrient translocation, especially across heterogeneous environments. Their experiments also found that resource sharing and genetic exchange between colonies were strongly limited in mature colonies compared with young germlings.

**Narrow result:** In the tested *N. crassa* system, hyphal fusion contributed materially to efficient nutrient movement within a colony. The study does not establish that every fungal species transports resources identically, that every network shares resources equally, or that physically adjacent colonies become one individual.

Fricker et al. (2017) review mycelia as dynamic networks in which network architecture and resource flows influence one another. They describe hyphal tip growth, branching, septation, orientation, and fusion as processes contributing to network formation. They also emphasize that the quantitative contributions of different transport mechanisms remain incompletely understood and that much mechanistic knowledge comes from a limited set of laboratory model species under conditions unlike many natural habitats.

**Qualification:** “The fungus is one network” can be a useful description of a particular mycelium, but it must not be generalized to every visible fungal growth or treated as proof of genetic uniformity.

### 2.3 Genetic identity is not identical to physical continuity

A mycelium can contain multiple nuclei. In heterokaryotic conditions, genetically different nuclei coexist within shared cytoplasm. Fusion between compatible hyphae can create cytoplasmic connection without erasing the genetic distinction among nuclei. Conversely, two regions may share a genetic origin without remaining physically connected.

Therefore:

- Physical connection does not automatically establish genetic identity.
- Genetic similarity does not automatically establish present physical continuity.
- Physiological integration must be demonstrated through the actual exchange or coordinated processes being studied.

The fungal individual is often difficult to delimit because a genet can be spatially extensive, while physiological modules and connected regions may vary in function and persistence.

## 3. Case B — Bees and ants: social integration without bodily fusion

### 3.1 Physical connection

A bee or ant colony is not normally a single continuous body. Its members remain separate animals with their own bodies, nervous systems, and boundaries. Colony-level coordination occurs through interactions among individuals and with the environment, including chemical signals, contact, food exchange, brood care, nest conditions, and task responses.

The colony's integration is therefore not the same physical condition as cytoplasmic continuity within a fused fungal mycelium.

### 3.2 Reproductive relationship and genetic composition

Members of a colony may be related through shared ancestry, but a colony is not necessarily genetically uniform. It may contain offspring from different matings, multiple reproductive lineages, and, depending on species and colony history, more than one reproductive individual. Genetic relatedness can influence cooperation, but it is not the same thing as physical connection or functional coordination.

### 3.3 Functional integration and task allocation

Eusocial colonies coordinate brood care, foraging, nest maintenance, defense, and reproduction. The pattern of activity can be colony-level even though the workers remain distinct organisms.

Gordon (2016) cautions that the phrase “division of labor” can mislead if it suggests that each worker has a fixed, permanent job. In many social insects, task allocation is flexible and distributed: an individual's activity changes with colony needs, resource availability, and the activities of other workers. Colony-level behavior can therefore arise from changing interactions rather than from a permanently assigned job for every member.

Canciani, Arnellos, and Moreno (2019) present a theoretical and organizational argument that highly complex eusocial colonies, including honeybees, can exhibit a higher-level form of biological individuality. They describe the “superorganism” as an inter-identity emerging through interactions among distinct constituent organisms. Their article is a hypothesis-and-theory contribution and an argument within an active debate; it is not proof that all bee or ant colonies should be classified as superorganisms.

Evolutionary-transition approaches also ask how formerly more autonomous units become integrated at a higher level. Carmel and Shavit (2020) propose four operational indicators: system size, inseparability, reproductive specialization, and non-reproductive specialization. They present these as a way to make comparisons explicit, while acknowledging that the concept of individuality is complex and that their operational scheme is incomplete. The indicators do not create one universally accepted threshold for individuality.

**Narrow result:** Some eusocial colonies exhibit strong functional and reproductive integration while their members remain physically distinct organisms. The extent and interpretation of colony-level individuality vary by species and analytical framework.

## 4. Case C — Ant–fungus agriculture: integration across species boundaries

Leafcutter ants provide a third arrangement. The ants do not merely live beside a fungus: they collect plant material and use it to cultivate a fungal crop. The fungus supplies food to the colony, while the ants provide substrate, cultivation, protection, and dispersal opportunities.

Dejean et al. (2023) review several forms of ant–fungus association. In higher attine ants, including leafcutters, the cultivated fungal association is generally obligate: the ants depend on the cultivar for food, and the cultivar depends on the ants for its cultivated conditions and persistence. The review distinguishes food agriculture from other ant–fungus associations in which fungi contribute to protection, plant growth, or nest construction. Not all fungus-growing ants use the same agricultural system, and not every ant–fungus association is the leafcutter model.

The garden is also not necessarily a two-part system. Elliot et al. (2025) review the fungus *Escovopsis*, which attacks attine fungal gardens, and discuss other microorganisms associated with these systems. Some bacterial associates, including *Pseudonocardia* in particular attine relationships, can contribute antimicrobial protection. Other microbes may be neutral, beneficial, parasitic, or pathogenic. The garden is therefore an ecological community with cooperation, dependency, defense, and conflict—not a perfectly harmonious or genetically unified organism.

### 4.1 What remains distinct?

- The ant colony consists of individual insects.
- The cultivated fungus remains a fungus, not an ant organ.
- The ant and fungal partners belong to different species and retain distinct reproductive histories.
- Associated bacteria and other microbes add further biological identities.
- The partners can be tightly interdependent without becoming genetically identical or physically fused into one organism.

This case demonstrates that functional integration can cross species boundaries. It does not demonstrate that the partners lose their individual identities.

The three cases should not be collapsed into one mechanism:

- Fungal integration can include actual cytoplasmic continuity.
- Social-insect integration is primarily behavioral, communicative, reproductive, and regulatory.
- Ant–fungus integration is a multispecies ecological and nutritional association.

They are comparable because each raises questions about the relation between constituent units and a larger organized system—not because they are biologically identical.

## 6. The central finding: integration need not erase distinction

**Supported within the cases examined:** Integration can increase while constituent distinctions remain.

The evidence supports several different forms of relationship:

1. **Continuity within a fungal network:** hyphal fusion can create cytoplasmic pathways that improve resource translocation, while nuclei and colony boundaries may still be genetically complex.
2. **Coordination among social insects:** colony-level activity can be organized through interactions among separate animals, with flexible task allocation and varying degrees of reproductive specialization.
3. **Interdependence across species:** ant–fungus agriculture can establish durable mutual dependence without converting the partners into one genetic organism.

This does not establish a universal law that every integrated system preserves all original distinctions unchanged. Some evolutionary transitions can involve profound changes in the autonomy, reproduction, or role of constituent units. The accurate conclusion is narrower: **functional integration alone is not sufficient evidence that constituent identities have disappeared.** The level and kind of individuality must be examined rather than assumed.

## 11. Chemical information transfer: coordination across preserved boundaries

### 11.1 The next question

The preceding cases establish that functional integration can occur among units that remain physically or genetically distinct. The next question is mechanistic:

**How can one living unit affect the behavior, physiology, development, or organization of another without the two units becoming one organism?**

Chemical transfer is one important answer, but it is not the only answer. Social insects also use tactile, visual, vibrational, and other channels. The evidence below examines chemical communication as a mechanism contributing to coordination and integration; it does not establish that all biological integration is chemical.

### 11.2 Separate the carrier, the information-bearing pattern, and the receiver

A useful analytical distinction is:

1. **Carrier / material:** the molecule or mixture physically emitted, deposited, or transferred.
2. **Information-bearing features:** chemical identity, mixture composition, relative ratios, concentration, dose, timing, location, and persistence.
3. **Receiver:** the organism or tissue with sensory receptors, transport mechanisms, and biochemical machinery capable of detecting or processing the material.
4. **Response:** a change in behavior, physiology, development, or subsequent signaling.

A molecule is not automatically a message merely because it is present. Its biological significance depends on the receiver and the conditions under which it is encountered. A chemical can function as a signal, a cue, a resource, a developmental regulator, or more than one of these, depending on context.

A **signal** is generally understood as a feature that has evolved because it affects a receiver; a **cue** is information available to a receiver whether or not the feature evolved for communication. This distinction prevents the assumption that every informative chemical was deliberately or specifically produced as a message.

The word *information* is used here operationally: a chemical feature carries information when differences in that feature are detectably associated with, and can influence, differences in receiver response. This is not a claim of conscious encoding, language, or intention.

### 11.3 Case D — Bee Orchid and the limits of a compelling mimic

The bee orchid, *Ophrys apifera*, is an especially useful witness because it forces a distinction between appearance, chemical mimicry, pollinator interaction, and actual reproductive outcome.

The genus *Ophrys* is widely known for sexual deception. In many species, flowers resemble female insects and produce volatile blends that mimic components of female insect sex pheromones. Male pollinators may approach and attempt pseudocopulation, during which pollinia can attach and later be transferred to another flower. Research on sexually deceptive *Ophrys* demonstrates that pollinator attraction can depend on chemical blends and their pattern, not simply on the presence of one generic odor.

**Species-level qualification is essential:** *O. apifera* does not follow the usual pattern of many other *Ophrys* species across much of its range. Kew describes it as generally self-pollinated; in the United Kingdom, autonomous self-pollination is especially important. Its flower retains a bee-like appearance, and literature reports chemical mimicry, but its reproductive route varies geographically and cross-pollination has also been reported in parts of its range. Therefore, genus-wide evidence for sexual deception must not be presented as proof that every *O. apifera* population depends on male-bee pseudocopulation.

This distinction strengthens rather than weakens the investigation: a biological structure may retain traits associated with one interaction while its realized reproductive pathway differs by population or environment. The evidence does not permit us to assume that appearance alone identifies the active mechanism in every location.

**Narrow result:** Chemical mimicry is well established in sexually deceptive *Ophrys* systems, while *O. apifera* requires species- and population-specific treatment because autonomous self-pollination is common and geographically variable.

### 11.4 Case E — Ant chemical profiles: recognition and coordinated response

Ants use chemical information in several forms. Cuticular hydrocarbons (CHCs) occur on the insect's outer surface and can contribute to nestmate recognition. In many species, recognition depends on a profile or signature mixture rather than a single universal “colony chemical.” Differences in composition and relative abundance can influence whether an encountered ant is treated as a nestmate or a non-nestmate.

Experimental work with harvester ants found that purified hydrocarbon fractions from non-nestmates could elicit more aggressive responses than nestmate-derived fractions. Reviews of ant chemical communication describe additional roles for chemical cues and signals in recruitment, alarm, trail following, defense, and interactions with other species.

These systems illustrate a key point: a chemical profile can contribute to maintaining a social boundary. Chemical communication does not simply dissolve distinctions between individuals; it can help the colony recognize, preserve, and defend distinctions between members and outsiders.

### 11.5 Case F — Trophallaxis: oral transfer can carry more than food

Trophallaxis is the exchange of liquid food between social insects, including adults and larvae. It has often been described primarily as food sharing. LeBoeuf et al. (2016), studying the Florida carpenter ant *Camponotus floridanus*, found that trophallactic fluid contains digestion-related and other proteins, hydrocarbons, microRNAs, and juvenile hormone, a developmental regulator.

The authors also tested the developmental significance of juvenile hormone transfer. When workers' food—and therefore the fluid passed to larvae—was supplemented with juvenile hormone, the larvae were reported to be twice as likely to complete metamorphosis and became larger workers. This provides experimental evidence that a substance carried through social fluid exchange can affect development in recipients.

A subsequent study of juvenile-hormone-esterase-like proteins found that these proteins are selectively abundant in *Camponotus* trophallactic fluid and investigated their potential role in regulating juvenile hormone availability. The findings support the possibility that oral fluid exchange participates in inter-individual developmental regulation, not merely caloric provisioning.

**Qualification:** The findings are species- and experiment-specific. They do not establish that every component detected in trophallactic fluid functions as a message, that every transferred molecule has a demonstrated effect, or that all social insects regulate development in the same way.

**Narrow result:** In the studied *C. floridanus* system, trophallaxis can transfer biologically active substances, and experimentally altering juvenile-hormone availability through worker-to-larva feeding can change developmental outcomes. This is stronger than a simple correlation between communication and colony organization.

### 11.6 Case G — Honeybee chemical regulation is a blend, not a single master switch

Honeybee colonies use multiple pheromonal systems. Queen-associated chemical blends can influence worker retinue behavior, reproductive physiology, brood care, and other colony activities. However, the queen mandibular pheromone (QMP) should not be treated as a solitary master control. Experimental work has shown that queens without mandibular glands can still influence important worker behaviors and physiology, indicating that other queen-produced signals contribute to regulation.

Honeybee organization also relies on non-chemical channels, including the dance communication system and direct interactions among workers. Chemical signaling is therefore one part of a multimodal regulatory system.

**Narrow result:** Colony regulation can depend on multiple chemical components and partially redundant signals, operating alongside non-chemical communication. A single chemical should not be treated as the complete explanation for colony-level organization.

### 11.8 What would establish causation rather than mere association?

For each proposed chemical mechanism, stronger tests include:

- **Removal or depletion:** remove the suspected source or signal and test whether the response changes.
- **Synthetic reconstruction:** reproduce the chemical blend and test whether it restores or elicits the response.
- **Dose and ratio tests:** vary concentration and component ratios to determine which features matter.
- **Receiver disruption:** block or alter the relevant receptor or sensory pathway and test whether the response is reduced.
- **Rescue:** restore the missing chemical or pathway and determine whether the original response returns.
- **Context controls:** compare the same chemical under different timing, location, receiver state, and environmental conditions.

A detected chemical is not automatically a causal signal. A behavioral response is not automatically evidence of colony-level integration. The chain from source to receiver to response must be established at the appropriate level.

### 11.9 Mechanistic synthesis: from transfer to system-level effect

The evidence supports the following descriptive sequence:

**Distinct producer → chemical material emitted or transferred → receiver detection and processing → behavioral, physiological, or developmental response → changed interactions among units → possible contribution to larger-scale coordination.**

The final step requires separate evidence. An effect on one recipient does not, by itself, prove that the colony has acquired a new higher-level property. To establish that, researchers must show how local responses alter interactions and how those interactions produce a measurable system-level outcome.

Chemical transfer can therefore be one mechanism by which distinct living units influence one another while retaining their organismal boundaries. It may contribute to integration without being sufficient, by itself, to explain every form of integration or individuality.

- **Integration:** repeated or coordinated chemical interactions can contribute to colony regulation, task coordination, and developmental organization.
- **Identity:** chemical communication can preserve or reinforce boundaries, as in nestmate recognition; it does not imply fusion of donor and receiver.
- **Persistence:** recurring transfer and response may contribute to the maintenance of a social organization, but persistence must be measured rather than inferred from one signaling event.
