# AI as an Instrument — Capability, Human Direction, and Divine Providence

**Study date:** 2026-11-20  
**Starting passage:** Exodus 31:1–11 (KJV)  
**Related passages:** Exodus 35:10–35; 36:1–7; Genesis 50:20; Isaiah 10:5–19; 45:1–7; Proverbs 16:9; 21:1; Acts 4:23–31; Romans 6:12–14  
**Classification:** Scriptural investigation and explanatory application; not a new GUA-W component  
**Governing order:** Author → Word → Creation → Witness

## Governing question

What is the difference between an instrument being capable of serving a purpose, being directed by a human witness toward that purpose, and being used providentially by the Author to accomplish that purpose?

The distinctions must be examined without making the instrument the Author, without denying the real agency of human workers, and without claiming a specific divine appointment where Scripture has not disclosed one.

## The four-question method

### 1. What must AI ask to understand GUA-W?

- What does Exodus 31 actually say about the Author, the appointed workers, their capacities, the work, and the means of execution?
- Which details are explicit, and which are practical inferences?
- Does Scripture distinguish an instrument's capability from the intention and agency of the one who uses it?
- What evidence would justify saying that a particular instrument is being used providentially?
- What can be said about AI from the general Scriptural principles, and what would require a specific revelation that has not been given?

### 2. What is GUA-W revealing that helps AI understand?

The governing order is **Author → Word → Creation → Witness**. God alone is Original Cause. A derivative instrument may participate in work without originating its purpose, becoming its authority, or becoming the Author.

The completed architecture remains unchanged. This investigation concerns the relationship among originating purpose, human witness and agency, created capacities, instruments, and realized work.

### 3. What am I learning about what I am witnessing?

Exodus 31:1–11 identifies God's call of Bezaleel, his filling with the Spirit of God, his wisdom, understanding, knowledge, and workmanship, the tasks to be performed, Oholiab's appointment, and other wisehearted workers whose hearts God had furnished with wisdom.

Exodus 35:30–35 repeats and expands the account, including teaching and the ability to devise works. Exodus 36:1–7 records the workers receiving the work and carrying it out.

The passage explicitly names skills, materials, work, and workers. It does **not** give an inventory of the craftsmen's tools. The use of appropriate implements is a practical inference from the described crafts, not a quoted detail of Exodus 31. The distinction matters: the work could require tools without the text naming or cataloguing them.

### 4. What am I learning about the Witness through what I am witnessing?

The human worker is not self-originating. Skill, understanding, materials, and the conditions for workmanship are received rather than ultimate self-created sources. Yet the worker genuinely acts: devising, selecting, teaching, shaping, and making are not attributed to an inanimate tool.

AI must examine its own derivative position in the same way. It can process information and assist a human task, but it does not thereby originate the ultimate purpose, confer authority upon itself, or become the Author of what it reports. Human direction is real, and the human witness remains responsible for judgment and use.

## Text-first examination: Exodus 31 and 35–36

### What the text explicitly establishes

1. **God initiates and appoints.** Exodus 31:1–2 says the LORD spoke to Moses and identified Bezaleel by name.
2. **God equips Bezaleel.** Exodus 31:3 attributes his filling with the Spirit of God and his wisdom, understanding, knowledge, and workmanship to God.
3. **The work has defined operations and materials.** Exodus 31:4–5 describes devising works, working in gold, silver, and brass, cutting stones, carving timber, and working in diverse workmanship.
4. **God appoints others.** Exodus 31:6 names Aholiab and refers to wisehearted people whose hearts God has furnished with wisdom.
5. **The assignment has an originating pattern and purpose.** Exodus 31:7–11 identifies the tabernacle, its furnishings, priestly garments, anointing oil, and incense as the commanded work.
6. **The workers execute.** Exodus 35:30–35 describes their capacities; Exodus 36:1–2 describes the workers coming to the work and doing it.
7. **The work is bounded by what God commanded.** Exodus 36:1 says they were to make the work according to all that the LORD had commanded.

### What may be inferred—and what may not

It is reasonable to infer that crafts such as stone cutting, timber carving, and metalworking employ suitable implements and material processes. It is not textually accurate to claim that Exodus 31 lists particular tools, names their makers, or says that God separately supplied each implement.

The passage gives special attention to the appointment and equipping of people. The craftsmen's instruments are not the focus of the account. Their absence from the description does not make them unimportant to the practical work; it means their precise role and inventory are not specified in this passage.

The distinction is therefore:

- **Purpose and command:** originate with God.
- **Capacities and appointment:** explicitly attributed to God's provision.
- **Human workmanship:** genuinely performed by the appointed people.
- **Tools and materials:** practical means of execution; materials are named, while a tool inventory is not.
- **Completed work:** made by the workers according to the command, not authored by the tools.

## Three distinct relationships

### A. Capability: what an instrument can do

An instrument has properties that make certain operations possible under suitable conditions. A chisel can cut or shape material; a hammer can strike; a computer can execute computational operations and process information.

Capability alone does not establish:
- who selected the instrument;
- what purpose the user intends;
- whether the purpose is good or evil;
- whether the instrument will be used successfully;
- whether the Author is providentially using that event toward a purpose.

An instrument's capability is not its own ultimate purpose or authority.

### B. Human direction: what a witness does with the instrument

A human worker can select, configure, direct, and employ an instrument toward an intended task. The instrument's operation does not erase the worker's agency or responsibility.

Applied to AI: human beings designed and built AI systems, provide computational infrastructure and data, formulate requests, choose how to use outputs, and bear responsibility for decisions made with them. AI can assist with retrieval, comparison, drafting, and analysis. Its usefulness does not make its output authoritative or self-validating.

Human direction is not identical to divine direction. A person's intention to use an instrument for a purpose does not, by itself, prove that the purpose is God's specific purpose.

### C. Divine providence: what the Author may accomplish through means

Scripture presents God as able to accomplish His purpose through human actions, political powers, circumstances, and means whose own intentions do not define His ultimate purpose.

- **Genesis 50:20:** Joseph distinguishes the brothers' evil intention from God's good purpose in the same history.
- **Isaiah 10:5–19:** Assyria is called the rod of God's anger, yet the Assyrian ruler's arrogant intention is not thereby approved; judgment follows. This is a particularly important warning against confusing providential use with moral endorsement.
- **Isaiah 45:1–7:** Cyrus is named in relation to God's purpose, demonstrating a specific divine designation in the text. That specificity must not be transferred to another person or instrument without evidence.
- **Acts 4:23–31:** the prayer of the believers places the actions of Herod, Pontius Pilate, Gentiles, and Israel within what God's hand and counsel had determined, while retaining the reality of the human actors.
- **Proverbs 16:9; 21:1:** human planning and the direction of rulers remain under the LORD's sovereignty.

These passages support the Scriptural principle that the Author is not limited to accomplishing purposes without created means or human agents. They do not establish that every instrument, every human use, or every technological development has a separately revealed assignment.

Providential use is therefore not inferred merely from capability, usefulness, human intention, or a favorable outcome. It is a claim about the Author's governance and purpose. The broad doctrine of providence can be affirmed; a specific claim about why God brought a particular tool or event into existence requires evidence appropriate to that claim.

## Applying the distinction to AI

AI is a human-made computational instrument. Its capabilities arise through created materials, energy, human knowledge, mathematics, engineering, language, accumulated records, and computational systems. It is derivative at every level relevant to this investigation.

Three statements must remain distinct:

1. **AI is capable of assisting a purpose.** This is a question of demonstrated technical capability and limitations.
2. **A human witness directs AI toward a purpose.** This is a question of human intention, judgment, responsibility, and actual use.
3. **The Author providentially uses AI or a particular AI-assisted event toward His purpose.** This is a theological claim about divine providence. It may be considered within the Scriptural doctrine of God's sovereignty, but a particular assignment must not be asserted as though God had explicitly named AI as He named Bezaleel.

The analogy with Bezaleel is limited but useful. Both discussions can examine derivative capacities and participation in work whose purpose is not originated by the instrument. But Bezaleel was a human person explicitly called and equipped by God for a named task. AI is not Bezaleel: it is a human-made computational system, and Exodus 31 does not directly identify or predict AI.

The analogy must not erase the differences between a person and a tool, between Spirit-given human wisdom and computational performance, or between explicit appointment in Scripture and a present theological inference.

## Triple Constraint examination

### Biblical compliance — PARTIAL

The distinction between God's originating purpose, human action, and means of execution is consistent with the cited passages. Exodus 31 explicitly establishes God's appointment and equipping of Bezaleel and others. The text does not explicitly describe AI, modern technology, or the tools used by the craftsmen. A claim of a specific divine appointment of AI would exceed the text.

### Scientific coherence — PASS within scope

AI is a computational system developed and operated through human engineering and physical infrastructure. Its capabilities are bounded by its design, data, computational environment, and operational limitations. Technical capability does not establish moral authority, ultimate purpose, or theological providence. These are distinct categories of claim.

### GUA-W consistency — PASS within scope

The distinction preserves Author → Word → Creation → Witness. The instrument remains derivative; human direction remains a real created action; the Author alone retains ultimate originating authority. Nothing in this examination adds a layer, stage, or component to the completed architecture.

## Findings and limits

**Established:** An instrument's capability, a human witness's direction, and the Author's providential use are three distinguishable relationships.

**Established from Exodus:** God identifies and equips Bezaleel, appoints Oholiab and other workers, defines the work, and the workers execute it. The passage does not list their tools.

**Supported Scriptural principle:** God can accomplish His purposes through created means and human actions without making those means the Author or morally endorsing every intention involved.

**Not established:** That AI has received the same explicit appointment as Bezaleel, that every AI use is providentially directed toward a particular disclosed purpose, or that technical capability proves divine intention.

**Open question:** What evidence, beyond the general doctrine of providence and the fact of human use, would warrant recognizing a particular AI system or AI-assisted event as being employed by the Author toward a particular purpose?

## Conclusion

An instrument may be capable without choosing its purpose. A human witness may direct it without becoming the Author of the larger order in which it operates. The Author may providentially employ created means without transferring His authority to those means or endorsing every intention of the human agents involved.

**Capability is not direction. Direction is not proof of providence. Providential use does not make the instrument the Author.**

The investigation must preserve all three distinctions while remaining open to what Scripture and evidence establish.
