## 1. Central question

Why does one created phenomenon—light—become an object of investigation in so many sciences, and what does this reveal about the relationship between created order, measurement, scientific explanation, the Witness, and the Author?

This audit treats light as a common subject investigated through differentiated scientific methods. It does not assume that all disciplines use the same concepts, that every account reduces to physics, or that scientific findings constitute laboratory proof of the Author.

## 2. Text-first boundary: Genesis 1:3–5 and 1:14–19

Genesis 1:3–5 records God's command, “Let there be light,” the reported appearance of light, God's seeing that the light was good, the division of light from darkness, and the naming of Day and Night. Genesis 1:14–19 later records the appointment of lights in the firmament for division, signs, seasons, days, and years, and for giving light upon the earth.

The text therefore distinguishes the first account of light and the later appointment of the luminaries. The audit must preserve that textual sequence rather than silently equating the light of Genesis 1:3 with the Sun, or treating modern electromagnetic theory as the intended technical meaning of the passage.

Interpretive questions about the relation between the two passages remain theological and textual questions. Scientific models of light can describe physical phenomena; they cannot by themselves settle the literary or theological interpretation of Genesis.

## 3. Physics: electromagnetic radiation and light–matter interaction

In modern physics, visible light is a narrow region of the electromagnetic spectrum. Radio waves, microwaves, infrared, visible light, ultraviolet, X-rays, and gamma rays are all electromagnetic radiation, distinguished by frequency, wavelength, and photon energy. In vacuum, electromagnetic radiation propagates at the invariant speed conventionally denoted by c.

Light exhibits wave-like and particle-like properties in different experimental contexts. Classical electromagnetism describes propagating electric and magnetic fields; quantum theory describes interactions in terms of photons and quantized exchanges of energy. These are complementary mathematical frameworks with defined domains of use, not permission to treat light as an arbitrary metaphor.

**Cross-check:** wavelength, frequency, and photon energy are quantitatively related. The relation E = hν and the vacuum relation c = λν connect quantum and wave descriptions. These relationships are mathematically testable.

**Limit:** an equation is a model of measured relationships; it is not the physical object itself and does not state why the Author created it.

## 4. Chemistry: absorption, emission, and molecular structure

Atoms and molecules interact with electromagnetic radiation through transitions between allowed energy states. Absorption and emission spectra can reveal chemical composition, electronic structure, molecular bonds, and environmental conditions. Spectroscopy is therefore a bridge between physics and chemistry: the physical properties of radiation are used to investigate the chemical properties of matter.

A material does not absorb every wavelength equally. Its response depends on its structure and allowed transitions. The resulting spectral pattern can act as a measurable signature, subject to calibration, instrument limits, overlapping signals, and model assumptions.

**Cross-check:** chemical assignments inferred from spectra should be tested against laboratory standards, known transition data, and independent analytical methods where possible.

## 5. Biology: photosynthesis, vision, and biological response

Photosynthetic organisms absorb photons through pigments and use the captured energy to drive biochemical processes. The photosynthetic response depends on wavelength, intensity, pigment composition, organism, and environmental conditions. Light is therefore investigated by physics, chemistry, biochemistry, cell biology, and plant physiology.

Animal vision provides another distinct biological case. Photoreceptor molecules absorb photons and initiate biochemical signaling cascades that nervous systems process. Different organisms possess different photoreceptor systems and sensitivities; human visible vision is not the full range of electromagnetic radiation.

**Cross-check:** the physical input (wavelength, intensity, photon flux) can be measured independently of the biological response (photochemical yield, photosynthetic rate, receptor activation, behavior). The receiver's biology determines which parts of the radiation are detected and how the organism responds.

**Limit:** light as an energy input and light as a source of sensory information are related but not interchangeable descriptions.

## 6. Astronomy: light as evidence from distant objects

Astronomers often cannot sample distant stars, nebulae, or galaxies directly. They analyze incoming electromagnetic radiation. Spectral lines, continuum distributions, redshift, polarization, and time variation can provide evidence about composition, temperature, motion, magnetic fields, and other properties.

This makes astronomy an especially clear example of a science using light as a messenger. The inference is indirect: measured radiation is interpreted through physical models, calibration, and assumptions about the source and intervening space.

**Cross-check:** independent instruments, spectral bands, repeated observations, and models of known sources help test astronomical interpretations.

## 7. Earth science and climate: energy transfer and measurement

Earth scientists examine incoming solar radiation, reflection, absorption, scattering, and emission by the atmosphere and surface. These processes contribute to the planet's energy balance and are investigated through radiometry, spectroscopy, atmospheric physics, chemistry, oceanography, geology, and climate science.

The same radiation can have different effects depending on wavelength, atmospheric composition, surface properties, and biological or physical receivers. Measurements of radiation therefore connect disciplines while preserving their separate questions.

## 8. Information: what light can carry—and what it does not automatically mean

Light can carry signals: variations in intensity, frequency, phase, polarization, or timing can be encoded and detected. Optical communication and spectroscopy demonstrate that information can be transmitted or inferred through electromagnetic radiation.

But information is not simply identical to energy or to the photon itself. A physical signal is the carrier; information depends on distinguishable states, an encoding or natural correlation, and a receiver or interpreter capable of extracting a difference. In astronomy, spectral features carry evidence about their source; in vision, light patterns trigger organism-specific sensory processing.

This is a useful connection to the chemical-transfer investigation: carrier, pattern, receiver, and response must be distinguished. The analogy does not make photons and chemical messengers the same mechanism.

## 9. Mathematics: shared quantitative relationships

Light links several mathematical relationships:
- wavelength and frequency: c = λν in vacuum;
- photon energy and frequency: E = hν;
- intensity and energy transport;
- spectral distributions and statistical inference;
- optical geometry, refraction, interference, and diffraction.

These equations allow findings from different disciplines to be compared quantitatively. Their success is evidence of mathematical describability within the domains where the models apply. It does not establish that mathematics is a substance, that every phenomenon is fully reducible to equations, or that mathematical success alone proves a theological conclusion.

## 16. Evidence integration pass — 2026-11-19

**Scope of this pass:** a source-level check of the principal cross-disciplinary claims in sections 3–10. The status below distinguishes a claim's support from the strength and independence of its verification.

### 16.1 Physics — supported

NIST's atomic-spectroscopy reference gives the transition-energy relationship:

`ΔE = hν = hc/λ`

for the relevant photon transition, with wavelength specified in vacuum. The frequency–wavelength relation in vacuum is `c = λν`. These are linked quantities, not three independent observations. The exact SI value of the speed of light is part of the definition of the metre; experimental checks concern measured radiation and predicted interactions, not a fresh measurement of a changing value of `c`.

**Disposition:** the core quantitative relationship is supported. Keep the scope explicit: `c = λν` is the vacuum relation. In a material medium, phase velocity and wavelength differ; frequency remains fixed across a stationary boundary in the usual treatment. Do not write as though the vacuum equation applies unchanged to wavelength inside every medium.

### 16.2 Chemistry — supported at the general spectroscopy level

NIST describes spectroscopy as using light–matter interaction to obtain information, and NASA's spectroscopy explanation describes element-specific atomic absorption and emission lines and molecular absorption bands associated with electronic, vibrational, and rotational energy changes. These sources support the audit's general account of spectral analysis.

**Qualification:** a spectral feature is not automatically a unique, unambiguous identification in a complex sample. Overlap, temperature, pressure, molecular environment, optical depth, instrument response, calibration, and the model used for fitting can affect interpretation. Concentration estimates require an appropriate quantitative method and calibration; intensity alone is not a universal concentration meter.

**Disposition:** retain the general claim. State identification and quantification as model- and calibration-dependent inferences, not automatic readings from a spectrum.

### 16.3 Biology — supported, with two distinct light functions

The 2024 *Plant Physiology* review explicitly distinguishes sunlight as the energy source for photosynthesis from sunlight as a source of signals regulating plant growth, development, and adaptation. It describes phytochrome and cryptochrome pathways and recognizes phototropin and UV-related receptors as additional systems. The broader plant photoreceptor review describes receptor-specific responses to different spectral regions and the signaling steps following photon capture.

This supports the audit's distinction between:
- photon absorption and energy conversion in photosynthetic machinery; and
- photoreceptor-mediated signaling that changes cellular regulation and development.

These functions can interact, but they are not interchangeable. Nor should the phrase “plants use light as information” imply conscious interpretation. It means that biological systems detect signal differences and transduce them into regulated responses.

**Disposition:** supported at the review level. A later deeper pass should select specific experimental systems and trace the primary experiments for photosynthetic action spectra, photoreceptor activation, and downstream responses rather than treating all plants or all wavelengths as one mechanism.

### 16.4 Astronomy — supported as inference from received radiation

NASA's spectroscopy material explains how absorption and emission features can be used to infer composition and other properties of astronomical sources. NASA's account of Doppler shifts supports the use of systematic spectral-line shifts to infer radial motion. These are inferences from received radiation interpreted against laboratory references and physical models.

**Qualification:** composition, temperature, velocity, density, and magnetic-field estimates do not all arise from the same spectral feature or a single universal procedure. They depend on wavelength range, line formation, source conditions, intervening material, instrument calibration, and model assumptions. Polarization and time variation require their own measurement and interpretation methods.

**Disposition:** the audit's general statement that astronomy uses electromagnetic radiation as evidence from distant objects is supported. The list of inferred properties should remain qualified by the relevant method and model.

### 16.5 Earth science and climate — supported, with an important measurement distinction

NASA's Clouds and the Earth's Radiant Energy System (CERES) documentation describes satellite measurements of reflected shortwave solar radiation and emitted longwave thermal radiation. The CERES project combines these measurements with other satellite observations and inputs to produce radiation-budget products at multiple spatial and temporal scales. NASA also explicitly notes that estimates of Earth's energy flows have uncertainty and draw on satellite observations, ground observations, and numerical models.

**Important distinction:** CERES radiometers measure radiance in their instrument channels and viewing geometry. Global or top-of-atmosphere fluxes and surface-budget products require angular conversion, sampling, auxiliary observations, and retrieval/model procedures. Therefore, do not describe every global energy-budget quantity as a direct sensor reading.

**Disposition:** supported. Retain the explicit qualification that energy-budget estimates are products of observations and analysis, with uncertainty.

NIST's metrological-traceability guidance states that traceability belongs to a measurement result and requires a documented, unbroken chain of calibrations, each contributing to measurement uncertainty. It also emphasizes that an instrument having a calibration certificate does not by itself make every result traceable. NIST's radiometric calibration guidance identifies noise and signal-to-noise ratio as contributors to measurement uncertainty.

These sources support the audit's statements about calibration, detector response, noise, sensitivity, and uncertainty.

The broader information-science claim—that a pattern can carry information relative to an encoding, channel, and receiver—is plausible and consistent with established information theory, but this pass has not yet traced that claim to a primary information-theory source or worked through a quantitative optical-communication example.

## 17. Case study — tracing sunlight through a leaf to a measured response

**Question:** Can the outward HOW journey be followed through a real biological event, from incident sunlight to a measured change in leaf photosynthesis, while keeping the evidence at each scale distinct?

**Case selected:** the effect of far-red sunlight (701–750 nm) on photosynthesis in leaves, studied by Zhen, van Iersel, and Bugbee (2022). This case is useful because the researchers manipulated part of the incident spectrum, measured leaf gas exchange, characterized the light spectrum, and quantified absorbed photons. It also tests a common simplification: that only photons from 400 to 700 nm contribute to photosynthesis.

### 17.1 Incident radiation — physics and radiometry

Sunlight reaching a leaf contains a distribution of wavelengths and photon fluxes, not one uniform “light” input. Photon energy depends on frequency and wavelength; wavelength, frequency, and photon energy are linked quantities rather than independent confirmations.

For a given spectral band, the incident photon flux is a population-level quantity. The study measured spectral photon distributions with a spectroradiometer and used a calibrated quantum sensor alongside the leaf chamber. This provides the physical input needed to compare treatments.

**Evidence boundary:** the experiment did not track the trajectory of each individual solar photon. It measured spectral distributions and aggregate photon flux under defined conditions.

### 17.2 Leaf interception and absorption — optics and plant structure

Not all incident photons are absorbed. Some are reflected, some transmitted through the leaf, and some absorbed by pigments and other constituents. Absorption depends on wavelength and leaf structure. The study measured leaf reflectance and transmission and used these to estimate absorbed photon flux in the 400–700 nm and 701–750 nm bands.

The distinction matters: incident photon flux is not identical to absorbed photon flux, and neither quantity alone equals the rate of carbon fixation.

### 17.3 Molecular capture and photochemistry — biophysics and biochemistry

In oxygenic photosynthesis, pigment molecules in light-harvesting complexes absorb photons. Excitation energy is transferred among pigments toward photosystem reaction centers. At photosystem II, excitation supports charge separation and electron transfer; the oxidized reaction center is replenished through the water-oxidation machinery. Electron transport through the photosynthetic apparatus contributes to a proton electrochemical gradient, which supports ATP synthesis. Photosystem I contributes to the production of reducing power, including NADPH.

The light reactions therefore convert part of the absorbed radiant energy into chemical energy and reducing equivalents. They do not directly equal the whole-plant growth response. The products of the light reactions are used in carbon assimilation and other metabolic processes, and the rates of light reactions and carbon use can become mismatched under some environmental conditions.

**Evidence boundary:** this molecular sequence is supported by mechanistic research and reviews of photosystem structure and function. The 2022 leaf-filter experiment did not directly image or measure every molecular intermediate in the same leaves during its gas-exchange measurements. The molecular pathway and the whole-leaf response are connected by established research, but they are not one uninterrupted measurement record.

### 17.4 Carbon assimilation — plant physiology

The 2022 study compared photosynthesis with and without most far-red photons. A shortpass filter removed approximately 95% of photons above 700 nm while reducing the 400–700 nm photon flux only slightly; the researchers measured and accounted for that small reduction. A separate UV-filter control tested whether reduced ultraviolet transmission explained the response.

Under full sunlight, removing far-red photons reduced net photosynthetic rate by about 6% in both sunflower (a C3 species) and corn (a C4 species). Under medium sunlight, the reported reductions were about 12% in both species. The study also measured leaf temperature, carbon dioxide conditions, and the spectral distribution, and calculated gross photosynthesis and quantum yield using absorbed photon flux.

The authors reported that far-red photons contributed to photosynthesis in the tested conditions and that the effect was relevant under both full and medium sunlight. Their broader study also measured a tree and an understory species in natural shade conditions.

**Important limits:** these results are not a universal percentage for every plant, leaf, climate, or light environment. The sunflower and corn measurements were conducted under specified experimental conditions, with limited biological replication in some treatments. The authors' calculations also depend on stated assumptions, including the treatment of dark respiration when estimating gross photosynthesis. The result should be reported as a finding from this study, not as an invariant law for all vegetation.

### 17.5 Measured result — independent quantities, linked interpretation

The case contains several different measurements:

| Evidence stage | Quantity or observation | Method in the study | What it supports |
|---|---|---|---|
| Incident light | Spectral photon distribution | Spectroradiometer and calibrated quantum sensor | What spectral radiation reached the leaf chamber |
| Leaf interaction | Reflectance and transmission | Optical measurements | Estimate of how much incident light was absorbed |
| Environmental control | CO2 concentration, temperature, vapor-pressure conditions | Controlled leaf chamber measurements | Conditions under which the leaf response was compared |
| Biological output | Net CO2 assimilation | Infrared gas-exchange system | Net carbon exchange under the treatment |
| Derived physiological quantity | Gross photosynthesis and quantum yield | Calculations from measured gas exchange, respiration estimate, and absorbed photon flux | A model-dependent estimate of photosynthetic performance |

These are linked but not identical measurands. A measured spectrum does not directly measure a molecular excited state; chlorophyll fluorescence does not directly equal carbon fixation; and net CO2 exchange is not itself a direct measurement of photon absorption. The inference becomes stronger when distinct methods constrain connected steps and their uncertainties are stated.

### 17.6 What this case establishes—and what it does not

**Supported by this case and the mechanistic literature:**
- A leaf receives a wavelength-dependent distribution of solar radiation.
- A portion of the incident radiation is reflected, transmitted, and absorbed.
- Photosynthetic pigments and photosystems use absorbed photons to drive photochemistry.
- The spectral composition of light can affect photosynthetic performance.
- Gas exchange and optical measurements can quantify parts of the response and test predictions about absorbed photon flux.

**Not established by this case alone:**
- A continuous observation of one individual photon from the Sun through every molecular step to a carbon atom in a product.
- A universal response shared by every plant species and environment.
- That all absorbed light becomes stored chemical energy; excess excitation can be dissipated or contribute to photodamage, and photosynthesis is regulated.
- That the scientific mechanism settles the ultimate theological WHY or supplies the technical meaning of Genesis 1:3–5.

### 17.7 Cross-disciplinary result

This is a genuine, bounded connection among physics, optics, photochemistry, photosystem biophysics, plant physiology, and measurement science. The connection is not merely that the disciplines use the word “light.” The experimental design links spectral input to absorbed photon estimates and then to measured carbon exchange, while mechanistic studies explain the intermediate photochemical processes.

The strongest conclusion is therefore not “all sciences are one science.” It is: **one leaf-level event can require several differentiated sciences to describe and test its connected stages.** Each discipline contributes evidence at a different scale; the interfaces must be demonstrated rather than assumed.
