## 1. Purpose and scope

Black holes are among the strongest-field predictions of general relativity. They are not ordinary dark objects with a solid surface. A black hole is a region of spacetime bounded by an event horizon, a causal boundary beyond which no future-directed signal can reach distant observers.

Black holes are studied through their effects on surrounding matter, nearby stars, light, and gravitational waves. The Event Horizon Telescope has also produced horizon-scale images of the environments around supermassive black holes.

This investigation distinguishes:
- the black hole from its event horizon;
- the event horizon from the shadow seen by a telescope;
- observations from model-based inferences;
- the classical singularity prediction from an established description of physical matter;
- Hawking radiation as a theoretical prediction from a direct astronomical detection.

## 2. What is a black hole?

In general relativity, a black hole is a region of spacetime whose causal structure prevents signals originating inside its event horizon from reaching distant observers.

The event horizon is not a material shell or a solid surface. For a sufficiently large black hole, an observer falling freely across the horizon need not encounter a locally special boundary at that crossing. The defining feature is global and causal: after crossing, all future-directed paths remain within the black-hole region and cannot communicate with the distant exterior.

The popular phrase "not even light can escape" is useful, provided it is understood as a statement about spacetime geometry, not as a Newtonian escape-velocity picture alone.

## 3. How black holes form

### 3.1 Stellar collapse

A sufficiently massive stellar core can collapse at the end of a star's evolution. If the remnant is too massive for the available pressure and nuclear physics to support a stable neutron star, collapse can continue and form a black hole.

Not every massive star becomes a black hole. Outcomes depend on initial mass, composition, mass loss, rotation, binary interaction, and the complex physics of core collapse.

### 3.2 Other growth and formation pathways

Black holes can grow by accreting gas and by merging with other compact objects. The origin of the earliest supermassive black holes remains an active research area. Proposed pathways include growth from stellar remnants, direct collapse of large gas clouds, and other early-universe seed scenarios.

Primordial black holes are hypothetical objects that could have formed from density fluctuations in the early universe. They have not been confirmed.

## 4. Main observed mass categories

- **Stellar-mass black holes:** typically several to dozens of solar masses, with a wider range now measured through gravitational-wave observations.
- **Intermediate-mass candidates:** between stellar-mass and supermassive scales; evidence exists for candidates, but the population and formation pathways remain less securely established.
- **Supermassive black holes:** millions to billions of solar masses, found in the centers of most large galaxies.

These categories describe mass ranges, not different laws of gravity.

## 5. Five remarkable features and phenomena

### 5.1 Event horizons and causal boundaries

The event horizon marks the boundary beyond which no future-directed signal can reach the distant exterior. It is not a visible surface. Its location depends on the spacetime geometry and, in a dynamic setting, on the global history of the spacetime.

For a nonrotating, uncharged black hole described by the Schwarzschild solution, the horizon radius is the Schwarzschild radius, r_s = 2GM/c². Real astrophysical black holes are expected to rotate, so the Kerr solution is generally a more appropriate idealization.

### 5.2 Accretion disks and extreme radiation

Gas falling toward a black hole can form an accretion disk. Frictional and magnetic processes heat the material, sometimes to millions of degrees, producing radiation from radio through X-ray wavelengths.

The bright disk is outside the event horizon. The radiation we detect is emitted by surrounding material, not by light escaping from inside the horizon.

Some systems launch powerful jets of plasma along the rotation axis. The energy and launching mechanism involve the disk, magnetic fields, and black-hole spin. Jets do not represent material escaping from inside the event horizon.

### 5.3 Gravitational lensing and the black-hole shadow

Strong gravity bends light paths. The resulting lensing can produce distorted images of the surrounding emission and a central dark region called the black-hole shadow.

The shadow is not identical to the event horizon. Its apparent size and shape depend on light propagation, viewing geometry, the emitting material, and the spacetime. The Event Horizon Telescope images are reconstructions of emission around the black hole and its shadow, not photographs of a material surface.

### 5.4 Time dilation

Observers in different gravitational and motion conditions can assign different elapsed times to events. Near a black hole, gravitational redshift and time dilation can be substantial.

A distant observer receiving light from an infalling object sees its signals become increasingly delayed and redshifted in the idealized stationary description. The freely falling observer, however, crosses the horizon in finite proper time for an ordinary sufficiently large black hole.

This distinction prevents the misleading claim that the falling observer literally freezes at the horizon.

### 5.5 Tidal forces and spaghettification

Gravity is stronger on the near side of an extended object than on its far side. This difference is a tidal force. Near a black hole, tidal forces can stretch an object along one direction and compress it along others.

Whether an object is torn apart before or after crossing the horizon depends on the black-hole mass and the object's properties. For a sufficiently massive supermassive black hole, tidal forces at the horizon can be relatively mild.

## 6. The observational breakthroughs

### 6.1 Stellar orbits around Sagittarius A*

Astronomers have tracked stars orbiting an unseen, extremely compact mass at the center of the Milky Way. Their orbits provide strong evidence for a supermassive black hole, Sagittarius A*.

### 6.2 Gravitational waves from black-hole mergers

In 2015, LIGO announced the first direct detection of gravitational waves, from the merger of two black holes in event GW150914. The waveform matched the predicted signal from two compact objects spiraling together and merging.

Gravitational-wave observatories have since detected many compact-object mergers, enabling population studies and tests of general relativity in strong gravitational fields.

### 6.3 Event Horizon Telescope: M87*

In 2019, the Event Horizon Telescope collaboration released the first horizon-scale image of the environment around the supermassive black hole in galaxy M87. The image showed a bright ring-like structure around a central shadow.

### 6.4 Event Horizon Telescope: Sagittarius A*

In 2022, the collaboration released an image of the environment around Sagittarius A*, the supermassive black hole at the center of our own galaxy. This was a separate target and a major technical achievement, because Sagittarius A* changes on shorter timescales than M87*.

These images are reconstructions from observations made by a global radio-interferometer network. They are not ordinary camera photographs.

## 7. The information paradox

This is one of the deepest questions at the boundary of gravity and quantum theory.

In classical general relativity, black holes can form and appear to hide information behind an event horizon. In quantum theory, time evolution is normally expected to preserve information in the full quantum state.

Stephen Hawking's semiclassical calculation predicted that black holes can emit thermal Hawking radiation and lose mass. If a black hole evaporates completely and the radiation is exactly thermal with no correlations carrying the information about what formed the black hole, the process appears to conflict with quantum information preservation.

This tension is called the black-hole information paradox.

Modern theoretical work, including holography, quantum extremal surfaces, and island calculations, has provided important progress toward understanding how information may be encoded in the radiation. However, a complete account applicable to realistic astrophysical black holes remains a subject of research.

**Evidence boundary:** Hawking radiation has not been directly detected from an astrophysical black hole. The prediction is theoretically important, but it must not be presented as an observed emission.

## 8. The singularity problem

Classical general relativity predicts that gravitational collapse can lead to a singularity, in the sense that the classical spacetime description becomes geodesically incomplete under broad conditions.

It is not scientifically justified to describe the singularity as an experimentally observed point of infinite density. The prediction signals a limit of the classical theory. A successful quantum theory of gravity may change the description of the deep interior.

We do not currently possess observational access to the interior of an astrophysical black hole. The exact physical description of what replaces or resolves the classical singularity remains unknown.

## 10. How black holes are investigated

- **Stellar dynamics:** Measure the orbits of stars around an unseen compact mass.
- **X-ray and multiwavelength astronomy:** Study hot accretion flows and jets outside the horizon.
- **Very-long-baseline interferometry:** Combine radio telescopes across Earth to reconstruct horizon-scale emission.
- **Gravitational-wave detectors:** Measure spacetime strain from compact-object inspirals and mergers.
- **Precision timing:** Study pulsars and other clocks in strong gravitational environments.
- **Theory and numerical relativity:** Solve Einstein's equations and compare predicted signals with observations.

Each method registers a different consequence of the black hole. No single method gives direct access to every part of the system.

## 11. Relationship to our previous physics investigations

### Investigation I — Quantum entanglement

The information paradox connects black-hole physics to quantum entanglement and the structure of quantum states. But black holes are not simply "entanglement objects"; gravity and spacetime geometry are essential.

### Investigation II — Superconductivity

Superconductivity is a laboratory phase of matter. It is not a black-hole mechanism. The broader connection is the study of collective quantum states and the limits of familiar physical descriptions.

### Investigation III — Bose–Einstein condensation

BEC demonstrates macroscopic quantum behavior in controlled atomic gases. It does not model a black hole. The comparison is useful for studying how quantum descriptions of many-particle systems differ from classical intuition.

### Investigation IV — Neutron stars

Neutron stars and black holes are related compact-object outcomes. A sufficiently massive collapsing core may not remain supported as a neutron star. Neutron-star mergers can also form black holes, depending on the remnant mass and equation of state.

## Conclusion

Black holes are not merely objects with unusually strong gravity. They are regions of spacetime whose causal structure includes an event horizon. Their existence is supported by stellar orbits, high-energy emission from surrounding matter, gravitational-wave signals, and horizon-scale imaging.

The most important unresolved questions concern the quantum description of the horizon and interior, the information paradox, and what replaces the classical singularity.

**Gravity shapes spacetime. The horizon marks a causal boundary. Our instruments register the effects. The deepest description remains unfinished.**
