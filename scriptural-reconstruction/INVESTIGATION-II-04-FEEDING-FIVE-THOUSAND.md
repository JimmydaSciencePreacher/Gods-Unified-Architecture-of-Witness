# Investigation II.4 — Feeding the Five Thousand: Provision in the Hands of Christ
**Primary texts:** Matthew 14:13–21; Mark 6:30–44; Luke 9:10–17; John 6:1–14 (KJV)  
**Related texts:** Exodus 16; 2 Kings 4:42–44; John 1:1–3, 14; John 6:22–59; John 20:30–31  
**Status:** Comparative Gospel observations recorded; historical and physical mechanism not described.

## Question
What do the four Gospel accounts report about Jesus' provision for the multitude, the disciples' participation, the abundance remaining, and the meaning Jesus later gives the event?

## Shared narrative outline
Jesus withdraws to a place apart; a great multitude follows. He has compassion and teaches and/or heals them (details vary by Gospel). As the day advances, the disciples raise the problem of feeding the people. The available food is identified as five loaves and two fishes. Jesus takes the food, looks to heaven, blesses or gives thanks, breaks it, and gives it to the disciples to distribute. The people eat and are filled. Twelve baskets of fragments are gathered.

## Differences to preserve
- Matthew, Mark, and Luke emphasize the disciples' concern about the remote place and the need to send the people away or obtain food.
- John records the dialogue with Philip, Andrew's identification of a lad with five barley loaves and two small fishes, and the approaching Passover (John 6:4–9).
- John records Jesus' question to Philip as a test, “for he himself knew what he would do” (John 6:6).
- The Gospel accounts differ in selected details and narrative emphasis; comparison must not erase them.

## What the text states
The food is inadequate by ordinary human assessment for the multitude. Jesus gives thanks/blesses, distributes through the disciples, and the people eat until filled. Twelve baskets remain. John records that the people identify Jesus as “that prophet” and that Jesus perceives their intention to take Him by force and make Him a king (John 6:14–15).

## Witness and agency
Jesus takes, blesses, and gives. The disciples distribute; the multitude receives; fragments are collected. The disciples are instruments of distribution, not the source of provision. John distinguishes Jesus' knowledge of what He will do from Philip's calculation of the problem.

## Canonical context
Exodus 16 and 2 Kings 4:42–44 provide important Scriptural contexts for provision of food in the wilderness or through prophetic ministry. John 6 must be read beyond the meal: Jesus later addresses the crowd's motives and identifies Himself as the bread of life (John 6:22–59). The physical provision is a sign within a larger discourse about Him.

## What the text does not state
The accounts do not describe a measurable multiplication process, the precise number of individual fragments, or a physical mechanism. Do not infer a modern production model. The event is not the same event as the feeding of the four thousand (Matthew 15; Mark 8).

## Triple Constraint
- **Biblical compliance — PASS:** the four accounts report the feeding; differences are retained; the later John 6 discourse is included.
- **Scientific coherence — PASS for disciplined description; mechanism UNRESOLVED:** no experimental or physical process data are supplied.
- **GUA-W consistency — PASS for the governing relationship; technical mapping UNRESOLVED:** Christ is the source of provision; disciples are derivative distributors; recipients witness the result.

## Provisional finding
The sign displays provision beyond the disciples' available resources, but John does not let the reader stop at the meal. The sign directs attention to the identity and teaching of Jesus. The disciples distribute what He gives; they do not originate the provision.

**The insufficiency is seen by the disciples. The provision comes from Jesus. The multitude is filled.**
