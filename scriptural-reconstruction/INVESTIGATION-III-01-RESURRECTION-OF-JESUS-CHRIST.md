# Investigation III.1 — The Resurrection of Jesus Christ

**Primary texts:** Matthew 28; Mark 16; Luke 24; John 20–21 (KJV)  
**Foundational Passion texts:** Matthew 27:32–66; Mark 15:21–47; Luke 23:26–56; John 19:16–42  
**Canonical witnesses:** John 1:1–14; John 2:18–22; John 10:17–18; John 11:25–26; Acts 2:22–36; Acts 13:26–39; Romans 6:4–11; Romans 8:11; 1 Corinthians 15; Hebrews 1:1–3; Revelation 1:17–18  
**Status:** Central Scriptural investigation opened; initial text-first findings recorded. Full historical, textual-critical, and scientific examination remains in progress.

## 1. Why the Resurrection receives its own central investigation

The Resurrection of Jesus Christ is not being entered as an ordinary healing account or simply as case study thirty-six in the preceding catalogue. Investigation II recorded thirty-five event-level studies of miracles and events of and surrounding Jesus. The Resurrection now receives its own central investigation because Scripture gives it a unique place in the identity, work, and testimony concerning Jesus Christ.

The distinction is explicit in the New Testament:

- Jesus raises others from the dead during His ministry (for example, Luke 7:11–17; Luke 8:49–56; John 11:1–44).
- Jesus predicts His own death and Resurrection (Matthew 16:21; 17:22–23; 20:18–19; John 2:19–22).
- The apostles proclaim that God raised Jesus from the dead (Acts 2:24, 32; 3:15; 4:10; 10:40; 13:30–37).
- Jesus declares that He has authority to lay down His life and to take it again (John 10:17–18).
- Paul identifies Christ as the firstfruits of them that slept (1 Corinthians 15:20–23).

The Resurrection is therefore examined on its own Scriptural terms. It is not reduced to a repeat of Lazarus' raising, a generic return to biological life, or a physical mechanism supplied by speculation.

## 2. Governing order and identity

The completed GUA-W remains:

**Author → Word → Creation → Witness**

God alone is Original Cause. GUA-W is the map/blueprint of the One Creation; it is not God and is not Jesus Christ. Jesus must not be reduced to a component, stage, or function inside the architecture.

John 1:1–3 identifies the Word as God and says all things were made by Him. John 1:14 declares that the Word was made flesh. Hebrews 1:1–3 identifies the Son as the One by whom God made the worlds and who upholds all things by the word of His power. Colossians 1:15–17 and 1 Corinthians 15:45–49 must be read in their own contexts alongside these passages.

The Resurrection investigation must preserve the full Scriptural testimony about the Father, the Son, and the Spirit. Scripture attributes the raising of Jesus to God the Father (Acts 2:24, 32; Galatians 1:1), records Jesus' declaration that He has authority to take His life again (John 10:17–18), and speaks of the Spirit in relation to the raising and life of Christ (Romans 8:11; compare 1 Peter 3:18). These statements must be examined canonically, not treated as competing explanations or collapsed into an invented mechanism.

## 4. Jesus declared His death and Resurrection beforehand

The Gospel accounts do not present the Resurrection prediction as an explanation invented after the tomb was found empty.

- **Matthew 16:21:** Jesus begins to show His disciples that He must go to Jerusalem, suffer, be killed, and be raised again the third day.
- **Matthew 17:22–23:** He tells the disciples that He shall be betrayed, killed, and raised again the third day.
- **Matthew 20:18–19:** He describes His condemnation, delivery to the Gentiles, mockery, scourging, crucifixion, and Resurrection on the third day.
- **Mark 8:31:** He teaches that the Son of man must suffer, be killed, and rise after three days.
- **Luke 18:31–33:** He tells the twelve what will happen in Jerusalem, including His death and rising again the third day.
- **John 2:19–22:** Jesus says, “Destroy this temple, and in three days I will raise it up.” John explains that He spoke of the temple of His body; after He was risen from the dead, His disciples remembered and believed the scripture and the word Jesus had said.
- **John 10:17–18:** Jesus says He lays down His life that He might take it again, and that He has power to lay it down and power to take it again.

**TEXT STATES:** Jesus speaks beforehand of His death and Resurrection. John explicitly interprets the temple saying in John 2 as referring to Jesus' body. John 10 records Jesus' own declaration concerning His authority in laying down and taking up His life.

**TEXTUAL OBSERVATION:** The prior declarations are part of the evidence the Gospel writers ask the reader to remember when considering the events after the Crucifixion.

**LIMIT:** A prediction is not itself a description of the physical process by which the predicted event occurs. The Gospels do not narrate the instant of Resurrection as a sequence of observable physical steps.

## 5. Establishing the death and burial

The Resurrection claim must not be detached from the death and burial that precede it.

The Passion accounts record Jesus' condemnation, scourging, crucifixion, death, and burial (Matthew 27:1–66; Mark 15:1–47; Luke 23:1–56; John 18:28–19:42).

Particular details to preserve include:

- The Gospel writers record Jesus being crucified.
- The accounts record His death; John 19:30 records His words, “It is finished,” and says He bowed His head and gave up the ghost.
- John 19:31–37 records the soldiers' actions, the fact that they did not break Jesus' legs because they saw that He was dead, and the piercing of His side. John connects these details with Scripture.
- Joseph of Arimathaea receives permission to take Jesus' body; the accounts record His burial in a tomb (Matthew 27:57–61; Mark 15:42–47; Luke 23:50–56; John 19:38–42).
- Matthew 27:62–66 additionally records the chief priests and Pharisees seeking a guard and the securing of the sepulchre.

**TEXT STATES:** The canonical accounts present Jesus as having died and been buried. The Resurrection proclamation is not presented as an escape from crucifixion before death.

**INVESTIGATION REQUIREMENT:** Historical and medical questions concerning Roman crucifixion, the signs of death, and burial practice require evidence from the appropriate historical and medical disciplines. Such research must be recorded separately from what the Gospel text explicitly says. No modern diagnosis should be retroactively inserted into the narrative as though the text supplied it.

## 6. The empty tomb accounts

The four Gospels each record the discovery that Jesus is no longer in the tomb. Their accounts must be read individually before any combined chronology is proposed.

### Matthew 28:1–10

Matthew records Mary Magdalene and the other Mary coming to see the sepulchre. An angel of the Lord descends, rolls back the stone, and sits upon it. The angel tells the women, “He is not here: for he is risen, as he said.” They are invited to see the place where the Lord lay and are sent to tell His disciples. Jesus then meets them, and they hold Him by the feet and worship Him.

### Mark 16:1–8

Mark names Mary Magdalene, Mary the mother of James, and Salome as those who come to the sepulchre. They find the stone rolled away and encounter a young man in a long white garment, who tells them that Jesus is risen and is not there. He directs them to see the place where they laid Him and to tell the disciples and Peter that Jesus goes before them into Galilee.

**TEXTUAL-CRITICAL NOTE:** The KJV includes Mark 16:9–20. The longer ending has a significant manuscript-history question that must be examined using textual evidence before any argument depends uniquely on those verses. Mark 16:1–8 and the wider canonical testimony must not be confused with the separate question concerning the ending of Mark.

### Luke 24:1–12

Luke records the women coming with prepared spices and finding the stone rolled away. They do not find the body of the Lord Jesus. Two men in shining garments remind them of Jesus' prior words concerning His death and Resurrection. The women report these things to the apostles; their words are initially received as idle tales. Peter runs to the sepulchre, sees the linen clothes laid by themselves, and departs wondering.

### John 20:1–10

John begins with Mary Magdalene coming early, while it is yet dark, and seeing that the stone has been taken away. She runs to Simon Peter and the other disciple. They run to the sepulchre and inspect the linen clothes and the napkin that had been about Jesus' head, folded or wrapped together in a place by itself. The disciples then depart to their own home.

John 20:11–18 continues with Mary remaining at the sepulchre, seeing two angels, and then encountering Jesus, whom she initially does not recognize. Jesus calls her by name; she recognizes Him and is sent to tell the disciples.

### Differences to preserve

- The Gospel writers name and group the women differently.
- The descriptions and number of heavenly messengers differ.
- The narratives preserve different details about the stone, the linen clothes, the women's responses, and the first encounters.
- Matthew records the women taking hold of Jesus' feet; John records Jesus speaking to Mary Magdalene and giving her a message.
- Luke records the apostles' initial response to the women's report and Peter's visit to the tomb.
- John gives particular attention to Peter and the other disciple examining the tomb.

These differences must be investigated rather than erased. A harmonized sequence may be considered only after each account has been accurately represented and the proposed sequence is shown to respect all the texts.

**IMPORTANT DISTINCTION:** An empty tomb and a Resurrection are related claims, but they are not identical statements. The Gospel accounts proceed beyond the empty tomb to encounters with the risen Jesus. The full claim must be examined through the complete testimony, not through the tomb report alone.

## 7. The risen Jesus: identity and embodied testimony

The Gospel accounts do not describe the risen Jesus as merely an idea, memory, or continued influence among His followers.

- **Matthew 28:9–10, 16–20:** Jesus meets the women; later the eleven meet Him in Galilee, worship Him, and receive His commission.
- **Luke 24:13–35:** Jesus joins two disciples on the road to Emmaus, reasons with them from the Scriptures, is recognized in the breaking of bread, and is reported to the others.
- **Luke 24:36–43:** Jesus stands among the disciples, says “Peace be unto you,” invites them to behold His hands and feet, says “a spirit hath not flesh and bones, as ye see me have,” and eats before them.
- **John 20:19–23:** Jesus comes to the disciples when the doors are shut, shows them His hands and side, and speaks peace and sends them.
- **John 20:24–29:** Thomas is absent at the first appearance. Jesus later addresses Thomas, invites him to behold His hands and reach toward His side, and Thomas answers, “My Lord and my God.”
- **John 21:1–14:** Jesus appears to disciples by the sea of Tiberias; the account records the miraculous catch of fishes, their recognition of Jesus, and His provision of bread and fish.
- **Acts 1:3:** Jesus shows Himself alive after His passion by many infallible proofs, being seen of the disciples over forty days and speaking of the things pertaining to the kingdom of God.

**TEXT STATES:** The risen Jesus is encountered, speaks, is recognized, shows the marks associated with His crucifixion, and—in Luke's account—declares that He has flesh and bones and eats in the disciples' presence.

**SUPPORTED INFERENCE:** The canonical witness intends the reader to understand the Resurrection as the raising of Jesus Himself, not merely the survival of His teaching or the persistence of His influence.

**LIMIT:** The texts do not provide a complete modern anatomical description or a scientific account of the risen body's properties. The statements about flesh and bones, eating, wounds, and recognition must be retained together rather than selectively emphasized.

## 8. Paul's testimony and the Resurrection proclamation

### 1 Corinthians 15:3–8

Paul records that Christ:

1. died for our sins according to the scriptures;
2. was buried;
3. rose again the third day according to the scriptures;
4. was seen by Cephas, then of the twelve, then by above five hundred brethren at once, then by James, then by all the apostles, and last of all by Paul.

**TEXT STATES:** Paul's testimony explicitly joins death, burial, Resurrection, and appearances. It names witnesses and connects the event with the Scriptures.

### 1 Corinthians 15:12–28

Paul argues that if there is no resurrection of the dead, then Christ is not risen; if Christ is not risen, apostolic preaching and faith are vain. He then declares, “But now is Christ risen from the dead, and become the firstfruits of them that slept.”

Paul places Christ's Resurrection in relation to Adam, death, resurrection, order, and the final subjection of all things to God.

### 1 Corinthians 15:35–58

Paul addresses the question of how the dead are raised and with what body they come. He distinguishes the body sown from the body raised and contrasts corruption with incorruption, dishonour with glory, weakness with power, and the natural body with the spiritual body.

**TEXTUAL OBSERVATION:** “Spiritual body” must not be silently translated into “immaterial body.” Paul calls it a body and contrasts its condition with the natural body. The meaning of the contrast must be established from the chapter's own argument and relevant canonical context.

**CANONICAL RELATIONSHIP:** Romans 6:9 says Christ being raised from the dead dieth no more; death hath no more dominion over Him. This distinguishes Christ's Resurrection from a return to mortal life that ends in another death.

## 9. Distinguishing Christ's Resurrection from the raising of others

The Gospels record Jesus raising the daughter of Jairus (Matthew 9:18–26; Mark 5:21–43; Luke 8:40–56), the widow's son at Nain (Luke 7:11–17), and Lazarus (John 11:1–44).

These accounts must not be treated as interchangeable with Jesus' own Resurrection.

- The narratives of those raised by Jesus do not describe them as the firstfruits of the final resurrection.
- Romans 6:9 declares that Christ, being raised from the dead, dies no more.
- 1 Corinthians 15:20–23 calls Christ the firstfruits and sets His Resurrection in relation to those who are His.
- 1 Corinthians 15:42–54 describes the resurrection of the dead in terms of incorruption and immortality.

**SUPPORTED DISTINCTION:** The canonical presentation of Christ's Resurrection is not simply that a dead person resumes mortal life. It is the decisive Resurrection of Christ as firstfruits, with death no longer having dominion over Him.

**LIMIT:** This distinction must not be expanded into a detailed physiology that the text does not supply.

# Central Investigation — Pass II: What the Resurrection Claim Actually Asserts

**Scope of this pass:** the Resurrection as a distinct event; the early apostolic proclamation; the identity and transformed embodiment of the risen Jesus; the canonical attribution to Father, Son, and Spirit; and the proper limits of historical and scientific inquiry.

This pass deepens the central investigation. It does not replace the dedicated empty-tomb, appearances, or Ascension studies, and it does not claim to narrate the unobserved instant of Resurrection.

## 16. Define the claim before testing explanations

The New Testament's Resurrection claim is not merely any one of the following:

- that Jesus' influence continued after His death;
- that His disciples remembered Him or experienced His presence;
- that His body was missing from the tomb;
- that a dead person returned temporarily to ordinary mortal life;
- that His followers adopted a new metaphor for hope.

Those may be proposed interpretations or partial descriptions, but none alone states the full canonical claim.

The central New Testament assertion is that **Jesus Christ, who truly died and was buried, was raised from the dead; He was encountered by witnesses; and He now lives beyond death's dominion.**

This formulation follows the combined testimony of the Gospel narratives, Acts, Romans 6:9, and 1 Corinthians 15:3–28. It must be tested from the actual texts, not protected from examination by a slogan.

## 17. The earliest recoverable apostolic proclamation — 1 Corinthians 15:3–8

Paul writes:

“For I delivered unto you first of all that which I also received, how that Christ died for our sins according to the scriptures; And that he was buried, and that he rose again the third day according to the scriptures: And that he was seen of Cephas, then of the twelve…”

He continues with the appearance to above five hundred brethren at once, James, all the apostles, and finally Paul himself.

### What Paul's wording establishes

1. Paul distinguishes what he received from what he delivered to the Corinthian church.
2. The proclamation joins death, burial, Resurrection, and appearances.
3. The formula is presented as prior tradition, not as an idea Paul claims to have originated in this letter.
4. Paul names Cephas, the Twelve, James, the apostles, and himself in the appearance list.
5. Paul adds that many of the five hundred remained alive at the time he wrote, while some had fallen asleep.

### What it does not establish by itself

- Paul does not identify in this paragraph the exact person from whom he first received the formula.
- The text does not give a calendar date for the formula's first composition.
- The phrase “according to the scriptures” does not name one exclusive proof-text.
- The appearance list is not a complete itinerary of every Gospel appearance.
- Paul's statement that many of the five hundred remained alive is an invitation to consider the claim; it is not a surviving transcript of interviews with those people.

### Historical significance

The language “delivered” and “received” is consistent with the transmission of an earlier tradition. James Ware's study of 1 Corinthians 15:3–5 surveys the debate and notes the broad scholarly recognition that the passage preserves tradition predating Paul's letter, while also emphasizing that the precise nature and interpretation of the formula remain debated.

**Finding:** The Resurrection proclamation is early in the life of the Christian movement relative to the composition of 1 Corinthians. The exact date, route of transmission, and full historical value of each named appearance require separate argument.

## 18. Paul as both transmitter and claimed witness

Paul is not only a transmitter of the received proclamation. He also says, “And last of all he was seen of me also, as of one born out of due time” (1 Corinthians 15:8).

In 1 Corinthians 9:1 he asks, “Have I not seen Jesus Christ our Lord?” In Galatians 1:11–17 he describes the revelation of Jesus Christ and his subsequent calling. Acts 9, 22, and 26 narrate his encounter in differing contexts and forms.

These texts must be distinguished:
- 1 Corinthians 15 places Paul's experience in the list of appearances.
- Galatians gives Paul's own account of revelation and calling.
- Acts provides Luke's narrative presentations of the encounter.
- The texts do not describe Paul's encounter in exactly the same narrative form as the earlier Gospel appearances.

**Finding:** Paul claims to have seen the risen Jesus. The precise phenomenology of his encounter is not identical in description to the encounters narrated in Luke 24 and John 20–21. Do not force them into identical circumstances.

## 19. The Father, the Son, and the Spirit in the Resurrection testimony

The New Testament attributes the Resurrection in more than one grammatical and theological form. These passages must be read together without making them compete.

### The Father raises the Son

- Acts 2:24: “Whom God hath raised up, having loosed the pains of death.”
- Acts 2:32: “This Jesus hath God raised up, whereof we all are witnesses.”
- Acts 3:15: Peter says that God raised Jesus from the dead.
- Galatians 1:1: Paul speaks of God the Father, “who raised him from the dead.”

### The Son speaks of His authority

John 10:17–18 records Jesus saying that He lays down His life and has power (authority) to take it again. He says this commandment He received of His Father.

John 2:19–22 records the saying about raising the temple of His body, which John explains in relation to His Resurrection.

These declarations must be retained alongside the apostolic statements that God raised Jesus. The texts do not authorize us to erase either the Father's action or the Son's own declared authority.

### The Spirit and resurrection life

Romans 8:11 says that if the Spirit of Him that raised up Jesus from the dead dwell in believers, He shall also quicken their mortal bodies by His Spirit that dwelleth in them.

The verse directly connects the Spirit with the life-giving power of the God who raised Jesus. Its immediate argument concerns the Spirit's indwelling and the future quickening of believers' mortal bodies.

1 Peter 3:18 is also relevant, but its phrase translated “quickened by the Spirit” has generated grammatical and interpretive debate. It should not be used as though every detail of its syntax were uncontested.

**Finding:** Scripture speaks of God the Father raising Jesus, Jesus declaring authority to take His life again, and the Spirit in relation to resurrection life. These are canonical statements to be held together. This investigation does not invent a mechanical sequence or divide the divine action into competing agents.

## 20. Continuity and transformation of the risen Jesus

The accounts preserve both continuity with the crucified Jesus and a transformed mode of risen life.

### Continuity

- The one who appears is identified as Jesus, not another person.
- Luke 24:39 records His invitation to behold His hands and feet.
- John 20:20 records that He shows the disciples His hands and side.
- John 20:27 records His invitation to Thomas concerning His hands and side.
- Matthew 28:9 records the women holding Him by the feet.
- Luke 24:41–43 records Him eating before the disciples.

These details connect the risen Jesus with the one who was crucified and buried.

### Transformation

- Romans 6:9 says Christ being raised from the dead dieth no more; death hath no more dominion over Him.
- 1 Corinthians 15:42–54 describes resurrection in terms of incorruption, glory, power, and immortality.
- John 20:19 records Jesus standing among the disciples when the doors were shut.
- Luke 24:31 records that He vanished out of the sight of the Emmaus disciples.

The narratives report these features; they do not give a physics lesson explaining how they occur. The closed doors and vanishing are not permission to invent a theory of matter, spatial passage, or bodily permeability.

### The boundary

Continuity does not mean the risen Jesus simply resumed mortal life as before. Transformation does not mean that the risen Jesus was an immaterial apparition. Luke's flesh-and-bones statement and eating scene must be read alongside Paul's teaching about the transformed resurrection body.

**Finding:** The canonical witness presents the risen Jesus as the same Jesus who was crucified, now living in a transformed condition in which death no longer has dominion over Him.

## 21. “A spiritual body” — 1 Corinthians 15:35–54

Paul asks what body the dead come with and answers through a series of contrasts:

- corruption / incorruption;
- dishonour / glory;
- weakness / power;
- natural body / spiritual body.

The Greek expression *sōma pneumatikon* is literally “spiritual body.” The adjective “spiritual” should not be automatically treated as meaning “made out of nonphysical spirit.” In Paul's argument, the contrast is between the present body characterized by the natural life of Adam and the resurrection body characterized by the life-giving Spirit and the last Adam.

However, the precise account of the body's material constitution remains debated among scholars. Some interpretations emphasize transformed flesh; others emphasize the radical transformation and heavenly mode of existence. The text itself insists on a body and on transformation; it does not provide a modern material specification.

The phrase “flesh and blood cannot inherit the kingdom of God” (1 Corinthians 15:50) appears in an argument that immediately explains the need for transformation: “this corruptible must put on incorruption, and this mortal must put on immortality” (15:53).

**Textual boundary:** Do not quote verse 50 in isolation to conclude that Paul denies bodily resurrection. Equally, do not use Luke's flesh-and-bones language to erase Paul's insistence that the resurrection body is transformed and incorruptible.

## 22. Christ the firstfruits — the order of resurrection

1 Corinthians 15:20 declares:

“But now is Christ risen from the dead, and become the firstfruits of them that slept.”

Paul develops this in verses 21–28:
- by man came death;
- by man came also the resurrection of the dead;
- in Adam all die;
- in Christ shall all be made alive;
- every man in his own order;
- Christ the firstfruits;
- afterward they that are Christ's at His coming;
- then the end, when the kingdom is delivered to God, even the Father.

“Firstfruits” is not merely a statement that Jesus happened to be the first person ever restored from death. The Gospels already record others being raised. Paul's argument concerns the order and guarantee of the eschatological resurrection: Christ's Resurrection is firstfruits of the resurrection harvest to come.

Colossians 1:18 calls Christ “the beginning, the firstborn from the dead; that in all things he might have the preeminence.” Revelation 1:5 calls Him “the first begotten of the dead.” These passages reinforce His unique primacy, while their immediate contexts must remain intact.

**Finding:** Christ's Resurrection is presented as the decisive beginning and guarantee of the resurrection of those who belong to Him, not as one isolated return to mortal life.

## 23. Historical inquiry: what can be assessed?

Historical inquiry cannot rerun the first century. It can, however, assess surviving sources, their date and transmission, their literary relationships, the social setting, and the claims they preserve.

The following questions are historically assessable:
1. Do the sources report Jesus' execution under Pontius Pilate?
2. Do early Christian sources proclaim that Jesus was raised and appeared?
3. How early can particular traditions be placed relative to Paul's letters and the Gospel narratives?
4. What do the sources say about burial and the tomb?
5. What can be established about the development and spread of Resurrection belief?
6. How do the sources differ, and what explanations best account for those differences?

### External evidence for the execution

Tacitus, *Annals* 15.44, writing in the early second century, associates Christus with execution under Pontius Pilate during Tiberius' reign. The *Survey of the Historical Jesus* entry on the trial and death of Jesus notes this brief Roman reference while also observing that it adds little detail beyond the Gospel tradition. The source is relevant to the historical setting of Jesus' execution, not an independent witness to the Resurrection.

Josephus, *Antiquities* 18.3.3, contains the well-known Testimonium Flavianum, whose received wording has been extensively debated because of apparent Christian interpolation. The passage cannot responsibly be quoted as though every phrase were certainly Josephus' original wording. Josephus' reference to James in *Antiquities* 20.9.1 is often considered separately and identifies James in relation to “Jesus who was called Christ.” Neither passage is a direct eyewitness report of the Resurrection.

### Early Christian testimony

1 Corinthians 15:3–8 is particularly important because Paul explicitly says he received and delivered the tradition. James Ware's peer-reviewed study in *New Testament Studies* examines the pre-Pauline formula and the continuing debate over what its wording implies about the nature of the Resurrection. The existence of early tradition is not the same question as whether every historical conclusion drawn from it is warranted.

### Historical boundary

A historian can establish that early followers proclaimed that Jesus had been raised and that named individuals and groups were said to have seen Him. A historian can assess the sources' proximity, genre, transmission, and explanatory adequacy. The further judgment that God raised Jesus is a claim about divine action; historians differ about whether and how historical method can adjudicate such a claim.

This distinction must not be turned into either of two errors:
- **Naturalistic overreach:** declaring in advance that divine action is impossible and then treating that philosophical premise as a historical finding.
- **Apologetic overreach:** declaring that the existence of early testimony, by itself, proves every element of a preferred harmonization or supplies a scientific mechanism.

The historical evidence must be described at the level it actually supports.

## 24. Scientific inquiry: scope and limits

The Resurrection is a singular past event, not a repeatable biological procedure available for laboratory replication. Scientific disciplines can clarify relevant background:
- Roman crucifixion and the physiological consequences of severe trauma;
- death, resuscitation, and the limits of ordinary biological recovery;
- ancient burial practices and tomb construction;
- the preservation, transmission, and dating of documentary evidence;
- the physical constraints relevant to proposed natural explanations.

But no laboratory experiment can reproduce the unique event as narrated, and no experiment can directly observe a first-century divine act.

This does not mean science is irrelevant. It means scientific conclusions must be confined to questions that scientific evidence and methods can address. A proposed natural explanation must be assessed on evidence; an absence of a known mechanism is not itself a scientific demonstration of impossibility. Conversely, a theological assertion cannot be presented as a laboratory result.

**Scientific disposition:** The Resurrection claim is not a mechanism identified by current biology or physics. Its assessment requires historical reasoning and theological interpretation in addition to relevant scientific background. No physical mechanism is supplied or invented in this investigation.

# Central Investigation — Pass III: The Resurrection in the Light of Genesis 1–2

**Scope:** Examine the canonical relationship among Genesis 1–2, the Word in John 1, the Passion and Resurrection in John 19–20, apostolic resurrection teaching, and the promised renewal of Creation. Distinguish direct Scriptural statements from literary allusions and theological synthesis.

## 29. The governing question

**What relationship does Scripture establish between the Creation account in Genesis 1–2 and the Resurrection of Jesus Christ?**

This question must not be answered by importing a preselected architecture into the text. We begin with the passages themselves.

The investigation distinguishes four levels:

1. **Explicit statement:** what a passage directly says.
2. **Literary correspondence or allusion:** language, imagery, sequence, or setting that plausibly recalls another passage.
3. **Canonical relationship:** how separate Scriptural statements illuminate one another when read together.
4. **Unresolved interpretation:** what the texts do not settle or what remains debated.

The phrase “new creation” is explicit in Paul (2 Corinthians 5:17; Galatians 6:15), but does not occur as that exact phrase in John's Gospel. A Johannine new-creation reading must therefore be demonstrated from John's language and narrative, not falsely presented as a direct quotation.

## 30. Genesis 1:1 and John 1:1 — the beginning and the Word

Genesis 1:1 (KJV):

“In the beginning God created the heaven and the earth.”

John 1:1 (KJV):

“In the beginning was the Word, and the Word was with God, and the Word was God.”

John 1:3 continues:

“All things were made by him; and without him was not any thing made that was made.”

John deliberately opens with the language “In the beginning,” placing the identity and work of the Word in relation to the beginning of Creation. John does not identify the Word as a created thing. The Word already “was”; all created things came into being through Him.

John 1:14 then declares:

“And the Word was made flesh, and dwelt among us…”

### Findings

- Genesis identifies God as Creator.
- John identifies the Word as with God and as God.
- John explicitly attributes the making of all created things to the Word.
- John explicitly identifies the Word as made flesh in Jesus Christ.

The relationship is not merely a verbal similarity. John 1:1–3 deliberately places the Word in the account of the beginning and identifies His relation to Creation. The full theological account of the relation between Father and Son must be read from the whole canon; John 1 must not be made to say less or more than it says.

**GUA-W relevance:** This is a direct canonical foundation for examining Creation and Christ together. It does not make GUA-W the Word, make the architecture divine, or make the investigator an author.

## 31. Genesis 2:1–3 and John 19:30 — completion and “It is finished”

Genesis 2:1 (KJV):

“Thus the heavens and the earth were finished, and all the host of them.”

Genesis 2:2–3 records God's finishing of His work, resting on the seventh day, and blessing and sanctifying that day.

John 19:30 records Jesus' final declaration:

“It is finished: and he bowed his head, and gave up the ghost.”

The verbal correspondence between “finished” in Genesis 2 and “It is finished” in John 19 is worth investigating. John also frames Jesus' death within a narrative rich in Scriptural fulfilment. However, the KJV's English wording alone cannot establish that John intends a direct quotation of Genesis 2:1. The Greek and Hebrew expressions, their contexts, and the broader narrative must be considered.

A further distinction is essential:

- Genesis 2:1–3 explicitly declares the completion of God's Creation work and His rest.
- John 19:30 explicitly declares the completion of the work Jesus has been given in the immediate Passion context (compare John 17:4).
- John 19 does not explicitly say, in that verse, “Genesis 2 is now being repeated.”

The canonical relationship may be theologically significant without being a one-to-one identity of events.

**Finding:** Completion is explicitly present in both passages, but the claim that John 19:30 intentionally echoes Genesis 2:1–3 is a literary-theological interpretation requiring cumulative evidence. It is not to be promoted to an explicit quotation.

## 32. The garden setting in John 18–20

John records a garden in the arrest narrative (John 18:1) and says that the place where Jesus was crucified contained a garden, with a new sepulchre in it (John 19:41). Mary Magdalene encounters the risen Jesus in John 20:1–18. She supposes Him to be the gardener (20:15).

Genesis 2–3 is centrally concerned with a garden, humanity, life, death, and the consequences of disobedience. John's garden setting can therefore evoke the Garden of Eden and the story of humanity's beginning.

But care is needed:

- John does not explicitly call the burial garden “Eden.”
- Mary supposes Jesus to be the gardener; John does not explicitly say she has correctly identified Him as a literal gardener.
- The garden correspondence is a literary and theological reading, not an additional historical location supplied by Genesis.

The cumulative setting is meaningful: arrest, crucifixion, burial, and the first narrated encounter with the risen Jesus are framed by garden imagery.

**Finding:** The garden motif is a substantial candidate for a Genesis 2–3 allusion in John's Passion and Resurrection narrative. It should be identified as an allusion, not as an explicit equation.

## 33. The first day of the week — John 20:1

John 20:1 begins:

“The first day of the week cometh Mary Magdalene early, when it was yet dark, unto the sepulchre…”

Genesis 1 repeatedly marks the days of Creation, beginning with the first day. The Resurrection occurring on the first day of the week has therefore been read by many interpreters as a new-creation signal.

The textual facts:
- John explicitly says it was the first day of the week.
- Genesis 1 explicitly structures the beginning of Creation through a sequence of days.
- John does not explicitly say, “This is the first day of a second creation week.”

The first-day connection is a plausible and influential canonical reading, but the theological conclusion must not be confused with the explicit wording.

The detail “when it was yet dark” is also part of John's account. John 1:4–5 has already associated life and light with the Word and darkness with the condition that does not overcome the light. Whether the darkness in John 20:1 is intended to carry the full symbolic weight of John 1 is a further interpretive question; it cannot be assumed from the word alone.

## 34. Mary Magdalene, recognition, and the risen Jesus

John 20:11–18 records Mary weeping at the sepulchre, seeing two angels, turning and seeing Jesus, but not initially recognizing Him. She supposes Him to be the gardener. Jesus calls her by name; she recognizes Him and answers, “Rabboni.”

The passage explicitly reports:
- Mary remains at the tomb and weeps.
- She sees Jesus but does not initially recognize Him.
- Jesus speaks her name.
- She recognizes Him.
- Jesus commissions her to go to His brethren with His message.

The narrative does not say that Jesus' body was merely a symbol or that Mary experienced only an inward idea. John identifies the one speaking with her as Jesus and records the encounter as an appearance of the risen Christ.

The recognition sequence is important, but it does not establish a universal rule about how every witness must recognize the risen Jesus. Luke 24:13–35 describes the Emmaus disciples' delayed recognition in a different narrative. The accounts should be compared, not flattened.

## 35. Genesis 2:7 and John 20:22 — breath and life

Genesis 2:7 (KJV):

“And the LORD God formed man of the dust of the ground, and breathed into his nostrils the breath of life; and man became a living soul.”

John 20:22:

“And when he had said this, he breathed on them, and saith unto them, Receive ye the Holy Ghost.”

The correspondence between divine breath in Genesis 2:7 and Jesus breathing on the disciples in John 20:22 is a significant interpretive question. The Greek verb in John 20:22, *emphysaō*, is rare in the New Testament and is widely recognized as capable of recalling the Greek Old Testament's language of God's breathing life into Adam in Genesis 2:7.

But the two passages are not identical events:
- Genesis 2:7 narrates God's formation of Adam and the imparting of the breath of life.
- John 20:22 narrates the risen Jesus breathing on His disciples and commanding them to receive the Holy Ghost.
- John 20:22 does not say that the disciples are newly created as Adam was, nor does it say that they become the first human beings.

The wider Johannine context includes Jesus' promise of the Comforter (John 14–16), His prayer for His disciples (John 17), His death and Resurrection, and His commissioning of them (John 20:21–23).

**Finding:** A deliberate Genesis 2:7 allusion is strongly plausible and widely discussed. Its immediate function in John concerns the gift of the Holy Ghost and the commissioning of the disciples. It must not be reduced to a biological re-creation or used to invent a new creation mechanism.

## 36. The risen Christ and the firstfruits of 1 Corinthians 15

Paul explicitly calls Christ “the firstfruits of them that slept” (1 Corinthians 15:20). He then contrasts Adam and Christ:

“For since by man came death, by man came also the resurrection of the dead.” (15:21)

And:

“For as in Adam all die, even so in Christ shall all be made alive.” (15:22)

Paul continues with the order: Christ the firstfruits; afterward those who are Christ's at His coming (15:23).

This is not merely a literary resemblance. Paul explicitly makes Adam and Christ central to his account of death and resurrection.

The passage establishes:
- Adam and Christ occupy contrasting representative positions in Paul's argument.
- Death is associated with Adam.
- Resurrection life is associated with Christ.
- Christ's Resurrection is firstfruits, the beginning of the harvest to come.
- The resurrection of those who belong to Christ follows in the order Paul describes.

The exact scope and interpretation of “all” in verses 22–23 must be read within Paul's argument, not detached from the distinctions he makes in the chapter.

**Finding:** The Adam–Christ relationship is an explicit Pauline theological argument. Christ's Resurrection is the firstfruits of the coming resurrection harvest.

## 37. Romans 8:18–23 — Creation's groaning and future liberation

Romans 8:19–22 speaks of the earnest expectation of the creature, the creation being made subject to vanity, its bondage of corruption, and its groaning and travailing together until now.

Romans 8:23 then speaks of believers who have the firstfruits of the Spirit and groan within themselves, waiting for the adoption, “to wit, the redemption of our body.”

This passage must be handled with precision.

Paul explicitly connects:
- Creation's present subjection and groaning;
- hope of liberation from the bondage of corruption;
- the manifestation of the sons of God;
- the redemption of the body;
- the firstfruits of the Spirit.

The passage does not say that every physical law changed at the Fall, nor does it provide a scientific chronology of how the natural world changed. The precise referent of *ktisis* (“creation” or “creature”) and the scope of its subjection have generated scholarly debate.

Within the canonical argument, however, the Resurrection is not an escape from the significance of Creation. It is bound to the promised redemption of embodied humanity and the liberation of Creation.

**Finding:** Romans 8 gives explicit apostolic grounds for connecting resurrection hope with the future liberation of Creation. It does not authorize an unsupported catalogue of altered physical laws.

## 38. Hebrews 1:1–3 — the Son and the upholding of all things

Hebrews 1:2–3 declares that God made the worlds by His Son and that the Son is:

“the brightness of his glory, and the express image of his person, and upholding all things by the word of his power…”

This passage directly identifies the Son's relation to Creation and its continuing sustentation. It also says that after making purification for sins, He sat down on the right hand of the Majesty on high.

The passage brings together:
- God's speaking;
- the Son as the one through whom the worlds were made;
- the Son's sustaining of all things;
- purification for sins;
- the Son's exalted position.

Hebrews 1 does not provide a scientific model of the forces that sustain the universe. It makes a theological declaration about the Son. Physical theories may describe regularities within Creation; they do not replace the passage's claim about the Son.

**Finding:** The risen and exalted Christ is not presented as unrelated to the original Creation. The same Son through whom God made the worlds is declared to uphold all things.

## 39. Revelation 21:1–6 — the consummation

Revelation 21 records a new heaven and a new earth, the holy city, the removal of death, sorrow, crying, and pain, and the declaration:

“Behold, I make all things new.” (Revelation 21:5)

The One sitting upon the throne says, “It is done” (21:6).

This passage is not a description of the instant of Jesus' Resurrection. It presents the consummation of God's purpose in the prophetic vision of Revelation.

The canonical relationship can be stated carefully:
- Genesis begins with God's Creation.
- The Gospels proclaim the death and Resurrection of Jesus.
- Paul identifies Christ as firstfruits and describes the future resurrection and liberation of Creation.
- Revelation presents the final removal of death and the making of all things new.

This is a canonical trajectory, not proof that every detail of Revelation 21 is already fully realized at the empty tomb.

The repeated language of completion (“It is finished” in John 19:30; “It is done” in Revelation 21:6) merits comparison. It does not, by itself, prove that the passages use an identical formula or describe the same moment.

## 46. Integrated canonical relationship — the sign, the Resurrection, and the witnesses (2026-11-18)

This section connects Investigation II's miracle records with the central Resurrection study and the Creator-authority / Human Will findings. It does not create a new Resurrection claim or replace the detailed work in the preceding sections.

### A. The distinction between a sign and the event to which it points

Cana (John 2:1–11) records Jesus' first sign in John's Gospel and the narrator's statement that it manifested His glory. The servants' obedience, the governor's tasting, and the disciples' belief are distinct witness activities. The sign is attributed to Jesus, not to the human participants.

The raising of Lazarus (John 11) is a powerful sign immediately preceding the Passion. Jesus identifies Himself as the resurrection and the life and calls Lazarus forth. The event points to His identity and the Father's sending of Him. Yet Lazarus is restored to life within the mortal human story; this event is not identical to Jesus' own Resurrection as firstfruits, which Paul treats as the ground and beginning of the resurrection order (1 Corinthians 15:20–23).

The Resurrection of Jesus must therefore retain its unique identity and canonical significance. It is not merely the largest item in a list of miracles, nor a repetition of the raising of Lazarus.

### B. The Father, the Son, and the Spirit — preserve the attributed actions

The central Resurrection study records the Father's raising of the Son, the Son's own statements concerning His authority over His life (John 10:17–18), and the Spirit's relation to resurrection life (for example Romans 8:11). Hebrews 1:1–3 identifies the Son as the One through whom the worlds were made and the One upholding all things. These statements must be held together without confusing the persons or assigning one text's grammatical subject to another passage.

The related Investigation III.2 examines John 20:19–23, Luke 24, Acts 1–2, and the promised Spirit. Its bounded conclusion is that the risen Son breathes upon the disciples and commands them to receive the Holy Ghost in John's account; Luke and Acts preserve the commission, the promise, the forty-day interval, and the public outpouring at Pentecost. The exact relation of John 20:22 to Acts 2 in extent, timing, and function remains unresolved. The disciples receive, are commissioned, and bear witness; they do not originate the Resurrection or the Spirit.

### C. The witness is real but derivative

The Gospel witnesses see, hear, touch, report, remember, and proclaim. Their testimony is indispensable to the apostolic proclamation, but witness is not authorship. This is consistent with John 20:30–31, where the signs are written so readers may believe that Jesus is the Christ, the Son of God, and have life through His name; and with 1 Corinthians 15:3–8, where Paul transmits the received proclamation and names witnesses to the risen Christ.

The Creator-authority finding sharpens the boundary: a creature's ability to observe, explain, report, or interpret does not confer authority to define or originate the event. Conversely, identifying Christ as the source and authority does not make the witnesses' actual seeing, hearing, believing, or reporting fictitious.

### D. Integrated finding

The miracle records and Resurrection testimony belong to one canonical account, but retain their distinctions:

1. Jesus' signs bear witness to His identity and authority.
2. The raising of Lazarus points to Jesus as the resurrection and the life but is not Christ's own Resurrection.
3. The Father raises the Son; the Son's authority and identity remain explicit in the Gospel testimony; the Spirit's role is stated in its own passages.
4. The risen Christ commissions witnesses; the Spirit empowers witness; the apostles proclaim what they received and saw.
5. The witnesses do not become the Author, cause, or authority of the work to which they testify.

**The sign points to Christ. Christ is raised. The witnesses proclaim Him. The testimony does not become the event's originating cause.**

### E. Triple Constraint and limits

- **Biblical compliance — PASS within scope:** the distinctions among Jesus' signs, Lazarus' raising, Christ's Resurrection, the Father's action, the Son's authority, the Spirit's work, and apostolic witness are preserved.
- **Scientific coherence — PASS within scope:** no physical mechanism is invented for the signs, Resurrection, or Spirit's work. Historical assessment remains distinct from theological attribution.
- **GUA-W consistency — PASS at the governing level:** Author, Word, Creation, and witness retain their proper distinctions. No new layer, Gear, or technical mechanism is established.
