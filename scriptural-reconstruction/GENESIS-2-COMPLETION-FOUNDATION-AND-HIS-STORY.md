# Genesis 2 — Completion as the Foundation of His Story

**Status:** Textual foundation and theological synthesis; architectural implications remain proposals  
**Scope:** KJV Genesis 2:1–17, with canonical comparison to John 19:30 and Revelation 21:6

## Purpose

This investigation examines what Genesis 2 establishes before the events of Genesis 3: the completed Creation, man's created position, appointed purpose, and the command that defines permission and boundary. It also records the theological correspondence identified as **the three completions**.

The expression **His Story** names the theological understanding of Scripture as the unfolding of the Author's purpose. The comparison below is a theological pattern, not a claim that three passages use one identical word.

## 1. The first completion: Genesis 2:1–3

> Thus the heavens and the earth were finished, and all the host of them. (Genesis 2:1, KJV)

> And on the seventh day God ended his work which he had made; and he rested on the seventh day from all his work which he had made. (Genesis 2:2, KJV)

> And God blessed the seventh day, and sanctified it: because that in it he had rested from all his work which God created and made. (Genesis 2:3, KJV)

### Textual observations

- The heavens, earth, and all their host are declared finished.
- God is the subject who ends/finishes His work.
- God rests/ceases from that work.
- God blesses and sanctifies the seventh day.

The Hebrew root כָּלָה (*kālāh*) appears in 2:1 and 2:2: the heavens and earth “were finished,” and God “ended” His work. Verse 2 also uses שָׁבַת (*šāḇaṯ*) for resting/ceasing. Verse 3 adds בָּרַךְ (*bāraḵ*, bless) and קָדַשׁ (*qādaš*, sanctify/make holy).

These are related statements, but they are not one undifferentiated action. Finishing, ceasing, blessing, and sanctifying are separately expressed.

### Foundation established

The passage identifies God as the One who creates and completes the work. The created order is the result of His work; it is not presented as the originating authority over that work.

This establishes the starting hierarchy for the investigation:

**Author → Word → Creation → Witness**

The text directly declares completion and divine action. The further architectural implication—that later events must be understood within the order established by the Creator—is a theological synthesis grounded in the sequence, not a dictionary definition of *kālāh*.

## 2. Genesis 2 establishes man's place, purpose, permission, and boundary

Genesis 2 continues beyond the declaration of completion. It records the formation and placement of man, his appointed work, and God's command.

| Passage | Textual content |
|---|---|
| Genesis 2:7 | The LORD God forms man from the dust, breathes into his nostrils the breath of life, and man becomes a living soul. |
| Genesis 2:8–14 | The LORD God plants the Garden, places man there, and the account describes the Garden and its rivers. |
| Genesis 2:15 | The man is placed in the Garden to dress/work it and to keep/guard it. |
| Genesis 2:16 | The LORD God commands the man and grants broad permission to eat from the trees of the Garden. |
| Genesis 2:17 | God states a specific prohibition and its consequence. |
| Genesis 2:18–25 | God provides the woman, brings her to the man, and the account describes their joining and original condition. |

### 2.1 Purpose is present before the Fall

Genesis 2:15 gives the purpose of the man's placement: to work/cultivate the Garden and to keep/guard it. The Hebrew verbs are עָבַד (*ʿāḇaḏ*) and שָׁמַר (*šāmar*).

Purpose is therefore not an explanation first introduced after the Fall. It is part of the pre-Fall account. The further GUA-W proposal that appointed purpose relates Constraint to Structure may be investigated from this foundation, but it is not a literal translation of Genesis 2:15.

### 2.2 Permission and boundary are given by God

Genesis 2:16–17 identifies:
- **Source of the command:** the LORD God.
- **Recipient:** the man.
- **Permission:** eating freely from the trees of the Garden.
- **Prohibition:** eating of the tree of the knowledge of good and evil.
- **Stated consequence:** “thou shalt surely die.”

The Hebrew emphatic construction אָכֹל תֹּאכֵל (*ʾāḵōl tōʾḵēl*) expresses the broad permission (“freely eat”). מוֹת תָּמוּת (*mōṯ tāmūṯ*) is the emphatic construction rendered “thou shalt surely die.” The latter does not, by its doubled form alone, specify every theological dimension or timing of death.

The command and boundary are established before the serpent's speech in Genesis 3.

### 2.3 The condition of the created environment before Genesis 3

Genesis 1:31 records God's comprehensive evaluation:

> And God saw every thing that he had made, and, behold, it was very good. And the evening and the morning were the sixth day. (KJV)

The Hebrew expression טוֹב מְאֹד (*ṭôḇ məʾōḏ*) is “very good.” Genesis 2 then describes the Garden planted by the LORD God, its trees as pleasant to sight and good for food (2:8–9), the provision of its river (2:10–14), man's appointed place and work (2:15), and the man and woman naked and not ashamed (2:25).

Read together, these passages establish the pre-Fall created condition as **very good, ordered, provided for, and without the shame and curse subsequently narrated**. “Perfect environment” is the theological synthesis used in this investigation for that original condition; it is not presented as a direct English quotation or as the lexical translation of one Hebrew word in Genesis 2.

Genesis 3 records a decisive change:
- The ground is cursed in connection with Adam's disobedience (Genesis 3:17).
- Thorns and thistles are named as part of the changed ground condition (3:18).
- Human food production is associated with toil and sweat (3:17–19).
- Man is told that he will return to the dust (3:19).
- The man and woman are expelled from the Garden, and the way to the tree of life is guarded (3:22–24).

The wider canonical account describes Creation as subjected to vanity and groaning, awaiting deliverance (Romans 8:20–22). This is relevant to the theological account of the Fall's effects upon Creation; it should not be reduced to a claim that every present-day physical process has been experimentally traced to one Genesis event.

**Scientific significance within GUA-W:** The condition of the environment is part of the claim being investigated, not incidental scenery. A pre-Fall environment described as very good and a post-Fall condition described through curse, toil, thorns, mortality, and exclusion are distinct states in the Scriptural account. Theology investigates that distinction from the revealed text. Physical and life sciences may investigate the present created order and its observable processes; their findings must not be silently substituted for the theological account, nor should theological conclusions be misrepresented as laboratory measurements.

## 3. Capacity to evaluate is distinct from authority to originate the standard

This distinction is central to reading Genesis 3.

- **Perception:** seeing or receiving information.
- **Knowledge:** knowing or becoming aware.
- **Evaluation:** regarding something as good, desirable, harmful, or wise.
- **Desire:** inclination toward what is perceived or evaluated.
- **Action:** taking or doing.
- **Authority:** the source or right by which a governing standard or command is established.

A creature's capacity to perceive, know, evaluate, desire, and act does not by itself make that creature the originating authority for the standard by which good and evil are defined.

Genesis 2:16–17 supplies the narrative structure: God commands; man receives the command; God states permission and prohibition. Genesis 2 does not narrate a delegation to man of authority to originate or independently redefine that standard.

**Text/inference distinction:** Genesis 2 does not contain the exact abstract sentence “man does not possess independent authority to define good and evil.” That is a theological inference from the stated source, recipient, permission, and prohibition. It should not be presented as a direct quotation. The narrative foundation for the inference is already present before Genesis 3.

## 4. Genesis 3 is read against the order already established

Genesis 3 does not introduce the first command or the first boundary. Those are already present in Genesis 2:16–17.

The narrative sequence in Genesis 3 can therefore be examined in this order:

1. **Prior word:** God has spoken (Genesis 2:16–17).
2. **Competing account:** the serpent questions and reframes God's word (Genesis 3:1–5).
3. **Perception and evaluation:** the woman sees the tree in terms of food, sight, and wisdom (Genesis 3:6).
4. **Desire and action:** she takes and eats, and gives to her husband, who also eats (Genesis 3:6).
5. **Changed awareness:** their eyes are opened and they know that they are naked (Genesis 3:7).
6. **Confrontation and accountability:** God questions them and addresses what has happened (Genesis 3:8–19).
7. **Changed condition and exclusion:** they are sent from the Garden and access to the tree of life is guarded (Genesis 3:22–24).

Perception and evaluation are not intrinsically evil. The question is whether human evaluation and action remain faithful to the already-given word or instead proceed on the basis of the serpent's competing account.

Genesis 3:22 acknowledges a changed human condition involving knowledge of good and evil. It does not state that man became the Author, that God's authority was transferred, or that man became the independent source of the standard. Knowledge and originating authority remain distinct.

## 5. The three completions — Creation, Redemption, Consummation

The three passages form a theological correspondence within His Story. They do not use the same original-language word and do not describe identical events.

### 5.1 Creation — Genesis 2:1–3

The heavens, earth, and all their host are declared finished. God ends His work, ceases from it, blesses and sanctifies the seventh day.

Hebrew completion language includes כָּלָה (*kālāh*); cessation is expressed by שָׁבַת (*šāḇaṯ*).

### 5.2 Redemption — John 19:30

> When Jesus therefore had received the vinegar, he said, It is finished: and he bowed his head, and gave up the ghost. (John 19:30, KJV)

The Greek τετέλεσται (*tetelestai*), from τελέω (*teleō*), is rendered “It is finished.” The declaration occurs in the account of Jesus' crucifixion.

### 5.3 Consummation — Revelation 21:6

> And he said unto me, It is done. I am Alpha and Omega, the beginning and the end. (Revelation 21:6, KJV)

The Greek form γέγοναν (*gegonan*) in the critical text is from γίνομαι (*ginomai*) and conveys that the things have come to pass or are accomplished. The declaration appears in the vision of the new heaven and new earth and the holy city (Revelation 21:1–6).

Revelation 16:17 also contains the KJV wording “It is done.” Revelation 21:6 is identified here specifically with the consummation/new-creation vision, not as the only occurrence of that English wording in Revelation.

### 5.4 The proposed correspondence

**Creation → Redemption → Consummation**

- Genesis 2: the created work is declared finished.
- John 19: the Son declares “It is finished” at the Cross.
- Revelation 21: the final order is announced with “It is done.”

This is a canonical and theological comparison. It does not claim that the three passages repeat one identical phrase, use one identical word, or describe one identical kind of completion.

## 6. Purpose before the Fall and the purpose of redemption

Genesis 2:15 records man's assigned work before the temptation. The wider canon also places Christ's redemptive purpose before its manifestation in time:

- **1 Peter 1:19–20:** Christ is described as foreordained before the foundation of the world and manifested in the last times.
- **Ephesians 1:4:** God is said to have chosen us in Christ before the foundation of the world.
- **Revelation 17:8:** speaks of names not written in the book of life from the foundation of the world.
- **Revelation 13:8:** has a grammatical attachment ambiguity concerning “from the foundation of the world”; the phrase may be read with the Lamb's being slain or with the names not written. It should not be used as unqualified proof of either grammatical attachment.

These passages warrant a wider investigation of God's purpose before the foundation of the world. Their individual wording and context must remain distinct.

- **U-007 — RESOLVED:** Knowledge of good and evil does not confer God's authority or transfer authority to establish the standard. ## 8. Triple Constraint review

The Triple Constraint is applied to theological claims as well as claims arising in the physical, biological, mathematical, and other sciences. Within GUA-W, **Theology is a Science**: it is a disciplined investigation of God, Creation, and revealed truth, with Scripture as its primary authority and with careful attention to language, context, inference, and coherence. Its method and evidence are not identical to those of experimental physics or biology; that difference does not make theological inquiry “not scientific.”

Scientific coherence therefore applies to the claims made here. The theological investigation makes claims about the original created condition, the order and purpose of Creation, and the change narrated in Genesis 3. Those are not claims outside the scope of the Triple Constraint merely because their primary evidence is Scripture. They must be examined through disciplined theological method, textual and linguistic analysis, canonical coherence, and careful distinction between what is stated and what is inferred. Where the investigation also makes claims about physical, biological, ecological, or other created processes, those claims must additionally be examined against the relevant observations and methods of those sciences.

The distinction is one of evidence, method, and source—not whether a scientific claim exists. Scripture provides the foundational account of the original Creation and the condition before the Fall. The other sciences investigate aspects of the created order and offer observations, models, and explanations that can be examined in relation to that account. The purpose is not to experimentally reconstruct the pre-Fall environment. Scripture shows the original condition; scientific findings are considered as witnesses to Creation and to the changes under investigation. Claims about particular physical, biological, ecological, or other processes must still be assessed using the evidence and methods appropriate to those sciences.

## 9. Working conclusion

Genesis 2 is not merely background to the Fall. It establishes the completed Creation and the Creator's prior ordering of man's place, purpose, permission, and boundary. Genesis 3 must be interpreted against that foundation.

The three completions offer a meaningful theological sequence within **His Story**: Creation, Redemption, and Consummation. The language and events differ, and the comparison preserves those differences.

Genesis 2 does not explicitly narrate every later event or state every later theological formulation. It does establish the foundational order from which the later account is read. The Fall does not become a new source of originating authority; it is narrated as a departure within an order already established by the Creator.

This document makes substantive theological claims about Creation's original condition and the change narrated in Genesis 3; within GUA-W, these are scientific claims in the theological discipline and are subject to the Triple Constraint. It does not yet establish a detailed physical mechanism for every environmental change, quantify the full scope of those changes, or establish a universal runtime or new canonical GUA-W node. Those further claims remain open to disciplined investigation and the repository's admissibility rules.

## 9. The three completions in relation to the first Adam, the promised Seed, and the last Adam

The three-completion comparison is not three isolated statements. Read in the order of Scripture, it is a connected witness across Creation, the Fall, redemption in Christ, and the consummation.

### 9.1 First completion — Creation and the first Adam

Genesis 2:1–3 declares the heavens, earth, and all their host finished; God ends His work, rests, blesses, and sanctifies the seventh day. Genesis 2:7 records the LORD God forming Adam from the dust and breathing into him the breath of life. Genesis 2:15–17 records man's placement, appointed work, permission, command, and boundary.

The first Adam is a creature within the completed Creation. He is not its author. His life is received; his place and work are given; the command is spoken by God. Genesis 3 then records the transgression and its consequences.

**Boundary:** The first completion is the completion of the created work described in Genesis 2:1–3. It must not be confused with the later completion of redemption or the final consummation. Nor should Genesis 2:1–5 be made to say that Adam was already formed in the order of verses 1–5; man's formation is narrated in 2:7.

### 9.2 The promise within the Fall narrative — Genesis 3:15

After the transgression, God declares, “I will put enmity” between the serpent and the woman and between their respective seed/offspring. The promise is God's declaration, not a humanly authored recovery plan.

Genesis 3:15 itself does not name Jesus or give the full chronology of the victory. The New Testament supplies the further witness: Revelation 12:9 identifies the dragon as the old serpent, the Devil, and Satan; Romans 16:20 speaks of God bruising Satan; Hebrews 2:14–15 and 1 John 3:8 connect Christ's coming and work with defeating the devil and destroying his works.

This is the promised conflict and victory carried forward within the history that follows the Fall. It is not a fourth completion in the three-completion pattern.

### 9.3 Second completion — Redemption accomplished by Christ

John 19:28–30 must be read as a unit. Jesus knows that all things are now accomplished, fulfills the Scripture concerning the vinegar, receives it, and declares, “It is finished” before giving up the ghost. John 17:4 also records Jesus speaking to the Father of having glorified Him on earth and finished the work given Him to do.

The New Testament identifies Christ as the last Adam (1 Corinthians 15:45) and contrasts the first man Adam with Christ, the second man (15:47). Romans 5:12–21 develops the parallel and contrast between the one man's disobedience and the obedience of Jesus Christ, and between condemnation and the gift of grace/righteousness.

**Finding:** The Cross is not a second creation event and does not mean the first Creation was unfinished. It is the distinct redemptive completion accomplished by Christ. The first Adam's disobedience and the last Adam's obedience belong to the canonical explanation of why this redemption is necessary and what Christ accomplishes.

**Boundary:** “It is finished” in John 19:30 is Jesus' declaration in the immediate context of His death. The scope of the finished work should be interpreted from John's Gospel and the wider New Testament, not reduced to a slogan or used to erase the resurrection, ascension, present intercession, or future consummation that Scripture also teaches.

### 9.4 Third completion — Consummation in the new Creation

Revelation 21:1–6 presents the new heaven and new earth, the holy city, God dwelling with His people, the end of death, sorrow, crying, and pain, and the declaration: “It is done. I am Alpha and Omega, the beginning and the end” (21:6).

Revelation 22:1–5 continues with the river of the water of life, the tree of life, the removal of the curse, and the servants of God seeing His face and reigning. These details deliberately bring the reader back to themes from Genesis: the tree of life, the curse, God's presence, and human life under God's order.

**Finding:** Revelation's consummation is not merely a return to the earlier Garden scene. It is the new-Creation conclusion revealed in the vision: the curse is no more, God's people see His face, and the tree of life appears in the city. The third completion is the consummation of the divine purpose, not a correction authored by the creature.

### 9.5 Integrated sequence

**Creation completed → Fall and promised Seed → Redemption accomplished in Christ, the last Adam → Consummation declared.**

The three completion declarations remain:
1. **Genesis 2:1–5 — Creation:** “were finished” / God “ended” His work.
2. **John 19:30 — Redemption:** “It is finished.”
3. **Revelation 21:6 — Consummation:** “It is done.”

The wording is not identical, and the events are not interchangeable. The unity is in the canonical testimony to the Author's purpose across the beginning, the redemptive work of Christ, and the new Creation.

**Central distinction:** The first Adam does not author Creation; the serpent does not author the promise; the last Adam does not act as an independent rival author; and the creature does not author the consummation. God remains the originating Author throughout.

### 9.6 GUA-W placement and methodological guardrails

This is recognition of a Scriptural relationship within the completed architecture, not an amendment or extension of GUA-W. The map witnesses to the Author's one Creation and His purpose; it is not God, not Christ, and not the source of the events it maps.

Do not:
- call the three passages three identical quotations;
- call Revelation 21:6 the only occurrence of “It is done” in Revelation (compare 16:17);
- claim Genesis 3:15 itself names Jesus or gives the complete chronology;
- conflate the first completion of Creation with the second completion of redemption;
- treat the new Creation as merely a rewind to Eden;
- make the first Adam and last Adam interchangeable;
- claim that the Cross eliminates the distinct future consummation announced in Revelation 21.

The investigation follows the order supplied by Scripture: beginning, transgression and promise, Christ's completed redemptive work, and final consummation.


## 10. The covenant people and preservation of the promised line

### 10.1 The question

Why did God call and maintain a people as His own in the Old Testament? One major answer, central to the canonical movement from Genesis 3:15 to the coming of Christ, is that God established and preserved the covenant line through which the promised Messiah would come.

This is not a claim that Israel's calling had only a genealogical purpose. Scripture gives Israel a wider covenant vocation, and that vocation must remain visible alongside the promised line.

### 10.2 The line as Scripture traces it

- **Genesis 3:15:** God declares enmity between the serpent and the woman and between their respective seed/offspring. The promise begins with God's declaration.
- **Genesis 12:1–3:** God calls Abram and promises that in him all families of the earth will be blessed.
- **Genesis 17:7, 19–21:** God establishes His covenant with Abraham and identifies Isaac, rather than Ishmael, as the son through whom the covenant is established.
- **Genesis 21:12; 25:23; 28:13–15:** the covenant promise is carried through Isaac and Jacob according to God's stated purpose.
- **Genesis 49:8–10:** Judah receives the royal/scepter promise in Jacob's blessing.
- **2 Samuel 7:12–16:** God makes the covenant promise concerning David's house, kingdom, and throne.
- **Isaiah 11:1–10; Jeremiah 23:5–6; Micah 5:2:** the prophets speak of the coming righteous ruler from David's line and the ruler from Bethlehem.
- **Matthew 1:1:** the Gospel opens by identifying Jesus Christ as “the son of David, the son of Abraham.”
- **Luke 1:31–33:** the angel announces that Jesus will receive the throne of David His father and reign over the house of Jacob.
- **Romans 1:3–4:** Paul describes God's Son as made of the seed of David according to the flesh and declared Son of God with power by the resurrection.
- **Galatians 3:8, 16:** Paul connects the blessing of the nations promised to Abraham with the Gospel and identifies the promised Seed in that Abrahamic promise as Christ.

### 10.3 Why Israel is more than a preserved genealogy

God's election of Israel includes covenant relationship and vocation:
- **Exodus 19:4–6:** Israel is called to be a peculiar treasure, a kingdom of priests, and a holy nation.
- **Deuteronomy 7:6–8:** Israel is chosen as a special people, with the text grounding the choice in God's love and oath, not Israel's numerical greatness or merit.
- **Romans 3:1–2:** Paul identifies an advantage of the Jew, especially that the oracles of God were committed to them.
- **Romans 9:4–5:** Paul names Israel's adoption, glory, covenants, giving of the law, service of God, promises, fathers, and the Messiah's coming according to the flesh.
- **Romans 11:1–2, 25–29:** Paul rejects the idea that God has cast away His people and speaks of God's continuing purpose and irrevocable gifts and calling.

Therefore the preserved line is central, but it is not the whole explanation of Israel's calling. Israel is the covenant people to whom God entrusted promises, revelation, worship, and the historical setting through which the Messiah came; through Abraham's Seed the blessing reaches the nations.

### 10.4 What “God protected His plan inside that seed line” can responsibly mean

As a concise theological summary, this is a sound way to describe God's providential preservation of His covenant promise and the historical line leading to Christ. The wording “protected His perfect Plan inside that Seed Line” is a summary, not a direct quotation from one verse. It should be supported by the specific covenant texts above rather than treated as a separate biblical formula.

The line was not preserved because its human members were sinless or independently secured God's purpose. The Old Testament itself records failures, judgment, exile, and the need for a remnant. The continuity rests on God's promise and faithfulness, not human perfection. See Isaiah 10:20–22; 11:1–10; Romans 9:6–13; 11:1–6.

### 10.5 Canonical finding

God's people Israel served within His covenant purpose; through the Abrahamic and Davidic promises the Messiah came according to the flesh. The purpose was never merely to preserve a family tree for its own sake: the promised Seed is connected to the blessing of all nations. The New Testament identifies Jesus as the promised Christ and locates Him within this history.

This is the historical and covenant bridge between Genesis 3:15 and the second completion in Christ. It does not make Israel the Author of the promise, nor does it make the genealogy the source of the Messiah's mission. God calls, promises, preserves, and fulfills.



## 11. John 17:4 and John 19:28–30 — the work given and the work declared finished

### 11.1 John 17:1–5 — the work given by the Father

John 17 begins with Jesus lifting His eyes to heaven and saying, “Father, the hour is come; glorify thy Son, that thy Son also may glorify thee” (17:1). He speaks of the authority given Him over all flesh and of giving eternal life to those given Him by the Father (17:2–3). He then says:

> I have glorified thee on the earth: I have finished the work which thou gavest me to do. (John 17:4, KJV)

Verse 5 immediately speaks of the glory He had with the Father before the world was.

**What the text states:** Jesus attributes the work to the Father as the One who gave it; Jesus says He has glorified the Father on earth and finished that given work. The prayer occurs before the arrest and crucifixion. The “hour” has arrived, and the prayer moves directly into Jesus' impending Passion.

**What the surrounding prayer shows:** John 17:6–8 speaks of manifesting the Father's name, giving the Father's words, and the disciples receiving and knowing the source of Jesus' mission. Verses 9–19 address those given to Him, their keeping, protection, sanctification, and sending. Verses 20–26 extend the prayer to those who will believe through the disciples' word and speak of unity, glory, love, and the revelation of the Father's name.

**Boundary:** John 17:4 does not provide an exhaustive itemized list of every act included in “the work.” Nor does the verse say that the crucifixion, death, Resurrection, and return to the Father have already occurred at the moment Jesus prays. It is not sound to use the past-tense “I have finished” to erase the Passion that immediately follows.

### 11.2 John 19:28–30 — the completion at the Cross

John 19:28 says Jesus, “knowing that all things were now accomplished, that the scripture might be fulfilled,” says, “I thirst.” After receiving the vinegar, Jesus says, “It is finished,” bows His head, and gives up the ghost (19:29–30).

The Greek form τετέλεσται (*tetelestai*) is the perfect form of τελέω (*teleō*), “to finish/complete.” The grammatical form is compatible with a completed action whose result stands; it must not be made to carry an entire doctrine by itself. The immediate narrative context is decisive: Jesus has fulfilled what is being brought to completion in His Passion and then actually dies.

**What the text states:** the completion declaration is spoken at the Cross, immediately before Jesus' death. John frames it with the statement that Jesus knows all things are now accomplished and that Scripture is fulfilled.

**Boundary:** John 19:30 does not mean that the Resurrection, Ascension, continuing intercession, or future consummation are unnecessary or have already occurred. The New Testament explicitly makes the Resurrection essential to the Gospel (1 Corinthians 15:3–4, 12–19; Romans 4:25). The Cross is not the final consummation of Revelation 21.

### 11.3 How the two declarations relate without contradiction

John 17:4 and John 19:30 are not competing claims and should not be flattened into one undifferentiated moment.

- **John 17:4:** Jesus speaks to the Father, before the Passion, of having glorified Him on earth and having finished the work given Him. The mission entrusted by the Father has been faithfully carried out in Jesus' earthly ministry and has reached the appointed “hour.”
- **John 19:28–30:** within the Passion itself, Jesus declares “It is finished” as the Scripture-fulfilling work reaches its completion at the Cross, and He gives up His life.

The first statement looks back over the work manifested in His earthly ministry and speaks from the threshold of the hour. The second is uttered at the climactic event of His death. John 17 does not say the Cross has already happened; John 19 does not cancel the ministry and revelation that preceded it.

### 11.4 The work is the Father's; the Son accomplishes it

John's Gospel repeatedly presents Jesus as sent by the Father and doing the Father's will: John 4:34; 5:19–23, 30, 36; 6:38–40; 8:28–29; 10:17–18; 12:49–50. These passages must be read in their contexts, but together they establish the pattern of commission, obedience, and accomplishment. Jesus does not present Himself as an independent rival source of the mission.

John 17:4 explicitly says the Father gave the work and Jesus glorified the Father by completing it. John 19:30 records the climactic completion at the Cross. The Resurrection then vindicates and manifests the victory and is indispensable to the apostolic Gospel witness.

### 11.5 Relationship to the three completions

This comparison sharpens, rather than replaces, the established three-completion pattern:

1. **Genesis 2:1–3 — Creation:** God finishes the created work.
2. **John 19:28–30 — Redemption:** the Son declares the redemptive work finished at the Cross.
3. **Revelation 21:1–6 — Consummation:** God declares “It is done” in the new-Creation vision.

John 17:4 is not a fourth completion. It is an essential interpretive witness to the work the Father gave the Son and to the Son's faithful accomplishment of that mission as He enters the hour. It helps us understand the second completion without replacing John 19:30 as the Cross declaration in the threefold comparison.

### 11.6 Integrated finding

The Father gives the work; the Son is sent and accomplishes it; the earthly ministry manifests the Father and His words; the Passion brings the Scripture-fulfilling work to its climactic completion at the Cross; the Son dies; God raises Him; and the final consummation remains ahead in Revelation.

This preserves the distinct statements and events. “Finished” is not a claim that every later event has already occurred; it is a declaration of the completion of the work in its stated context. The Resurrection is not an optional appendix to the Cross, and the final new Creation is not collapsed into either John 17 or John 19.



## 12. The Father gives; the Son receives, keeps, and raises — John 6:37–45, 64–65 and John 17

### 12.1 Read John 6 in its discourse

John 6:37–40 must be read within the Bread of Life discourse (6:22–71), not as an isolated formula.

- **6:35:** Jesus identifies Himself as the bread of life and speaks of coming to Him and believing on Him.
- **6:36:** He identifies the problem of seeing and yet not believing.
- **6:37:** “All that the Father giveth me shall come to me”; the one coming to Him He will not cast out.
- **6:38:** Jesus says He came down from heaven not to do His own will, but the will of the One who sent Him.
- **6:39:** The Father's will is that Jesus lose nothing of all that the Father has given Him, but raise it up again at the last day.
- **6:40:** The one seeing the Son and believing on Him has everlasting life, and Jesus will raise that one at the last day.
- **6:44–45:** No man can come to Jesus except the Father who sent Him draw him; the one who has heard and learned of the Father comes to Jesus.
- **6:64–65:** Jesus identifies unbelief among the hearers and says that no man can come to Him unless it is given to him by the Father.

### 12.2 What the passage explicitly establishes

The passage places several realities together without treating them as rivals:

1. **The Father's giving:** the people are described as given by the Father to the Son (6:37, 39).
2. **Their coming:** all that the Father gives shall come to the Son (6:37).
3. **The Son's reception:** the one who comes is not cast out (6:37).
4. **The Son's commission:** Jesus came to do the will of the Father who sent Him (6:38).
5. **The Son's keeping and raising:** He is to lose nothing of what the Father has given Him and will raise it at the last day (6:39).
6. **Human believing:** the one seeing the Son and believing on Him has everlasting life (6:40).
7. **The Father's drawing and teaching:** coming is not represented as originating in autonomous human capacity; the Father draws, and the one who has heard and learned of the Father comes (6:44–45).
8. **The Father's enabling/giving:** 6:65 repeats that coming to Jesus is not possible unless it is given by the Father.

The text does not erase the human act of coming or believing. Nor does it present that act as the independent originating cause of the Father's giving, drawing, or the Son's saving work.

### 12.3 John 17 — the same Father/Son relationship in the prayer

John 17 develops the commission and the people given to the Son in the hour before the Passion.

- **17:1–3:** the hour has come; the Father has given the Son authority over all flesh, that He should give eternal life to as many as the Father has given Him. Eternal life is described in relation to knowing the only true God and Jesus Christ whom He sent.
- **17:4–5:** Jesus says He has glorified the Father on earth and finished the work the Father gave Him; He then speaks of the glory He had with the Father before the world was.
- **17:6–8:** Jesus has manifested the Father's name to the men given Him out of the world; they were the Father's, were given to the Son, have kept the Father's word, and have received the words given by the Father through the Son.
- **17:9–11:** Jesus prays for those given Him, speaks of them as the Father's, and asks the Father to keep them in His name.
- **17:12:** Jesus says He kept those given Him while He was with them; the verse also names “the son of perdition” in connection with the fulfillment of Scripture. The exact relation of this clause to the wording “those that thou gavest me I have kept” should be handled from the verse and its context, not simplified into an unsupported universal formula.
- **17:20:** Jesus explicitly extends the prayer beyond the immediate disciples to those who will believe on Him through their word.
- **17:24:** Jesus speaks of those the Father has given Him and desires that they be with Him and behold His glory.

### 12.4 What “given” does and does not mean here

The repeated giving is the Father's action and belongs to the Father's authority and purpose. The Son receives those given, manifests the Father's name and words, keeps them, gives eternal life, and promises their resurrection. The language is not a transfer of authorship from the Father to the Son; it is the Father's commission and the Son's faithful accomplishment within the unity expressed in John's Gospel.

Do not collapse every occurrence of “given” into an identical referent without examining its local context. John 17:6–19 directly concerns the disciples with Jesus; 17:20 explicitly includes later believers through their word; 17:24 speaks of those given to the Son in the prayer's wider scope. The passage itself broadens the prayer's horizon.

### 12.5 The covenant seed line and the people given to the Son must not be conflated

The Old Testament covenant line through Abraham, Isaac, Jacob, Judah, and David is the historical line through which the Messiah came according to the flesh. John 6 and 17, however, are speaking about those the Father gives to the Son and the Son's saving work toward them. These are related within God's one purpose, but they are not interchangeable categories.

Natural descent alone is not equivalent to saving reception:
- John 8:39–47 distinguishes mere appeal to Abrahamic descent from doing Abraham's works and hearing God's words.
- Romans 9:6–8 distinguishes Israel according to the flesh from the children of promise.
- Galatians 3:7, 26–29 explains the children of Abraham in relation to faith in Christ and belonging to Christ.

Therefore, do not say that every physical descendant in the preserved line is thereby one of those given to the Son for eternal life. The preserved covenant line serves the coming of the Messiah; the Father's giving in John 6 and 17 concerns the people whom the Son receives, keeps, and raises.

### 12.6 Relationship to John 17:4 and John 19:30

John 6:37–40 gives the Father's purpose concerning those given to the Son: they come, are received, are not lost, and are raised at the last day. John 17 shows the Son speaking of the authority, people, words, keeping, and work given by the Father as the hour arrives. John 19:28–30 records the climactic Cross declaration, “It is finished.”

These passages form a coherent sequence of commission and accomplishment, but they do not provide a complete chronological chart of every saving operation. Do not infer a precise order of regeneration, faith, or every aspect of salvation from the word “given” alone. The passages establish divine initiative, the Son's commission and keeping, human coming and believing, and promised resurrection. Any more detailed chronology requires explicit additional Scriptural evidence.

### 12.7 Integrated finding

The Father gives; the Son is sent; those given come; the Son receives and does not cast out the one who comes; the Son keeps those given Him and gives eternal life; those who believe have everlasting life; and the Son promises to raise them at the last day. The human response is real, but the originating initiative and saving purpose described in these passages belong to the Father and are carried out by the Son.

This is the people-focused dimension of the work given to the Son. It clarifies John 17:4 without making it a fourth completion, and it clarifies the redemptive mission that reaches its climactic declaration at John 19:30. The Resurrection and final raising remain essential to the complete canonical account.
