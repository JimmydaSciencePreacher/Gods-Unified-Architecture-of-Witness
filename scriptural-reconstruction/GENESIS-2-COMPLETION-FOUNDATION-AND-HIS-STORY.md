# Genesis 2 — Completion as the Foundation of His Story

**Status:** Textual foundation and theological synthesis; architectural implications remain proposals  
**Scope:** KJV Genesis 2:1–17, with canonical comparison to John 19:30 and Revelation 21:6

## Purpose

This investigation examines what Genesis 2 establishes before the events of Genesis 3: the completed Creation, man's created position, appointed purpose, and the command that defines permission and boundary. It also records the theological correspondence identified as **the three completions**.

The expression **His Story** names the theological understanding of Scripture as the unfolding of the Author's purpose. The comparison below is a theological pattern, not a claim that three passages use one identical word.

## 1. The first completion: Genesis 2:1–3

> Thus the heavens and the earth were finished, and all the host of them. (Genesis 2:1, KJV)

> And on the seventh day God ended his work which he had made; and he rested on the seventh day from all his work which he had made. (Genesis 2:2, KJV)

> And God blessed the seventh day, and sanctified it: because that in it he had rested from all his work which God created and made. (Genesis 2:3, KJV)

### Textual observations

- The heavens, earth, and all their host are declared finished.
- God is the subject who ends/finishes His work.
- God rests/ceases from that work.
- God blesses and sanctifies the seventh day.

The Hebrew root כָּלָה (*kālāh*) appears in 2:1 and 2:2: the heavens and earth “were finished,” and God “ended” His work. Verse 2 also uses שָׁבַת (*šāḇaṯ*) for resting/ceasing. Verse 3 adds בָּרַךְ (*bāraḵ*, bless) and קָדַשׁ (*qādaš*, sanctify/make holy).

These are related statements, but they are not one undifferentiated action. Finishing, ceasing, blessing, and sanctifying are separately expressed.

### Foundation established

The passage identifies God as the One who creates and completes the work. The created order is the result of His work; it is not presented as the originating authority over that work.

This establishes the starting hierarchy for the investigation:

**Author → Word → Creation → Witness**

The text directly declares completion and divine action. The further architectural implication—that later events must be understood within the order established by the Creator—is a theological synthesis grounded in the sequence, not a dictionary definition of *kālāh*.

## 2. Genesis 2 establishes man's place, purpose, permission, and boundary

Genesis 2 continues beyond the declaration of completion. It records the formation and placement of man, his appointed work, and God's command.

| Passage | Textual content |
|---|---|
| Genesis 2:7 | The LORD God forms man from the dust, breathes into his nostrils the breath of life, and man becomes a living soul. |
| Genesis 2:8–14 | The LORD God plants the Garden, places man there, and the account describes the Garden and its rivers. |
| Genesis 2:15 | The man is placed in the Garden to dress/work it and to keep/guard it. |
| Genesis 2:16 | The LORD God commands the man and grants broad permission to eat from the trees of the Garden. |
| Genesis 2:17 | God states a specific prohibition and its consequence. |
| Genesis 2:18–25 | God provides the woman, brings her to the man, and the account describes their joining and original condition. |

### 2.1 Purpose is present before the Fall

Genesis 2:15 gives the purpose of the man's placement: to work/cultivate the Garden and to keep/guard it. The Hebrew verbs are עָבַד (*ʿāḇaḏ*) and שָׁמַר (*šāmar*).

Purpose is therefore not an explanation first introduced after the Fall. It is part of the pre-Fall account. The further GUA-W proposal that appointed purpose relates Constraint to Structure may be investigated from this foundation, but it is not a literal translation of Genesis 2:15.

### 2.2 Permission and boundary are given by God

Genesis 2:16–17 identifies:
- **Source of the command:** the LORD God.
- **Recipient:** the man.
- **Permission:** eating freely from the trees of the Garden.
- **Prohibition:** eating of the tree of the knowledge of good and evil.
- **Stated consequence:** “thou shalt surely die.”

The Hebrew emphatic construction אָכֹל תֹּאכֵל (*ʾāḵōl tōʾḵēl*) expresses the broad permission (“freely eat”). מוֹת תָּמוּת (*mōṯ tāmūṯ*) is the emphatic construction rendered “thou shalt surely die.” The latter does not, by its doubled form alone, specify every theological dimension or timing of death.

The command and boundary are established before the serpent's speech in Genesis 3.

## 3. Capacity to evaluate is distinct from authority to originate the standard

This distinction is central to reading Genesis 3.

- **Perception:** seeing or receiving information.
- **Knowledge:** knowing or becoming aware.
- **Evaluation:** regarding something as good, desirable, harmful, or wise.
- **Desire:** inclination toward what is perceived or evaluated.
- **Action:** taking or doing.
- **Authority:** the source or right by which a governing standard or command is established.

A creature's capacity to perceive, know, evaluate, desire, and act does not by itself make that creature the originating authority for the standard by which good and evil are defined.

Genesis 2:16–17 supplies the narrative structure: God commands; man receives the command; God states permission and prohibition. Genesis 2 does not narrate a delegation to man of authority to originate or independently redefine that standard.

**Text/inference distinction:** Genesis 2 does not contain the exact abstract sentence “man does not possess independent authority to define good and evil.” That is a theological inference from the stated source, recipient, permission, and prohibition. It should not be presented as a direct quotation. The narrative foundation for the inference is already present before Genesis 3.

## 4. Genesis 3 is read against the order already established

Genesis 3 does not introduce the first command or the first boundary. Those are already present in Genesis 2:16–17.

The narrative sequence in Genesis 3 can therefore be examined in this order:

1. **Prior word:** God has spoken (Genesis 2:16–17).
2. **Competing account:** the serpent questions and reframes God's word (Genesis 3:1–5).
3. **Perception and evaluation:** the woman sees the tree in terms of food, sight, and wisdom (Genesis 3:6).
4. **Desire and action:** she takes and eats, and gives to her husband, who also eats (Genesis 3:6).
5. **Changed awareness:** their eyes are opened and they know that they are naked (Genesis 3:7).
6. **Confrontation and accountability:** God questions them and addresses what has happened (Genesis 3:8–19).
7. **Changed condition and exclusion:** they are sent from the Garden and access to the tree of life is guarded (Genesis 3:22–24).

Perception and evaluation are not intrinsically evil. The question is whether human evaluation and action remain faithful to the already-given word or instead proceed on the basis of the serpent's competing account.

Genesis 3:22 acknowledges a changed human condition involving knowledge of good and evil. It does not state that man became the Author, that God's authority was transferred, or that man became the independent source of the standard. Knowledge and originating authority remain distinct.

## 5. The three completions — Creation, Redemption, Consummation

The three passages form a theological correspondence within His Story. They do not use the same original-language word and do not describe identical events.

### 5.1 Creation — Genesis 2:1–3

The heavens, earth, and all their host are declared finished. God ends His work, ceases from it, blesses and sanctifies the seventh day.

Hebrew completion language includes כָּלָה (*kālāh*); cessation is expressed by שָׁבַת (*šāḇaṯ*).

### 5.2 Redemption — John 19:30

> When Jesus therefore had received the vinegar, he said, It is finished: and he bowed his head, and gave up the ghost. (John 19:30, KJV)

The Greek τετέλεσται (*tetelestai*), from τελέω (*teleō*), is rendered “It is finished.” The declaration occurs in the account of Jesus' crucifixion.

### 5.3 Consummation — Revelation 21:6

> And he said unto me, It is done. I am Alpha and Omega, the beginning and the end. (Revelation 21:6, KJV)

The Greek form γέγοναν (*gegonan*) in the critical text is from γίνομαι (*ginomai*) and conveys that the things have come to pass or are accomplished. The declaration appears in the vision of the new heaven and new earth and the holy city (Revelation 21:1–6).

Revelation 16:17 also contains the KJV wording “It is done.” Revelation 21:6 is identified here specifically with the consummation/new-creation vision, not as the only occurrence of that English wording in Revelation.

### 5.4 The proposed correspondence

**Creation → Redemption → Consummation**

- Genesis 2: the created work is declared finished.
- John 19: the Son declares “It is finished” at the Cross.
- Revelation 21: the final order is announced with “It is done.”

This is a canonical and theological comparison. It does not claim that the three passages repeat one identical phrase, use one identical word, or describe one identical kind of completion.

## 6. Purpose before the Fall and the purpose of redemption

Genesis 2:15 records man's assigned work before the temptation. The wider canon also places Christ's redemptive purpose before its manifestation in time:

- **1 Peter 1:19–20:** Christ is described as foreordained before the foundation of the world and manifested in the last times.
- **Ephesians 1:4:** God is said to have chosen us in Christ before the foundation of the world.
- **Revelation 17:8:** speaks of names not written in the book of life from the foundation of the world.
- **Revelation 13:8:** has a grammatical attachment ambiguity concerning “from the foundation of the world”; the phrase may be read with the Lamb's being slain or with the names not written. It should not be used as unqualified proof of either grammatical attachment.

These passages warrant a wider investigation of God's purpose before the foundation of the world. Their individual wording and context must remain distinct.

## 7. Revised framing of the open questions

The earlier wording of U-006 and U-007 risked treating the original authority structure as though it still had to be discovered from Genesis 3 alone. The investigation now begins with the prior foundation in Genesis 2.

- **U-006 — Human will:** The components and operation of will still require wider Scriptural study. Genesis 2 already supplies man's created position, appointed work, and received command before the Fall.
- **U-007 — Knowledge and authority:** The exact nature of the knowledge in Genesis 3:22 remains a question for contextual study. The prior command and its source are already explicit in Genesis 2:16–17. No transfer of originating authority is stated in Genesis 3:22.

The unresolved work concerns the precise nature of changed knowledge, the relation among perception, desire, choice, and action, and the consequences of the Fall—not whether God had already spoken and established the command.

## 9. Working conclusion

Genesis 2 is not merely background to the Fall. It establishes the completed Creation and the Creator's prior ordering of man's place, purpose, permission, and boundary. Genesis 3 must be interpreted against that foundation.

The three completions offer a meaningful theological sequence within **His Story**: Creation, Redemption, and Consummation. The language and events differ, and the comparison preserves those differences.

Genesis 2 does not explicitly narrate every later event or state every later theological formulation. It does establish the foundational order from which the later account is read. The Fall does not become a new source of originating authority; it is narrated as a departure within an order already established by the Creator.

No scientific mechanism, universal runtime, or new canonical GUA-W node is established by this document. Architectural implications remain subject to evidence and the repository's admissibility rules.
