# Hebrew Language Audit — Genesis 2:8–14

**Status:** PHASE 2 LANGUAGE AUDIT — INITIAL FINDINGS  
**Text base:** KJV Genesis 2:8–14, compared with the Masoretic Hebrew and selected translation notes  
**Purpose:** Examine planting, placing, growth, the two named trees, the river, its division, and the named lands/rivers without turning these details into predetermined GUA-W stages.

## 1. Genesis 2:8 — God plants and places

KJV:

> And the LORD God planted a garden eastward in Eden; and there he put the man whom he had formed.

### 1.1 נָטַע — *nāṭa‘*, “planted”

The verb translated “planted” is נָטַע (*nata‘*), whose ordinary range includes planting or setting plants/trees in the ground. It is used elsewhere for planting vineyards, trees, and figuratively for establishing.

**Textual finding:** The verse attributes the planting of the Garden to the LORD God. The text does not describe the method by which He planted it.

**Architectural caution:** “Planting” is a concrete action in the narrative. It may be compared with preparation or establishment, but it is not itself a Hebrew technical term for GUA-W Structure, Framework, or Pattern.

### 1.2 גַּן — *gan*, “garden”

The noun גַּן (*gan*) means garden or enclosed cultivated place. The NET notes suggest “orchard” because the description emphasizes fruit trees, while the traditional KJV “garden” is well established.

**Finding:** Retain the KJV “garden” as the project's base text. “Orchard” is a contextual translation proposal, not a reason to replace the KJV wording.

### 1.3 מִקֶּדֶם — *miqqedem*, “eastward/from the east”

The phrase can be rendered “eastward” or “in the east,” depending on the syntactic and geographical reading. The text locates Eden in relation to the east; it does not provide coordinates.

### 1.4 שׂוּם / שִׂים — *śîm*, “put/place”

The verb translated “put” is from שׂוּם (*sim*), “put, set, place.” The verse says God placed the man there, and identifies him as the one He had formed.

**Textual sequence:** God plants the Garden; God places in it the man whom He had formed.

**Important distinction:** Formation and placement are separately narrated. The verse does not say that Adam placed himself in the Garden or prepared it.

## 2. Genesis 2:9 — God causes growth; two trees are identified

KJV:

> And out of the ground made the LORD God to grow every tree that is pleasant to the sight, and good for food; the tree of life also in the midst of the garden, and the tree of knowledge of good and evil.

### 2.1 צָמַח — *ṣāmaḥ*, “sprout/grow”

The verb is from צָמַח (*tsamach*), “sprout, spring up, grow.” In Genesis 2:9 it appears in a causative Hiphil form: the LORD God caused the trees to grow/sprout from the ground.

**Finding:** The text explicitly attributes the causing of this growth to God. It is not merely an observation that trees happened to appear.

**Architectural question:** The sequence distinguishes planting in verse 8 from causing growth in verse 9. Whether these should be represented as separate operations in GUA-W is a proposal to test, not a lexical conclusion.

### 2.2 מַרְאֶה — *mar’eh*, “sight/appearance”

The phrase translated “pleasant to the sight” describes trees desirable or pleasing in appearance. The text includes visual quality as part of the description.

### 2.3 מַאֲכָל — *ma’akhal*, “food”

The phrase “good for food” identifies the trees as suitable/desirable for eating. The verse therefore describes both appearance and food provision.

**Finding:** The Garden's trees are described with more than one attribute. Do not reduce the text to a single functional criterion.

### 2.4 עֵץ הַחַיִּים — *‘ēṣ haḥayyîm*, “tree of life”

The phrase is literally “tree of the life.” Genesis 3:22–24 later associates eating from this tree with living forever and the exclusion from access to it.

**Textual finding:** The tree is named “the tree of life” and is located in the midst of the Garden.

**Interpretive note:** The NET note reads the construct as a tree whose fruit gives life, in light of Genesis 3:22. This is a grammatical/theological interpretation, not an independent statement in Genesis 2:9 that explains the tree's mechanism.

### 2.5 עֵץ הַדַּעַת טוֹב וָרָע — *‘ēṣ hadda‘at ṭov vara‘*, “tree of the knowledge of good and evil”

The noun דַּעַת (*da‘at*) is related to יָדַע (*yada‘), “know.” The phrase names the tree by its relation to knowledge of good and evil.

The exact scope of “knowledge of good and evil” is debated. Interpretations include moral discernment, comprehensive knowledge, or a wisdom associated with maturity and the divine. Genesis 3:5–6 and 3:22 must be considered alongside Genesis 2:9 and 2:17.

**Finding:** The tree's name explicitly associates it with knowledge of good and evil. The name alone does not settle the precise nature of that knowledge.

**GUA-W caution:** Do not prematurely identify the tree with human autonomy, free will, a data structure, or a scientific mechanism. Such interpretations must be tested against Genesis 2:16–17, Genesis 3, and wider Scripture.

### 2.6 “In the midst of the garden”

The verse identifies the two trees as being in the midst/center of the Garden. Genesis 3 later refers to the tree of life and the tree from which God had commanded the man not to eat. The exact spatial relation between the two trees should not be overdrawn beyond the wording.

## 3. Genesis 2:10 — One river and its purpose

KJV:

> And a river went out of Eden to water the garden; and from thence it was parted, and became into four heads.

### 3.1 נָהָר — *nahar*, “river”

The verse introduces a river associated with Eden. It is singular at the point of origin in the narrative.

### 3.2 יָצָא — *yatsa’*, “go out/issue forth”

The form יוֹצֵא (*yotse*) is an active participle from יָצָא (*yatsa’*), “go out, come forth.” It describes the river as issuing from Eden. The participle may be read with a continuing or durative sense; translations differ between “went out,” “flows,” and “was flowing.”

**Finding:** The text describes the river as coming from Eden. The precise temporal perspective of the participle is a grammatical and translation question.

### 3.3 שָׁקָה — *shaqah*, “water”

The infinitive לְהַשְׁקוֹת (*lehashqot*) is a Hiphil form meaning “to water” or “to cause to drink.” The river's stated purpose is to water the Garden.

**Textual finding:** The narrative explicitly gives the river a watering function.

**Architectural caution:** A source and its provision to a region may be a useful relation to examine, but this does not establish a universal “propagation” or “energy distribution” law.

### 3.4 פָּרַד — *parad*, “divide/separate”

The verb יִפָּרֵד (*yippared*) is Niphal, commonly understood as “it divides” or “it is divided.” From there, the river is associated with four heads/streams.

**Finding:** The account presents one river issuing from Eden, watering the Garden, and then dividing into four.

**Architectural question:** This is a textual example of a singular source-flow and subsequent branching. Whether it supports a GUA-W propagation pattern must be examined alongside other Scriptural examples and must not be equated automatically with branching in physics, biology, or networks.

## 4. Genesis 2:11–14 — Four named rivers and geographic description

The passage names:
1. Pishon.
2. Gihon.
3. Hiddekel (identified in many translations with the Tigris).
4. Euphrates.

It also describes lands and geographic relations:
- Pishon circles/flows around the land of Havilah, where gold is found.
- The land is described as having good gold, bdellium, and onyx (the identity of some materials is debated).
- Gihon circles/flows around the land of Cush.
- Hiddekel flows toward/east of Assyria.
- The fourth river is the Euphrates.

### 4.1 סָבַב — *sābab*, “go around/surround”

The verb used in the descriptions of Pishon and Gihon has the sense of going around or surrounding. The KJV's “compasseth” is an older rendering.

### 4.2 קֶדֶם — *qedem*, “east/front”

The description of Hiddekel uses a directional term often rendered “east of” or “in front of” Assyria, depending on context and translation.

### 4.3 Geographic identifications

The Tigris and Euphrates are identifiable ancient river names. The locations of Pishon, Gihon, Havilah, and the Cush named here are disputed. Biblical “Cush” often refers to regions south of Egypt, but the NET notes argue that this context may refer to a Mesopotamian region. These identifications should not be presented as settled geography.

**Finding:** The text names rivers and lands and provides geographic descriptions. It does not provide enough information to locate every feature with certainty on a modern map.

**Important caution:** The existence of the Tigris and Euphrates does not by itself prove a modern geographical reconstruction of Eden. Nor does uncertainty about Pishon and Gihon justify dismissing the entire passage as non-geographical.

## 5. The sequence in Genesis 2:8–14

| Verse | What the text says |
|---|---|
| 2:8 | God plants the Garden in Eden, eastward; God places there the man He had formed |
| 2:9 | God causes trees to grow from the ground; trees are described as pleasing to sight and good for food; two particular trees are named |
| 2:10 | A river issues from Eden to water the Garden; from there it divides into four heads |
| 2:11–12 | The first river is named Pishon and described in relation to Havilah and its resources |
| 2:13 | The second river is named Gihon and described in relation to Cush |
| 2:14 | The third is Hiddekel/Tigris, associated with Assyria; the fourth is Euphrates |

### Sequence observations

1. Planting and placing are attributed to God.
2. The man is identified as already formed when placed in the Garden.
3. The text attributes the trees' growth to God.
4. The trees have described qualities and include two specially named trees.
5. The river has a stated watering function.
6. The river's division and the four named branches are described.
7. The account includes specific land and resource descriptions, while some geographic identifications remain uncertain.

## 7. Open questions

1. Does *gan* in this context mean a garden, orchard, or cultivated enclosure? Which translation best preserves the KJV and the passage's emphasis?
2. How do *nata‘* (plant), *sim* (place), and *tsamach* (cause to grow) relate in the narrative?
3. Does Genesis 2:9's “every tree” describe all tree kinds in the Garden, and how does this relate to Genesis 1:11–12?
4. What is the relation between the Tree of Life and the Tree of Knowledge, including their placement and the later narrative?
5. What does “knowledge of good and evil” mean in Genesis 2–3, and how does it relate to the command and the serpent's claim?
6. Is the river in 2:10 a continuing river described from the narrator's standpoint, or a past flow? Examine the participle and discourse structure.
7. What are the best-supported identifications of Pishon, Gihon, Havilah, and Cush? Which remain unknown?
8. Does the river's source-and-branch description contribute to a wider Scriptural pattern of provision and propagation, or is it a geographical detail whose architectural use should remain limited?
9. How do the Garden's preparation and provision relate to the man's appointed work in 2:15?

## 8. Provisional conclusion

Genesis 2:8–14 describes God planting the Garden, placing the previously formed man there, causing trees to grow, identifying two particular trees, and describing a river that waters the Garden and divides into four named rivers. The passage combines actions attributed to God, descriptions of the Garden's provision, and geographic information.

The clearest textual distinctions are:
- planting is not the same verb as placing;
- placing the man is distinct from forming him;
- planting the Garden is followed by a separate statement that God caused trees to grow;
- the river's stated function is to water the Garden;
- the one-river/four-heads description is explicit, while the wider geographic reconstruction is not settled.

These distinctions provide material for investigating preparation, placement, provision, and branching. They do not yet establish a universal Structure, Framework, Pattern, or Propagation law. No canonical GUA-W node or relationship is changed by this audit.
