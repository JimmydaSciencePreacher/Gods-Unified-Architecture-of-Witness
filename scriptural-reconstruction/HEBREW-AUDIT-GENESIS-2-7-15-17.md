# Hebrew Language Audit — Genesis 2:7, 2:15–17

**Status:** PHASE 2 LANGUAGE AUDIT — INITIAL FINDINGS  
**Text base:** Masoretic Hebrew text, compared with the KJV and selected translation/lexical notes  
**Governing rule:** Lexical meaning informs interpretation; it does not authorize importing a complete GUA-W architecture into a word. Strong's numbers are identifiers, not definitions. Each claim must be checked in context.

## 1. Genesis 2:7 — Formation, breath, and the living man

KJV:

> And the LORD God formed man of the dust of the ground, and breathed into his nostrils the breath of life; and man became a living soul.

### 1.1 יָצַר — yāṣar, “formed”

The verb translated “formed” is יָצַר (*yatsar*). It can mean to form, fashion, or shape. The NET notes connect the term with the work of a potter or artist and note its use for deliberate shaping.

**Textual finding:** Genesis 2:7 attributes the forming of man from the dust of the ground to the LORD God.

**What this supports:** The verse distinguishes the material named (“dust of the ground”) and the action attributed to God (“formed”).

**What it does not establish by itself:** A modern biological process, a literal potter's wheel, a complete model of the human body, or the full technical definition of a GUA-W Formation stage.

### 1.2 נָפַח — nāphaḥ, “breathed”

The verb translated “breathed” is נָפַח (*naphach*): to breathe or blow. The verse says God breathed into the man's nostrils.

**Textual finding:** The action is explicitly attributed to God, and the location/body feature named is the man's nostrils.

**What this supports:** The narrative distinguishes God's forming from God's breathing.

**What it does not establish by itself:** A physiological mechanism, a detachable substance, or an equation between this action and modern respiration.

### 1.3 נְשָׁמָה — nĕshāmāh, “breath”

The phrase in Genesis 2:7 is נִשְׁמַת חַיִּים (*nishmat ḥayyim*), conventionally rendered “breath of life.” The noun *neshamah* can refer to breath and, in some contexts, spirit or life-breath.

A caution is necessary here. Some notes on Genesis 2:7 suggest that *neshamah* is used for humans but not animals. That proposed distinction cannot be stated as an uncontested lexical rule. Genesis 7:22 uses the same noun in the phrase נִשְׁמַת־רוּחַ חַיִּים (*nishmat-ruaḥ ḥayyim*) for the breath in the nostrils of creatures on dry land. The immediate Flood context includes human beings and land animals (Genesis 7:21–23). BDB also lists broader uses such as “every breathing thing” in other passages.

**Finding:** Genesis 2:7 uniquely narrates God breathing into the man's nostrils in this particular formation account. But the lexical evidence does not justify claiming that *neshamah* is categorically exclusive to human beings throughout Scripture.

**GUA-W consequence:** Do not build a human-only ontology on an overstated dictionary claim. Any human distinction must be established from the full Scriptural account, not by asserting that the word *neshamah* never applies to animals.

### 1.4 נֶפֶשׁ חַיָּה — nefeš ḥayyāh, “living soul/being/creature”

The result clause is וַיְהִי הָאָדָם לְנֶפֶשׁ חַיָּה (*vayehi ha-adam le-nefesh ḥayyah*): the man became a *nephesh ḥayyah*.

The KJV renders this “a living soul.” Many modern translations render it “a living being” or “living creature.” The same Hebrew phrase is used of animals in Genesis 1:20, 1:24, 1:30, and 2:19.

**Finding:** The phrase *nephesh ḥayyah* is not a human-exclusive label in Genesis 1–2. It is applied to the man in Genesis 2:7 and to other living creatures elsewhere in the same creation account.

**Important distinction:** This does not make the account of man's formation identical in every respect to the accounts of animals. Genesis 2:7 specifically narrates God forming the man from dust and breathing into his nostrils. The distinction must be grounded in the full sequence and context, not in pretending that the shared phrase *nephesh ḥayyah* is exclusive to man.

### 1.5 The clause sequence

Genesis 2:7 presents three clauses:
1. God formed the man from dust of the ground.
2. God breathed into his nostrils the breath of life.
3. The man became a *nephesh ḥayyah*.

**Textual conclusion:** These clauses are distinguishable and must not be collapsed. The last clause states the resulting condition.

**Limit:** Clause order alone does not prove a measurable time interval, a detachable soul-substance, or a complete metaphysical anatomy. “Formation → divine breathing → living man” is a defensible description of the narrated sequence. Calling it a fully specified universal “Animation mechanism” would go beyond the evidence.

## 2. Genesis 2:15 — Placement, work, and keeping

KJV:

> And the LORD God took the man, and put him into the garden of Eden to dress it and to keep it.

### 2.1 וַיַּנִּחֵהוּ — wayyanniḥēhū, “and put/placed him”

The verb in Genesis 2:15 is from the root נוּחַ (*nuach*) in a causative form. It conveys causing to rest, settling, or placing. The NET notes distinguish this verb from the verb used for God's putting the man in the Garden in Genesis 2:8.

**Finding:** The KJV's “put” is a legitimate translation, but the Hebrew verb gives additional nuance of settling/placing. Do not treat Genesis 2:8 and 2:15 as necessarily repeating the exact same Hebrew verb.

### 2.2 עָבַד — ‘ābad, “dress/work/serve/cultivate”

The first purpose infinitive is לְעָבְדָהּ (*lə‘ovdāh*), from עָבַד (*abad*). The root has a broad range including work, serve, and, in an agricultural context, till or cultivate.

**Finding:** “Dress it” in the KJV means to work/cultivate the Garden, not to clothe it. The word's broader range includes service, but the Garden context points to work upon or care of the Garden.

**Limit:** Do not infer from the root alone that Adam's task was specifically priestly worship. That proposal requires examination of usage and context elsewhere in Scripture.

### 2.3 שָׁמַר — šāmar, “keep/guard/watch”

The second purpose infinitive is וּלְשָׁמְרָהּ (*ulešomrāh*), from שָׁמַר (*shamar*): to keep, watch, guard, preserve, or take care of.

**Finding:** The KJV “keep” carries a guarding/keeping sense. Alongside *abad*, the wording supports work and keeping/guarding as the man's appointed tasks.

**Limit:** A proposed connection with later priestly or sanctuary service may be worth investigating, but it is not established merely by noting the two roots here.

### 2.4 The grammatical purpose construction

Both verbs are infinitive constructs introduced by לְ (*lə*, “to/for”) and carry a feminine singular suffix referring to the Garden.

The verse therefore gives the reason/purpose for placing the man there: **to work/cultivate it and to keep/guard it.**

**Strong textual result:** Purpose is explicit in Genesis 2:15. It is not an architectural term imported into the verse.

**Architectural proposal:** A prepared place and an appointed human task are directly related in this passage. This is a sound basis for investigating Purpose as a relationship between the Creator's prepared environment and the creature's assigned activity. The formula “Purpose bridges Constraint to Structure” remains a GUA-W proposal, not a translation of the Hebrew.

## 3. Genesis 2:16–17 — Command, permission, prohibition, consequence

### 3.1 צָוָה — ṣāwāh, “command”

Genesis 2:16 says the LORD God commanded the man. The verb is צָוָה (*tsavah*), to command or charge.

**Finding:** The passage explicitly identifies the source of the command as the LORD God and its recipient as the man.

### 3.2 אָכֹל תֹּאכֵל — ’āḵōl tōḵēl, “thou mayest freely eat”

The Hebrew construction uses an infinitive absolute followed by a finite verb from the same root אכל (*akal*, “eat”). The construction intensifies or emphasizes the permission. The KJV's “freely eat” conveys the force.

**Finding:** Genesis 2:16 is not only a restriction passage. It first grants broad permission: the man may freely eat from every tree of the Garden.

### 3.3 מוֹת תָּמוּת — mōt tāmūt, “thou shalt surely die”

Genesis 2:17 uses an infinitive absolute followed by a finite form of מות (*mut*, “die”). This is an emphatic Hebrew construction commonly rendered “you shall surely die.”

**Finding:** The doubled verbal form intensifies the certainty of the stated consequence. It does not mean two separate deaths simply because the root appears twice.

**Limit:** The expression itself does not settle every theological question about the timing and dimensions of death. The phrase “in the day” and the later Genesis 3 narrative must be examined together. Do not prematurely force the wording to mean only immediate physical death, only spiritual death, or two separate deaths.

### 3.4 The sequence in the command

Genesis 2:16–17 gives:
1. God as commander.
2. A broad permission.
3. A specific prohibition.
4. A stated consequence.

**Architectural observation:** Constraint in this passage is not an impersonal physical law. It is a Creator-given command with authority, permission, prohibition, and consequence. A scientific comparison must preserve that category distinction.

## 5. Open questions

1. How do *yatsar* and *asah* function across Genesis 1–2, and what distinctions can be established without assuming they are technical stages?
2. How do *ruach*, *neshamah*, and *nephesh* relate across Genesis 1–2, 6–7, Job, and Ecclesiastes?
3. What is the exact relation between the shared *nephesh ḥayyah* designation for humans and animals and the distinctive formation/breath account of Genesis 2:7?
4. Does Genesis 2:15's use of *abad* and *shamar* have a deliberate relation to later tabernacle/temple service language? This requires a corpus comparison, not a word-match alone.
5. What is the relationship between the purpose clauses in Genesis 2:15 and the command/boundary in 2:16–17?
6. What does “in the day” mean in Genesis 2:17 in the context of Genesis 3 and the wider canon?
7. What exactly is the referent and scope of “all the host” in Genesis 2:1? A separate language audit is needed.

## 6. Provisional conclusion

The Hebrew examination strengthens several textual distinctions while correcting two possible overstatements.

First, Genesis 2:7 does distinguish God's forming, God's breathing, and the man's becoming a living *nephesh*. However, neither *neshamah* nor *nephesh ḥayyah* can safely be used to claim an absolute human/animal lexical separation. Genesis 7:22 and the shared *nephesh ḥayyah* usage must remain in the evidence record.

Second, Genesis 2:15 explicitly gives the man's purpose in the Garden: to work/cultivate it and to keep/guard it. This is strong textual grounding for appointed work, stewardship, and a prepared-place/assigned-task relationship. The further GUA-W mapping remains an architectural proposal.

Third, Genesis 2:16–17 presents both permission and boundary, with emphatic Hebrew constructions. The command belongs to the Creator's authority; it must not be reduced to an impersonal scientific constraint.

No canonical GUA-W node is changed by this audit.
