**Status:** PHASE 2 LANGUAGE AUDIT — INITIAL FINDINGS  
**Text base:** KJV wording compared with the Masoretic Hebrew, morphology aids, and translation/commentary notes  
**Question:** What does Genesis 2:4–6 establish about the account heading, the field vegetation, the absence of rain and a man to work the ground, and the water described in verse 6?

## 1. Genesis 2:4 — “These are the generations”

KJV:

> These are the generations of the heavens and of the earth when they were created, in the day that the LORD God made the earth and the heavens,

### 1.1 תּוֹלְדוֹת — *tōledōt*, “generations”

The phrase is אֵלֶּה תוֹלְדוֹת (*’elleh toledot*), “these are the generations/account of.” The noun is related to the idea of begetting or what proceeds from a source. In Genesis it functions repeatedly as a section-heading formula (compare Genesis 5:1; 6:9; 10:1; 11:10, 27; 25:12, 19; 36:1, 9; 37:2).

**Textual observation:** Genesis 2:4 uses a recognizable Genesis heading formula. It identifies the *toledot* of the heavens and earth.

**Interpretive caution:** The formula's function in Genesis is commonly understood as introducing the account or history associated with the named subject. Whether a heading is read as summarizing what precedes, introducing what follows, or functioning as a hinge between both must be assessed in the literary structure of this particular passage. Do not settle that question by the dictionary gloss “generations” alone.

**Architectural question:** Does the verse mark a transition from the broad creation account to the account of what proceeds within the created order? That is a literary-structural question, not a lexical definition of a GUA-W layer.

### 1.2 בְּהִבָּרְאָם — *behibbare’am*, “when they were created”

This form is from בָּרָא (*bara’*, “create”), in a Niphal infinitive construction with a plural suffix: “when they were created.”

**Finding:** The heavens and earth are described as created. The grammatical form does not itself specify a complete theory of how creation occurred.

### 1.3 בְּיוֹם עֲשׂוֹת — *beyom ‘asot*, “in the day that [the LORD God] made”

The verb עֲשׂוֹת (*‘asot*) is the infinitive construct of עָשָׂה (*‘asah*, “do/make”). The verse uses *bara’* and *‘asah* in the same sentence.

**Important restraint:** Their occurrence together does not establish two separate creation mechanisms or a rigid technical distinction between two GUA-W stages. The roots have overlapping semantic range in biblical usage; their contextual function must be examined across passages.

**Literary observation:** Genesis 2:4 refers to the heavens and earth as created and then speaks of the LORD God making earth and heavens. The order “heavens and earth” / “earth and heavens” is present in the Hebrew and KJV. Do not assume the reversed order is a technical sequence without evidence.

## 2. Genesis 2:5 — Field growth, rain, and the absence of a man to work the ground

KJV:

> And every plant of the field before it was in the earth, and every herb of the field before it grew: for the LORD God had not caused it to rain upon the earth, and there was not a man to till the ground.

The Hebrew syntax is compact and the KJV wording is difficult in modern English. Modern translations often express the verse as “no shrub of the field had yet grown” and “no plant of the field had yet sprouted.” The NET notes explain the two negative clauses as background information, followed by causal clauses.

### 2.1 שִׂיחַ — *śîaḥ*, “plant/shrub of the field”

This noun is rare. It appears in Genesis 2:5 and in descriptions of vegetation in Genesis 21:15 and Job 30:4, 7. The NET notes suggest it refers to wild or uncultivated plants.

**Finding:** The precise botanical category is not certain. “Shrub,” “bush,” or “plant” are translation choices. Avoid assigning it a precise modern taxonomic group.

### 2.2 עֵשֶׂב — *‘ēśeb*, “herb/plant of the field”

This is a broader vegetation term used elsewhere in Genesis 1:11–12 and 1:29–30. The NET notes suggest that in this verse it may refer to cultivated grains or field vegetation.

**Finding:** The pairing of *śîaḥ* and *‘ēśeb* may distinguish types or contexts of vegetation, but the precise botanical division is debated. The text does not supply a modern ecological classification.

### 2.3 טֶרֶם — *ṭerem*, “before/not yet”

The term indicates that the described condition had not yet occurred at the relevant point in the narrative: the field growth had not yet appeared/sprouted.

**Finding:** Verse 5 describes a “not yet” condition. It should not be paraphrased as “no vegetation existed anywhere on Earth” without resolving the scope of “of the field” and the relation to Genesis 1:11–12.

### 2.4 מָטָר — *māṭār*, “rain”

The clause says the LORD God had not caused it to rain upon the earth. The text names rain as one of the absent conditions.

**Finding:** The verse explicitly says rain had not been sent/caused upon the earth in the described situation. It does not say that water was wholly absent; verse 6 immediately describes water rising from the earth and watering the ground.

### 2.5 אָדָם — *’ādām*, “man”

The clause says there was no man to work/till the ground. The word *’adam* is also connected in the narrative with *’adamah*, “ground/soil,” especially in Genesis 2:7.

**Finding:** The verse identifies the absence of a human worker as another condition associated with the field's not-yet growth. This anticipates the formation and placement of the man in verses 7–8 and his appointed work in verse 15.

### 2.6 עָבַד — *‘ābad*, “till/work”

The infinitive of *‘abad* appears here in relation to the ground. It is the same root used in Genesis 2:15 for the man's work in the Garden. Its range includes work and service; in an agricultural setting, till/cultivate is appropriate.

**Finding:** Genesis 2:5 and 2:15 use the same root for working/cultivating the ground/Garden. This is a meaningful lexical link within the chapter.

**Limit:** The shared root does not by itself prove that the two verses describe precisely the same task, location, or phase. The immediate objects and contexts differ: “the ground” in 2:5 and the Garden in 2:15.

## 3. Genesis 2:6 — The disputed word אֵד (*’ēd*)

KJV:

> But there went up a mist from the earth, and watered the whole face of the ground.

Hebrew:

> וְאֵד יַעֲלֶה מִן־הָאָרֶץ וְהִשְׁקָה אֶת־כָּל־פְּנֵי הָאֲדָמָה

### 3.1 אֵד — *’ēd*, rendered “mist”

This noun is rare, occurring in Genesis 2:6 and Job 36:27. Its precise meaning is disputed. The traditional “mist” follows an older interpretive tradition, while the Septuagint and Vulgate render it with a word meaning spring/fountain. Some modern notes discuss a possible cognate related to subterranean water or a spring/stream.

**Finding:** The exact identity of the water source is uncertain. The KJV's “mist” must be preserved as the project's base translation, while the lexical and translation uncertainty is recorded openly.

**Do not claim:** that the verse definitively describes modern atmospheric mist, a groundwater spring, a river system, or a particular hydrological cycle. The Hebrew noun does not settle that question.

### 3.2 עָלָה — *‘ālāh*, “went up/rose”

The verb means to go up or rise. The form in context has been understood as describing recurring or customary action: water rose from the earth.

### 3.3 שָׁקָה — *šāqāh*, “watered/gave drink”

The verb is causative in form and means to water or give drink. The verse states that the rising *’ēd* watered the whole surface of the ground.

**Textual finding:** Whatever the exact identity of *’ēd*, the narrative function is clear: it rises from the earth and waters the ground.

## 4. The sequence of Genesis 2:4–7

The passage gives the following progression:

1. **2:4 — Account heading:** the *toledot* of the heavens and earth; reference to their creation and God's making.
2. **2:5 — Not-yet conditions:** field vegetation has not yet appeared/grown; rain has not been caused; no man is present to work the ground.
3. **2:6 — Watering condition:** *’ēd* rises from the earth and waters the ground.
4. **2:7 — Formation:** the LORD God forms the man from the dust of the ground, breathes into his nostrils the breath of life, and the man becomes a living *nephesh*.

The text places the absence of a man to work the ground immediately before the account of God forming the man. It later states that God places the man in the Garden to work and keep it (2:15).

**Strong textual connection:** Genesis 2:5, 2:7, and 2:15 form a meaningful chapter-level sequence involving the ground, the man, and work.

**Limit:** Do not assume the narrative is giving a complete chronological account of every plant on the planet. The scope of “field vegetation,” the literary relation between Genesis 1 and 2, and the precise meaning of *’ēd* require continued investigation.

## 5. Genesis 1 and Genesis 2 — contradiction or change of focus?

Genesis 1:11–12 describes God commanding the earth to bring forth grass, herb yielding seed, and fruit tree yielding fruit; the earth brings forth vegetation. Genesis 2:5 describes field vegetation as not yet present/grown under stated conditions, including the absence of rain and a man to work the ground.

Several readings have been proposed:
- Genesis 2:5 refers to particular field plants or cultivated growth, not all vegetation.
- Genesis 2:4–7 retells or focuses on the creation account from the perspective of the ground and the formation of man.
- The passage describes a particular stage or condition associated with the Garden and the man.
- Other literary or chronological readings are possible and need to be evaluated.

**Current status:** UNRESOLVED. The text-first audit must not declare a contradiction merely by assuming “plant of the field” means every plant everywhere. It must also not declare complete harmony without demonstrating the scope and sequence from the text.

## 7. Questions carried forward

1. Does the *toledot* heading in Genesis 2:4 introduce what follows, summarize what precedes, or function as a hinge?
2. What is the precise scope of *śîaḥ* and *‘ēśeb* in Genesis 2:5?
3. Does “of the field” indicate a particular environment, uncultivated plants, cultivated crops, or a broader category?
4. How should Genesis 2:5 be related to the vegetation created in Genesis 1:11–12?
5. What is the best-supported meaning of *’ēd* in Genesis 2:6, given the Hebrew occurrence, Job 36:27, ancient versions, and cognate evidence?
6. Does the repeated *‘abad* root in Genesis 2:5 and 2:15 indicate a deliberate narrative link between the absence of a ground-worker and the man's later commission?
7. How does the ground (*’adamah*), man (*’adam*), and work/cultivation vocabulary contribute to the passage's literary structure?

## 8. Provisional conclusion

Genesis 2:4–6 describes an account heading, a field condition in which specified vegetation had not yet grown, the absence of rain, the absence of a man to work the ground, and a water source that rises and waters the ground. The exact scope of the vegetation terms and the precise meaning of *’ēd* remain open.

The same *‘abad* root appears in Genesis 2:5 and 2:15, creating a meaningful textual link between the absence of a man to work the ground and the later appointment of the man to work and keep the Garden. That connection is worth carrying into the GUA-W inquiry, but it must not be inflated into a complete theory of Purpose or Structure.

No canonical GUA-W node or relationship is changed by this audit.
