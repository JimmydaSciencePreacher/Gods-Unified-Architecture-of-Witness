# Investigation III.E — The Empty Tomb: What the Witnesses Found

**Primary texts:** Matthew 28:1–10; Mark 16:1–8; Luke 24:1–12; John 20:1–18 (KJV)  
**Related texts:** Matthew 27:57–66; Mark 15:42–47; Luke 23:50–56; John 19:38–42; John 2:19–22; John 10:17–18; 1 Corinthians 15:3–8  
**Status:** Four-Gospel comparative examination written; textual and historical questions identified; full Resurrection investigation remains open.

## 1. Investigative question

**What did the witnesses actually find when they came to the sepulchre, and what does the Written Word tell us about what was—and was not—there?**

The word “empty” is convenient shorthand, but it must be used precisely. John reports linen cloths and a separate face-cloth inside the tomb. The central claim is not that every object or occupant was absent; it is that Jesus' body was no longer there where it had been laid.

This investigation examines each Gospel account individually before considering their shared testimony. It does not assume a single harmonized chronology or treat the empty tomb alone as the entirety of the Resurrection claim.

## 2. Matthew 28:1–10 — The stone, the guard, and the announcement

Matthew names Mary Magdalene and the other Mary as coming to see the sepulchre after the sabbath, as it began to dawn toward the first day of the week.

Matthew records:

1. A great earthquake.
2. An angel of the Lord descending from heaven.
3. The angel rolling back the stone and sitting upon it.
4. The keepers shaking and becoming as dead men.
5. The angel telling the women, “Fear not ye: for I know that ye seek Jesus, which was crucified.”
6. The declaration, “He is not here: for he is risen, as he said.”
7. The invitation, “Come, see the place where the Lord lay.”
8. The instruction to go quickly and tell His disciples.
9. Jesus meeting the women; they hold Him by the feet and worship Him.

Matthew then records the chief priests and elders being informed by the guards. They give the soldiers money and instruct them to say that the disciples came by night and stole Jesus' body while they slept. Matthew records the resulting report being spread among the Jews.

**TEXT STATES:** Matthew does not merely report that the women found the stone moved. He records the angel's explicit Resurrection announcement, an invitation to inspect the place, the women's encounter with Jesus, and a competing explanation of the missing body attributed to the guards.

**DISTINCTIVE DETAIL:** Matthew alone records the guard narrative and the accusation that the disciples stole the body while the guards slept.

**LIMIT:** Matthew does not narrate the instant of Resurrection. The angel's descent and rolling back of the stone are narrated in connection with the women's visit, but the text does not explicitly say that Jesus waited inside until the stone was moved. Do not turn narrative sequence into an unreported account of the precise instant or mechanics of the Resurrection.

## 3. Mark 16:1–8 — The stone already moved and the young man

Mark names Mary Magdalene, Mary the mother of James, and Salome as bringing spices to anoint Jesus. They question who will roll away the stone. On arrival, they see that the stone has already been rolled away.

They enter the sepulchre and see a young man sitting on the right side, clothed in a long white garment. They are alarmed. He tells them:

- “Be not affrighted.”
- “Ye seek Jesus of Nazareth, which was crucified.”
- “He is risen; he is not here.”
- “Behold the place where they laid him.”

He instructs them to tell the disciples and Peter that Jesus goes before them into Galilee, as He had said. The women go out quickly and flee from the sepulchre; Mark records trembling and astonishment, and says they said nothing to any man because they were afraid.

**TEXT STATES:** The stone is already moved when the women arrive. The body is not there. The young man announces that Jesus is risen and directs them to the place where He had been laid. Mark 16:1–8 records no appearance of Jesus to the women within this passage.

**DISTINCTIVE DETAIL:** Mark names Salome, records the women's concern about moving the stone, identifies the messenger as a young man, and ends the passage with the women's fear and silence.

**TEXTUAL-CRITICAL BOUNDARY:** The KJV prints Mark 16:9–20. The longer ending has a well-known manuscript and literary question: Codices Vaticanus and Sinaiticus end Mark at 16:8, while other ancient witnesses preserve the longer ending, and early Christian writers provide evidence of its circulation. Scholars differ on whether the longer ending is original, lost from some manuscript lines, or a secondary ending. The KJV text is retained as the project's primary reading; the textual question is recorded openly. The central empty-tomb findings here are already present in Mark 16:1–8 and do not depend on resolving verses 9–20.

## 4. Luke 24:1–12 — The women, the absent body, and Peter's inspection

Luke says the women come very early on the first day of the week, bringing the spices they had prepared. They find the stone rolled away and enter, but do not find the body of the Lord Jesus.

Two men in shining garments stand by them. The women are afraid and bow their faces to the earth. The messengers ask why they seek the living among the dead and declare that Jesus is risen. They remind the women of Jesus' words in Galilee concerning the Son of man being delivered into the hands of sinful men, crucified, and rising again the third day.

Luke names Mary Magdalene, Joanna, Mary the mother of James, and other women as reporting these things to the apostles. The apostles do not believe their words, regarding them as idle tales. Peter rises and runs to the sepulchre; he stoops down and sees the linen clothes laid by themselves, then departs wondering at what had come to pass.

**TEXT STATES:** The women do not find Jesus' body. The messengers connect the event with Jesus' prior words. The women report to the apostles; their report is initially disbelieved. Peter inspects the tomb and sees linen clothes.

**DISTINCTIVE DETAIL:** Luke names Joanna and other women; records the reminder of Jesus' prior predictions; records the apostles' initial disbelief; and describes Peter departing in wonder after seeing the linen clothes.

**TEXTUAL NOTE:** Luke 24:12 is present in the KJV and many manuscripts, though its manuscript history has been discussed because it is absent from some important witnesses. Its content should be considered with that textual note rather than silently ignored or treated as beyond question. Luke 24:1–11 independently records the women's discovery and report.

## 5. John 20:1–10 — Mary, Peter, the other disciple, and the burial cloths

John begins with Mary Magdalene coming to the sepulchre early, “when it was yet dark.” She sees that the stone has been taken away and runs to Simon Peter and the other disciple, whom Jesus loved. Her report is: “They have taken away the Lord out of the sepulchre, and we know not where they have laid him.”

Peter and the other disciple run toward the tomb. The other disciple arrives first and stoops down, seeing the linen clothes lying, but does not enter. Peter arrives and enters; he sees the linen clothes and the napkin that had been about Jesus' head, not lying with the linen clothes but wrapped together in a place by itself. The other disciple then enters, sees, and believes. John immediately adds: “For as yet they knew not the scripture, that he must rise again from the dead.” The disciples then go away again unto their own home.

### The sequence in John's account

1. Mary Magdalene arrives while it is still dark and sees the stone removed.
2. She reports that the Lord has been taken away; at this point her words show that she has not yet understood the event as Resurrection.
3. Peter and the other disciple run to the tomb.
4. The other disciple arrives first, looks in, and sees the linen clothes.
5. Peter enters and sees the linen clothes and the separate napkin.
6. The other disciple enters, sees, and believes.
7. John says they did not yet know the Scripture that Jesus must rise from the dead.
8. The two disciples return home.
9. John 20:11–18 then continues with Mary remaining outside, weeping, seeing two angels, and encountering Jesus, whom she initially supposes to be the gardener until He calls her by name.

### What John actually says about the cloths

The KJV says the napkin was “not lying with the linen clothes, but wrapped together in a place by itself.” This is a description of the cloth's position and condition in John's narrative.

It does **not** explicitly say:
- that the linen cloths retained the exact shape of Jesus' body;
- that the body passed through the cloths;
- that the napkin was folded into a special signal or coded message;
- that the cloths prove a particular physical mechanism;
- that the disciples immediately understood the full meaning of what they saw.

The Greek term translated “napkin” (soudarion) refers to a cloth used around the head or face; its exact form and use in this burial are not fully described. The wording translated “wrapped together” has generated interpretive debate. We must not turn a disputed translation nuance into an established physical reconstruction.

### What did the beloved disciple believe?

John 20:8 says the other disciple “saw, and believed.” The immediate object of that belief is debated. Some interpret it as belief in the Resurrection; others argue that verse 9's statement that they did not yet know the Scripture about rising from the dead may indicate a more limited belief, perhaps accepting Mary's report that the body was gone. The narrative does not explicitly spell out the full content of the belief in verse 8.

**TEXT STATES:** The disciple sees and believes; the next verse says they did not yet know the Scripture that Jesus must rise from the dead. Both statements must be preserved. The investigation should not settle the ambiguity by simply quoting verse 8 and omitting verse 9.

## 6. Four-Gospel comparison: what is shared and what differs

**Comparison rule:** A detail reported by one Gospel and not mentioned by another is not automatically a contradiction. A contradiction claim requires showing that two accounts make mutually exclusive claims about the same point, with the relevant narrative scope established. Conversely, one must not claim that every difference is automatically harmonized; some sequence and detail questions remain open.

## 7. What does “the empty tomb” mean?

The phrase “empty tomb” can obscure John's details if taken to mean that absolutely nothing remained inside the burial place. John explicitly reports linen cloths and a separate napkin. Luke 24:12 also reports linen cloths.

For this investigation, “empty tomb” means **the absence of Jesus' body from the place where He had been laid**, not the absence of every object or every person from the tomb.

The distinction matters:

- Matthew, Mark, and Luke explicitly record that the body is not there / the place where He lay is empty of His body.
- John describes the body as absent while reporting the remaining burial cloths.
- The messenger(s) interpret the absence through the declaration that Jesus is risen.
- The later appearances provide testimony beyond the tomb itself.

A modern scholarly discussion has questioned whether “empty tomb” is an overly broad phrase for first-century family tombs, which could contain other burials. That critique is useful as a reminder to state the claim precisely: the Gospel claim concerns the absence of Jesus' body from the specific place where He had been laid. It does not require claiming that the entire rock-cut tomb contained no other material or burial spaces.

## 8. Competing explanation in Matthew: theft allegation

Matthew 28:11–15 records a specific counterclaim: the chief priests and elders pay the soldiers to say that Jesus' disciples stole the body while the guards slept.

This report must be separated into its components:

**TEXT STATES:**
- The guards report to the chief priests what happened.
- The chief priests and elders consult.
- They give the soldiers money.
- They instruct the soldiers to claim that the disciples stole the body while they slept.
- Matthew says this report was commonly spread among the Jews at the time of his writing.

**LOGICAL OBSERVATION:** The proposed explanation presupposes that the body is missing; it disputes the cause of the absence. It does not claim that the body remained in the tomb.

**LIMIT:** Matthew is the sole canonical Gospel recording this guard-and-bribery episode. The other Gospels do not independently narrate the guards. Historical evaluation of the episode must therefore distinguish Matthew's testimony from independently corroborated details. The narrative does not provide a modern transcript or independent record of the soldiers' later statements.

## 9. Historical and textual-critical assessment

### A. The women as first discoverers

The Gospel accounts place women at the discovery of the tomb, with Mary Magdalene central in all four. Historical scholars have debated the implications of this testimony. One argument is that women being named as the initial discoverers is not an obvious invention designed to maximize public credibility in the ancient setting. This is an argument about historical plausibility, not a proof by itself. It must be weighed with the sources, their dates, literary aims, and transmission.

The texts also record that the women's report was initially doubted by the apostles (Luke 24:11). This is part of the testimony and should not be omitted when assessing how the narratives portray the first report.

### B. The burial cloths

John's cloth details are a distinctive part of the account. Scholarship notes that the exact meaning of the terms and the arrangement of the cloths are debated. The text supports saying that the cloths remained and that the head-cloth was in a distinct position. It does not support a precise reconstruction of how the body left the cloths.

The cloths are narrative evidence within John's account, not independently available archaeological artifacts. No surviving burial cloth is identified by John as an artifact recovered from the tomb.

### C. The textual question in Mark

The KJV includes Mark 16:9–20. Many modern critical editions mark the longer ending as textually disputed because it is absent from some important early Greek witnesses, including Codices Sinaiticus and Vaticanus, while it is present in many later manuscripts and known in early Christian tradition. Scholarly explanations differ. The investigation must accurately report both the manuscript evidence and the existence of serious arguments for the longer ending's antiquity or originality. No conclusion about the empty tomb in Mark 16:1–8 depends on resolving that debate.

### D. Historical limits

No identified archaeological object from Jesus' tomb is available to independently verify the exact location, the body’s absence, or the cloth arrangement described in the Gospels. Archaeology can illuminate first-century tombs, burial customs, and material settings; it cannot simply identify an unmarked or disputed tomb as Jesus' and certify what was or was not inside it on a particular morning.

Historical analysis can compare the written sources, their relationship, and the development of Resurrection proclamation. It cannot reproduce the past event experimentally. The claim of Resurrection must be assessed as a historical and theological claim, while the limits of each method remain explicit.

## 10. Witness, registration, and agency

The narratives distinguish the event from the people who encounter and report it:

- **The women:** come to the tomb, see the stone moved, encounter messengers, and report what they find and hear.
- **Mary Magdalene:** initially interprets the absent body as removal; later encounters Jesus and recognizes Him when He calls her by name (John 20:11–18).
- **Peter:** enters and inspects the tomb; Luke records his wonder, John records his inspection of the linen cloths and napkin.
- **The other disciple:** sees the linen cloths, enters, and believes; John also records that the disciples had not yet understood the Scripture concerning the Resurrection.
- **The angelic messengers / young man:** announce that Jesus is risen and direct the visitors to the place where He had lain.
- **The guards in Matthew:** are witnesses to the events around the tomb in Matthew's narrative and later become the subject of the proposed theft explanation.
- **The chief priests and elders in Matthew:** respond to the report with a competing explanation and payment.
- **The Gospel writers:** preserve distinct accounts and emphases.

**GUA-W distinction:** The witnesses do not cause the absence of the body or the Resurrection. They register what they encounter and report what they are told or understand. Their initial interpretations are not all the same: Mary supposes removal; the women in Luke are reminded of Jesus' words; the disciples initially disbelieve; the beloved disciple sees and believes while the text says they do not yet understand the Scripture.

This is a particularly important distinction between an event, its registration, an interpretation of what was registered, and subsequent testimony.

## 11. Canonical context

The empty-tomb narratives explicitly recall Jesus' prior words:

- Matthew 28:6 — “He is risen, as he said.”
- Mark 16:7 — “as he said unto you.”
- Luke 24:6–8 — the messengers remind the women of Jesus' words in Galilee; the women remember His words.
- John 2:19–22 — Jesus' saying concerning the temple of His body is remembered after He is risen.
- John 10:17–18 — Jesus declares His authority to lay down His life and take it again.
- 1 Corinthians 15:3–8 — Paul joins death, burial, Resurrection, and appearances in the received proclamation.

**Canonical observation:** The empty tomb is not presented as an isolated puzzle. The Gospel accounts connect it to Jesus' prior declarations and the subsequent encounters with Him. The apostolic proclamation likewise joins burial and Resurrection rather than treating the missing body as the whole message.

## 12. Relationship to the completed GUA-W

The completed GUA-W remains unchanged. The established governing order remains:

**Author → Word → Creation → Witness**

The relevant observation is not that a new architecture stage has been discovered. It is that the narratives visibly distinguish:

1. **Prior Word:** Jesus' earlier declarations concerning His death and rising.
2. **Recorded event:** the tomb is found with Jesus' body absent.
3. **Registration:** women and disciples see the moved stone, absent body, and (in Luke and John) burial cloths.
4. **Initial interpretation:** Mary supposes removal; the messengers announce Resurrection; the disciples respond with differing degrees of understanding and belief.
5. **Testimony:** the women report; the disciples inspect; the message is carried onward.

This is a descriptive comparison with the established Witness distinction, not a new runtime sequence or Gear. The event is not caused by the witnesses' observation, and their first interpretations do not determine what happened.

Jesus Christ is not reduced to a component of GUA-W. The completed architecture is not being reconstructed from this episode.

## 13. Triple Constraint Law

### Biblical compliance — PASS for the recorded empty-tomb testimony

All four Gospels report the discovery that Jesus' body is no longer in the place where He was laid. Their individual details, differences, and distinctive claims are preserved. John’s burial cloth description and the immediate qualification in John 20:9 are not omitted. The KJV longer ending of Mark is retained while its textual question is identified.

### Scientific coherence — PARTIAL; historical assessment remains contested and bounded

Historical methods can examine source relationships, the social setting, burial practices, the role of women in the narratives, and the early Resurrection proclamation. Archaeology can illuminate tombs and burial customs but cannot independently identify the tomb or verify its contents on the reported morning. The burial cloths are not available as independently authenticated artifacts. Historical interpretations of the empty-tomb tradition differ. No laboratory mechanism is asserted.

### GUA-W consistency — PASS at governing level; no new Gear established

The distinction between event, witness registration, interpretation, and testimony is consistent with the established derivative Witness principle. The witnesses do not author or cause the event. The completed GUA-W remains unchanged. This episode may furnish an example for examining the Witness distinction, but it does not by itself establish a new architecture component.

## 14. Findings

1. **All four Gospel accounts report Jesus' body absent from the place where He was buried.**
2. **“Empty tomb” must be defined carefully.** John and Luke report burial cloths remaining; the claim concerns the absence of Jesus' body, not the absence of all objects.
3. **Matthew alone records the guards and the bribery/theft allegation.** The allegation is a competing explanation for the body's absence, not a denial that the body was missing in Matthew's narrative.
4. **Mark's account ends at 16:8 in a major early manuscript tradition.** The KJV includes 16:9–20; the longer ending's textual history remains a separate question.
5. **Luke records the women's report being disbelieved and Peter inspecting the tomb.**
6. **John supplies the most detailed account of the linen cloths and the separate head-cloth.** The exact arrangement and its physical significance remain disputed; popular reconstructions must not be presented as the text itself.
7. **John 20:8–9 must be read together.** The beloved disciple sees and believes, while the narrator says the disciples did not yet know the Scripture that Jesus must rise from the dead.
8. **The narratives distinguish observation from interpretation.** Mary initially supposes that the body has been taken; the messengers announce Resurrection; the disciples' understanding develops.
9. **The empty tomb is not the whole Resurrection testimony.** The appearances of the risen Jesus are separately recorded and will receive their own dedicated examination.
10. **No direct archaeological artifact independently verifies the reported tomb contents.** Historical inquiry remains possible, but claims must stay within the evidence.

## 15. Unresolved questions

1. What is the most responsible account of the differences in the women named and the times described by the four Gospels?
2. How should the angel / young man descriptions be compared without assuming that every narrative detail is identical?
3. What is the best-supported textual history of Mark 16:9–20, and what conclusions depend on that passage?
4. What exactly does John mean by the head-cloth being “wrapped together in a place by itself”?
5. What does the beloved disciple believe in John 20:8, especially in light of verse 9?
6. What can be established historically about the guard and theft allegation in Matthew 28:11–15?
7. How should the women's testimony and the apostles' initial disbelief be evaluated historically?
8. What can first-century tomb archaeology clarify about the setting, and what remains inaccessible to archaeology?
9. How do the empty-tomb narratives relate to the appearance traditions without forcing a premature chronology?
10. What is the relation between the absence of the body, the remaining cloths, and the canonical claim that Jesus was raised?

## 16. Provisional disposition

**Four-Gospel empty-tomb testimony:** WRITTEN.  
**Textual differences:** PRESERVED; further harmonization UNRESOLVED.  
**Mark 16:9–20:** TEXTUAL-CRITICAL QUESTION OPEN; KJV reading retained.  
**Burial-cloth interpretation:** PARTIAL; exact arrangement and physical significance UNRESOLVED.  
**Historical assessment:** IN PROGRESS; no direct archaeological verification of the specific tomb contents.  
**GUA-W consistency:** PASS at governing level; no new Gear established.

The next dedicated study must move from the tomb to the appearances of the risen Christ. The appearances are not to be used to erase the details of the tomb, nor is the tomb to be treated as a substitute for the appearance testimony.

**The body was buried. The witnesses came. The body was not there. The cloths remained in John's account. The messengers announced that Jesus was risen. The witnesses then had to understand, report, and encounter what followed.**
