# Investigation III.2 — The Breath of the Risen Christ

**Primary texts:** Genesis 2:7; Ezekiel 37:1–14; John 1:1–4; 3:5–8; 7:37–39; 14–16; 19:30, 34–35; 20:19–23; Acts 1–2; Romans 8:11; 1 Corinthians 15:42–49 (KJV).  
**Related texts:** Luke 24:36–43; John 10:17–18; 1 Peter 3:18; Matthew 16:19; 18:18; Luke 24:46–49; Acts 2:32–39.  
**Status:** TEXT-FIRST CANONICAL INVESTIGATION AND INITIAL SCHOLARLY INTERPRETIVE AUDIT WRITTEN; the precise relationship between John 20:22 and the Pentecostal outpouring in Acts 2 remains unresolved. The scholarly audit maps major interpretive questions and positions but is not an exhaustive history of interpretation or a final adjudication.

## 1. Governing question

**What is the relationship between God's breathing life into Adam, the risen Christ breathing upon His disciples, and the reception of the Holy Ghost?**

The investigation follows the wording and sequence of the KJV texts, examines the Hebrew and Greek terms, compares the canonical witnesses, and then tests each conclusion under the Triple Constraint Law. It does not assume that the events are identical merely because they share breath-language. It does not invent a physical mechanism for the giving of the Spirit.

The governing order remains:

**Author → Word → Creation → Witness**

God alone is Original Cause. Jesus Christ is the Word made flesh, not a component of GUA-W. The Spirit is not reduced to a physical force or an architectural metaphor. The disciples receive and testify; they do not originate the life, authority, or message they bear.

## 2. Genesis 2:7 — God gives life to the formed man

KJV: “And the LORD God formed man of the dust of the ground, and breathed into his nostrils the breath of life; and man became a living soul.”

### Hebrew observations

- **יָצַר — yāṣar:** to form or fashion. The verse first identifies the LORD God as the agent who forms man from the dust.
- **נָפַח — nāphaḥ:** to breathe or blow. The form in Genesis 2:7 is וַיִּפַּח (*wayyippaḥ*), “and [He] breathed.”
- **נְשָׁמָה — nĕšāmāh:** breath. In the phrase נִשְׁמַת חַיִּים (*nišmat ḥayyîm*), “breath of life.”
- **חַיִּים — ḥayyîm:** life / living.
- **נֶפֶשׁ חַיָּה — nepeš ḥayyāh:** living being, living creature, traditionally “living soul” in the KJV.

### What the text states

1. God forms man from the dust.
2. God breathes into man's nostrils the breath of life.
3. Man becomes a living being.

The grammatical and narrative agency is clear: God gives; man becomes alive. The verse does not describe man as the source of his own life.

### Necessary qualification

The expression *nepeš ḥayyāh* is not exclusive to human beings. Related wording describes living creatures in Genesis 1:20, 1:24, and 2:19. Genesis 2:7 must not be made to say that animals are not living beings, nor should “living soul” be turned into an unsupported account of a detachable substance. The distinctive statement here is the narrated act of God forming the man and breathing into him.

## 3. Ezekiel 37:1–14 — breath enters the dead

Ezekiel is carried by the Spirit of the LORD into a valley full of dry bones. The LORD asks whether the bones can live. Ezekiel is commanded to prophesy.

The sequence in the vision is significant:

1. The prophet speaks as commanded.
2. There is a noise and shaking; bones come together.
3. Sinews, flesh, and skin appear.
4. Yet “there was no breath in them” (v. 8).
5. Ezekiel is commanded to prophesy to the breath.
6. Breath comes into them; they live and stand upon their feet (vv. 9–10).
7. The LORD interprets the vision: “These bones are the whole house of Israel” (v. 11).
8. The LORD promises to open their graves, bring them into the land, and put His Spirit in them (vv. 12–14).

### Hebrew word and context

**רוּחַ — rûaḥ** can mean wind, breath, or spirit. The word's range does not mean that every occurrence has all meanings simultaneously. The immediate context and the LORD's interpretation govern its sense. In this passage the imagery deliberately moves among breath, wind, and Spirit, culminating in God's promise: “I shall put my spirit in you, and ye shall live” (v. 14).

### What this adds

Ezekiel 37 is not merely an account of biological respiration. The LORD explicitly identifies the bones with the whole house of Israel and the vision with restoration from a condition described as hopeless. The vision uses the life-giving imagery of breath to witness to God's power to restore His people.

**Canonical relationship:** Genesis 2:7 presents the giving of life to the formed man; Ezekiel 37 presents the LORD's promised restoration of Israel through the imagery of breath entering the dead. They are related witnesses to God's life-giving agency, but they are not the same event.

## 4. John 1:1–4 — life is in the Word

John opens by declaring that the Word was in the beginning, was with God, and was God. All things were made by Him; without Him was not any thing made that was made. “In him was life; and the life was the light of men” (v. 4).

John 1:14 identifies this Word: “the Word was made flesh, and dwelt among us.”

This passage prevents a mistaken reading of the later breath event. Jesus is not presented as a created instrument who independently manufactures spiritual life. John identifies Him with the Word through whom all things were made and in whom life is. The risen Christ's action in John 20 must be read in continuity with this identity, not as a detached technique.

**GUA-W boundary:** This is a canonical identification of Christ in relation to God and Creation. It does not make Jesus a component of the map.

## 5. John 3:5–8 — birth of the Spirit and the wind analogy

Jesus tells Nicodemus that a man must be born of water and of the Spirit. He distinguishes flesh born of flesh from spirit born of the Spirit. He then says:

“The wind bloweth where it listeth, and thou hearest the sound thereof, but canst not tell whence it cometh, and whither it goeth: so is every one that is born of the Spirit” (John 3:8).

The Greek **πνεῦμα — pneuma** can mean wind, breath, or spirit. John uses the word in a context where the wind analogy illuminates the Spirit's work. The analogy concerns the unseen source and direction of the wind and the discernible sound/effect; it does not identify the Holy Spirit as merely atmospheric movement.

**Text states:** new birth is of the Spirit; the Spirit's work is not controlled or fully traced by the human observer.

**Limit:** The passage does not give a physical mechanism for spiritual birth.

## 6. John 7:37–39 — the promised Spirit and Jesus' glorification

At the feast, Jesus invites the thirsty to come to Him and drink. He speaks of living water flowing from the believer. John then supplies the interpretation: Jesus spoke of the Spirit, whom believers should receive; “for the Holy Ghost was not yet given; because that Jesus was not yet glorified.”

This note is decisive for the sequence. John explicitly relates the promised reception of the Spirit to Jesus' glorification. It does not authorize collapsing every later event into one moment. Death, Resurrection, exaltation/glorification, and the Spirit's reception must be distinguished while their canonical relationship is examined.

## 7. John 14–16 — the Spirit's testimony and work

Jesus' farewell discourse supplies the interpretive frame for the promised Comforter:

- **John 14:16–17:** The Father gives “another Comforter,” the Spirit of truth, who dwells with and shall be in the disciples.
- **John 14:26:** The Comforter, the Holy Ghost, whom the Father sends in Jesus' name, teaches and brings Jesus' words to remembrance.
- **John 15:26:** The Spirit of truth proceeds from the Father and is sent by Jesus; He testifies of Christ.
- **John 16:7:** Jesus says His departure is connected with the Comforter's coming.
- **John 16:8–11:** The Spirit reproves the world concerning sin, righteousness, and judgment.
- **John 16:13:** The Spirit of truth guides into all truth and speaks what He hears.
- **John 16:14–15:** He glorifies Christ and receives of Christ's and shows it to the disciples.

The Spirit's work is not an independent rival message. The text repeatedly relates His work to the Father, the Son, Christ's words, and testimony concerning Christ. The disciples are receivers and witnesses of what is given.

## 8. John 19:30, 34–35 — death must remain distinct from the later breathing

John 19:30 says Jesus bowed His head and “gave up the ghost.” The Greek expression is **παρέδωκεν τὸ πνεῦμα — paredōken to pneuma**, literally “he handed over/yielded the spirit.” The KJV renders it “gave up the ghost.”

John 19:34 then records the soldier piercing Jesus' side, with blood and water coming out; verse 35 emphasizes the testimony of the one who saw it.

These statements belong to John's account of the Crucifixion and death. They must not be equated automatically with John 20:22. John 19 records Jesus' death; John 20 records the risen Jesus appearing to His disciples and breathing upon them.

**Limit:** The text does not say that the blood and water physically produce the Holy Ghost, nor does it supply a complete medical explanation of the phenomenon. Such claims are not admitted.

## 9. John 20:19–23 — the risen Christ breathes upon the disciples

The setting and order matter:

1. It is the first day of the week, the same day on which the risen Jesus has been encountered.
2. The disciples are assembled with the doors shut for fear.
3. Jesus comes and stands in their midst, saying, “Peace be unto you.”
4. He shows them His hands and His side; the disciples are glad when they see the Lord.
5. Jesus again says, “Peace be unto you,” and declares, “as my Father hath sent me, even so send I you” (v. 21).
6. He then breathes upon them and says, “Receive ye the Holy Ghost” (v. 22).
7. He speaks of remitting and retaining sins (v. 23).

### The Greek wording

John 20:22:

**καὶ τοῦτο εἰπὼν ἐνεφύσησεν καὶ λέγει αὐτοῖς· λάβετε πνεῦμα ἅγιον**

Transliteration: *kai touto eipōn enephysēsen kai legei autois: labete pneuma hagion.*

- **ἐνεφύσησεν — enephysēsen:** “he breathed.” From **ἐμφυσάω — emphysaō**, to breathe into/upon.
- **λάβετε — labete:** “receive” or “take” (imperative plural).
- **πνεῦμα — pneuma:** spirit/breath.
- **ἅγιον — hagion:** holy.

### The Genesis 2:7 correspondence

The verb **ἐνεφύσησεν** is especially important. It is the same Greek verb used in the Septuagint (LXX) translation of Genesis 2:7 for God's breathing into Adam. It is a distinctive lexical correspondence, not merely a general similarity between two passages that happen to mention breath. The verb is rare in the New Testament and occurs here in John 20:22.

The Greek Old Testament also uses this breathing vocabulary in Ezekiel 37's vision. Thus John 20:22 stands in a recognizable Scriptural field that includes the creation of Adam and the prophetic vision of life/restoration through breath.

**Supported inference:** John deliberately evokes the Scriptural creation/breath motif. In the immediate context, the risen Jesus breathes upon the disciples and commands them to receive the Holy Ghost. The scene therefore carries new-creation resonance.

**Boundary:** The correspondence does not make the disciples new Adams in the same sense as Christ, nor does it make the events identical. Genesis 2:7 describes God's giving life to the formed man; John 20:22 describes the risen Christ's action toward His commissioned disciples and the reception of the Holy Ghost.

### Did the disciples receive the Holy Ghost at that moment?

Two major readings must be represented fairly:

1. **Actual impartation on the Resurrection day:** Jesus' imperative “Receive ye the Holy Ghost,” following His breathing, is read as the actual giving/receiving of the Spirit. Pentecost then represents a distinct subsequent public outpouring, empowerment, or manifestation for witness.
2. **Enacted sign or anticipatory promise:** The breathing is read as a symbolic or prophetic action pointing forward to the fuller reception/empowerment at Pentecost in Acts 2.

The wording strongly establishes that Jesus intentionally joins His breathing with the command to receive the Holy Ghost. It does not, by itself, settle every theological question about the exact relation between this moment and Acts 2. John 7:39, John 14–16, John 20:22, and Acts 1–2 must be held together.

**Status of the exact relation:** UNRESOLVED as a matter of interpretive chronology and theological description. Do not erase John 20:22 to protect a preferred reading of Pentecost; do not erase Acts 2 to make John 20:22 the whole account.

## 10. John 20:23 — authority follows reception and sending

Jesus' statement about remitting and retaining sins follows immediately after the breathing and command to receive the Holy Ghost. It must be studied in the context of the sending in verse 21 and alongside Matthew 16:19; 18:18; Luke 24:46–49; and Acts 2:38.

The text gives the disciples a commission and speaks of their authority in relation to sins. It does not make them independent sources of forgiveness or grant them authority to alter the Gospel. The wider canonical witness places forgiveness in the proclamation of repentance and remission in Christ's name (Luke 24:46–49; Acts 2:38), under the authority of the risen Lord.

**Interpretive caution:** Christian traditions differ on the precise ecclesial and sacramental implications of John 20:23. This investigation records the immediate context and canonical links without claiming to settle every denominational dispute.

## 11. Acts 1–2 — promise, Ascension, outpouring, and witness

### Acts 1

- **Acts 1:3:** Jesus shows Himself alive after His Passion by many infallible proofs, appearing over forty days and speaking of the kingdom of God.
- **Acts 1:4–5:** He commands the disciples to wait for the promise of the Father and recalls the promise of baptism with the Holy Ghost.
- **Acts 1:8:** “Ye shall receive power, after that the Holy Ghost is come upon you: and ye shall be witnesses unto me” in Jerusalem, Judaea, Samaria, and the uttermost part of the earth.
- **Acts 1:9–11:** Jesus is taken up; the disciples are given the promise of His return.

The Spirit's coming is expressly connected with power for witness. The disciples do not author the testimony; they are commissioned to bear witness to Jesus.

### Acts 2

- A sound from heaven comes “as of a rushing mighty wind” (v. 2).
- Cloven tongues “like as of fire” appear (v. 3).
- They are all filled with the Holy Ghost and speak with other tongues as the Spirit gives them utterance (v. 4).
- Peter interprets the event through Joel's prophecy (vv. 16–21).
- Peter proclaims Jesus' death and Resurrection, declaring that God raised Him up (vv. 22–32).
- Peter says Jesus, being exalted and having received of the Father the promise of the Holy Ghost, “hath shed forth this, which ye now see and hear” (v. 33).

The expressions “as of” and “like as” mark comparisons in the description of sound and appearance. The text does not say the Holy Ghost is merely wind or fire.

### The sequence established by Acts

The narrative gives this order: the risen Jesus instructs His disciples; the disciples wait for the Father's promise; Jesus ascends; the Holy Ghost comes upon them; they speak as the Spirit gives utterance; Peter bears witness to Jesus' Resurrection and exaltation.

Acts 2:32–33 is especially important: the apostles are witnesses that God raised Jesus, and the exalted Jesus is the One who sheds forth what the crowd sees and hears. Resurrection, exaltation, the promised Spirit, and public witness are explicitly related.

### Relation to John 20:22

Acts 1–2 does not explicitly say, “John 20:22 was only symbolic,” nor does it explicitly say, “the disciples received nothing in John 20 and received the Spirit for the first time at Pentecost.” Those are interpretive conclusions. The two passages must be read without forcing either to cancel the other.

A possible distinction is between the Resurrection-day impartation/commission in John and the later corporate outpouring and public empowerment in Acts. That distinction is a plausible harmonizing interpretation, not an explicit sentence in either passage. It remains a working interpretation, not a settled textual fact.

## 12. Romans 8:11 — the Spirit who raised Jesus gives life

KJV: “But if the Spirit of him that raised up Jesus from the dead dwell in you, he that raised up Christ from the dead shall also quicken your mortal bodies by his Spirit that dwelleth in you.”

The Greek verb translated “quicken” is **ζῳοποιήσει — zōopoiēsei**, from **ζῳοποιέω — zōopoieō**, to make alive or give life.

Paul relates:
- the Spirit to the raising of Jesus from the dead;
- the Spirit's indwelling in believers;
- God's promised giving of life to their mortal bodies.

The verse does not merely use “breath” as a loose metaphor. It gives a canonical statement about the Spirit, Christ's Resurrection, indwelling, and future life. The exact scope and timing of the quickening are considered within Romans 8 and the wider resurrection teaching, not reduced to present emotional renewal alone.

## 13. 1 Corinthians 15:45–49 — first Adam and last Adam

Paul quotes the Genesis description: “The first man Adam was made a living soul.” He then contrasts it with: “the last Adam was made a quickening spirit” (v. 45).

The surrounding argument concerns resurrection, the natural body and spiritual body, the earthly and heavenly, and the image borne by those who belong to Christ.

This is a major canonical bridge between Genesis 2:7 and Christ's Resurrection. Paul explicitly places Adam and Christ in a representative contrast: the first Adam is associated with the natural/earthly order; Christ, the last Adam, is associated with life-giving resurrection.

### Important safeguards

- “Quickening spirit” must not be used to claim that the risen Jesus is immaterial or lacks a body. Luke 24:39 records Jesus saying, “a spirit hath not flesh and bones, as ye see me have.”
- “Spiritual body” in 1 Corinthians 15 must not be silently converted into “nonphysical body.” Paul's argument is about the body raised and transformed.
- The text does not say that Christ is merely another Adam or simply repeats Adam's creation. “Last Adam” marks a distinctive and climactic relation in Paul's argument.

**Supported canonical relationship:** Genesis 2:7 and 1 Corinthians 15:45 are explicitly joined by Paul; Christ is the last Adam, and His life-giving work belongs to the resurrection order.

## 14. Consolidated Scriptural sequence

The texts establish a connected testimony while preserving the distinctions between events:

1. **Genesis 2:7 — Creation:** God forms man and gives him life by breathing into him.
2. **Ezekiel 37:1–14 — Prophetic restoration:** the LORD uses breath/Spirit imagery to promise life and restoration to the whole house of Israel.
3. **John 1:1–4, 14 — Identity of the Word:** life is in the Word; the Word is made flesh.
4. **John 7:37–39; 14–16 — Promise:** Jesus promises the Spirit and explains the Spirit's relation to the Father, the Son, truth, remembrance, and testimony.
5. **John 19:30 — Death:** Jesus gives up the ghost; this is not the same narrated event as John 20:22.
6. **John 20:19–23 — Risen Christ and commission:** Jesus appears, shows His wounds, sends the disciples, breathes upon them, and commands them to receive the Holy Ghost.
7. **Acts 1 — Waiting and Ascension:** Jesus instructs the disciples and commands them to wait for the Father's promise; He ascends.
8. **Acts 2 — Outpouring and witness:** the Spirit comes; the disciples speak as given utterance; Peter testifies to the Resurrection and exaltation of Jesus.
9. **Romans 8:11 — Indwelling and promised life:** the Spirit associated with Jesus' Resurrection dwells in believers and is related to the future quickening of their mortal bodies.
10. **1 Corinthians 15:45–49 — Adam/Christ relationship:** Paul explicitly connects the first Adam's living-soul description with Christ as the last Adam, the life-giving Spirit.

| Acts 2 connects the Spirit's coming with witness to the risen/exalted Jesus | PASS | Acts 1:8 and 2:32–33 state this directly |

| 1 Corinthians 15:45 explicitly joins Adam and Christ in a last-Adam contrast | PASS | Paul's direct quotation and argument |
| The Holy Ghost is merely physical wind or breath | FAIL | The canonical texts describe the Spirit as personal divine agent who teaches, testifies, guides, and glorifies Christ |

### B. Scientific coherence

**PASS within linguistic, textual, and historical scope:** The Hebrew and Greek terms can be examined, their occurrences compared, and the literary/canonical correspondences described without claiming more than the evidence establishes.

**PARTIAL / domain boundary:** The biblical texts do not provide a reproducible physical mechanism for the giving of the Holy Ghost or for resurrection life. Experimental physiology can describe ordinary respiration; it cannot convert the theological claim into a measured respiratory process merely because the text uses breath-language.

The investigation therefore distinguishes:
- ordinary physical breathing;
- breath as a Scriptural image of life;
- the Spirit as presented by the biblical texts;
- the historical claim of the Resurrection;
- the theological claim of the Spirit's impartation and indwelling.

Scientific coherence requires these categories not be confused. The absence of an experimentally specified mechanism is not itself a disproof of the Scriptural claim, and it is not permission to invent one.

### C. GUA-W consistency

**PASS:** The governing hierarchy remains intact. God is Original Cause; the Word is not a created source; life is received; the risen Christ commissions; the Spirit empowers and testifies; disciples bear witness. No new layer or Gear is proposed. No witness is promoted to Author.

### Overall disposition

The central Scriptural relationships pass the Triple Constraint within their stated scope. The exact interpretation of John 20:22 in relation to Pentecost remains unresolved. No proposed physical mechanism is admitted. No new GUA-W Gear is established by this investigation.

## 17. Findings, limits, and questions retained

### Findings supported by the texts

1. Genesis 2:7 presents God as the giver of life to the formed man.
2. Ezekiel 37 uses breath/Spirit imagery to describe the LORD's promised restoration of Israel.
3. John 1 identifies life as being in the Word and identifies the Word as made flesh.
4. John 7 and John 14–16 connect the promised Spirit with Jesus' glorification, the Father's giving, Christ's sending, and the Spirit's testimony.
5. John 20:22 uses the distinctive Greek breathing verb found in the LXX of Genesis 2:7.
6. The risen Christ joins His breathing with the command to receive the Holy Ghost and with the sending of His disciples.
7. Acts 1–2 connects the promised Spirit with power for witness and the public testimony that God raised and exalted Jesus.
8. Romans 8:11 connects the Spirit with Christ's Resurrection, indwelling, and future quickening.
9. 1 Corinthians 15:45–49 explicitly connects Adam and Christ through the first-Adam / last-Adam contrast.

### Questions retained as unresolved

- Does John 20:22 describe an actual impartation of the Holy Ghost on the Resurrection day, an enacted sign anticipating Pentecost, or a relation best described through both an initial reception and a later distinct empowerment?
- How should the timing and theological relation of John 20:22, Acts 1:4–8, and Acts 2 be expressed without suppressing any passage?
- What is the precise relation between the authority in John 20:23 and the proclamation of repentance and remission in Luke 24:46–49 and Acts 2:38?
- What further intertextual significance, if any, should be assigned to Ezekiel 37 in John's presentation beyond the shared breath/Spirit field and new-life imagery?
- What does the wider Pauline argument establish about the relation between the indwelling Spirit now and the future quickening of mortal bodies?

### Claims not admitted

- That ordinary inhalation/exhalation is itself the Holy Ghost.
- That the blood and water in John 19:34 physically generated or transmitted the Holy Ghost.
- That John 20:22 and Acts 2 must be identical in every respect.
- That John 20:22 can be ignored because Pentecost occurs later.
- That “quickening spirit” means the risen Christ is an immaterial being without a body.
- That the disciples become independent sources or authors of spiritual life, forgiveness, or revelation.
- That this study establishes a new GUA-W layer, Gear, or mechanism.

## 18. Concluding assessment

The strongest discovery in this investigation is not a loose metaphor. It is a direct lexical correspondence: the Greek verb John uses when the risen Christ breathes upon His disciples is the same distinctive verb used in the Greek translation of Genesis 2:7 for God's breathing into Adam. Ezekiel 37 supplies a further Scriptural witness in which breath, Spirit, life, and restoration are deliberately related.

John's own sequence matters. The Word in whom life is (John 1) is made flesh; Jesus promises the Spirit in relation to His glorification; after His Resurrection He appears to His disciples, sends them, breathes upon them, and commands them to receive the Holy Ghost. Acts then records the promised Spirit's coming in power and the disciples' public witness to the risen and exalted Christ. Romans 8 and 1 Corinthians 15 carry the testimony toward the life promised to those who belong to Christ.

The witness does not create the life it receives. The disciples do not originate the Spirit's testimony. They are sent and empowered to bear witness to the risen Christ.

This is a substantial canonical relationship to record in the completed GUA-W. It is not permission to invent a mechanism or add an architectural component. The John 20:22 / Acts 2 question remains open until the full Scriptural and historical evidence warrants a more exact conclusion.

**Investigation III.2 is recorded as a text-first canonical study, with the unresolved questions explicitly preserved.**


## 19. Scholarly audit — the interpretive problem is wider than “symbolic or actual”

A focused review of Adrian P. Rosen's 2025 article, “Interpretive Questions Related to the Gift of the Spirit in John 20:22,” *Journal of the Evangelical Theological Society* 68.2 (2025): 285–97, materially sharpens this investigation. Rosen explicitly does not defend one interpretation. He maps the questions that an adequate interpretation must address. That restraint is useful: the article is a guide to the debate, not a verdict that can be substituted for the text.

### 19.1 The Genesis 2:7 connection is substantial, but its precise force must be tested

Rosen reports that most scholars understand Jesus' breathing/blowing in John 20:22 against Genesis 2:7, because John uses ἐμφυσάω (*emphysaō*), the verb found in the Septuagint's account of God's breathing into Adam. He also records dissenting scholars who deny or do not use that connection, and proposals that Ezekiel 37:9 is the more immediate Scriptural background.

This refines, rather than overturns, the earlier finding:

- The lexical correspondence with the LXX of Genesis 2:7 is real and significant.
- A deliberate creation/breath allusion is strongly supported and widely recognized.
- Ezekiel 37 remains a serious canonical background because its Greek text also uses this breathing vocabulary in a restoration vision.
- The presence of an allusion does not, by itself, decide whether the act in John 20:22 conveys the Spirit at that moment, signifies a future gift, or performs more than one narrative/theological function.

The verb has also been discussed as “breathe on” or “blow on.” Rosen notes the debate and cites arguments for both renderings. The KJV's “breathed on” remains the text under examination; no doctrinal conclusion should rest on an English preposition alone.

### 19.2 The imperative “Receive” requires its own grammatical and contextual examination

John 20:22 joins Jesus' action with the imperative λάβετε (*labete*), “receive.” Rosen identifies several possibilities debated by interpreters: an anticipatory command pointing forward; an invitation or command calling for the disciples' response; a performative utterance that effects what it declares; or an utterance that explains the significance of the breathing/blowing.

These options are not interchangeable. If the breathing itself conveys the Spirit, the command may explain or accompany that act. If the breathing is symbolic, the command may still be interpreted as anticipatory or as the actual bestowal. The grammar and discourse must be examined together; the imperative alone does not settle the event's timing or function.

The expression πνεῦμα ἅγιον (“Holy Spirit”) is anarthrous in Greek. Rosen notes that some have argued from the absence of the article, while other grammarians and commentators regard the anarthrous construction as entirely compatible with reference to the Holy Spirit. The absence of the article is therefore not sufficient evidence to reduce the phrase to an impersonal “holy breath” or mere life-force.

### 19.3 Two separate axes must not be collapsed

Rosen's most useful contribution for the present investigation is the distinction between questions of **extent/timing** and questions of **function**.

**Extent and timing questions:**
- Is the action wholly symbolic and anticipatory, with no impartation at that moment?
- Is there an actual but partial or temporary bestowal?
- Is there a complete bestowal on Resurrection day?
- Is John 20:22 one stage in a process that includes John 19:30 and/or Acts 2?
- Is the reception described corporately, individually, or in both senses?

**Function questions:**
- Is the Spirit's work here life-giving or new-creation oriented?
- Is it empowerment for mission?
- Is it revelatory or prophetic enablement?
- Does it concern the indwelling presence of the Spirit?
- Is it related to the disciples' commissioned authority in John 20:21–23?

A proposed answer to one axis does not automatically answer the other. For example, saying that John 20:22 is an actual gift does not yet establish whether its function is life-giving, ministerial, prophetic, or several of these. Saying Pentecost is an empowerment for public witness does not prove that no genuine reception occurred earlier.

### 19.4 The immediate context supplies essential controls

John 20:19–23 must be read as one connected sequence:

1. The risen Jesus appears and gives peace.
2. He shows His hands and side; the disciples recognize the Lord.
3. He sends them as the Father sent Him.
4. He breathes/blows upon them and says, “Receive ye the Holy Ghost.”
5. He speaks of remitting and retaining sins.

The commission in verse 21 and the authority statement in verse 23 frame the Spirit saying in verse 22. This strongly associates the Spirit passage with the disciples' sent witness and ministry. It does not prove that the Spirit's only function here is ministerial authorization; the creation/breath allusion may also carry life-giving significance. Both dimensions require examination.

The earlier Johannine promises also remain in view: John 7:39 relates the promised Spirit to Jesus' glorification; John 14–16 describes the Comforter, the Father's sending, Christ's sending, remembrance, testimony, conviction, and guidance. A satisfactory account must explain how John 20 relates to these promises rather than selecting one saying and ignoring the others.

### 19.5 The scholarly positions cannot be reduced to two simple camps

Rosen's survey documents a wider range of proposals, including:

- a symbolic or anticipatory sign fulfilled at Pentecost;
- an actual Resurrection-day gift, with Pentecost as a distinct public outpouring or empowerment;
- an actual but partial or temporary reception followed by a fuller experience;
- a distinction between life-giving work in John and mission-empowerment in Acts, or the reverse;
- a process extending across the Cross, Resurrection day, and Pentecost;
- a relationship between the Spirit's gift and the commissioning/authority in John 20:21–23;
- interpretations involving the fulfillment of selected Paraclete promises at different points.

These are scholarly proposals, not facts established merely by their existence. Their variety demonstrates why the question must be resolved from the texts and arguments rather than by counting commentators or importing a denominational system.

### 19.6 What the evidence presently permits

**Established by the KJV text:**
- The risen Jesus breathes upon the disciples and commands them to receive the Holy Ghost (John 20:22).
- This occurs within His Resurrection-day appearance and commission (John 20:19–23).
- Acts records a later event in which the disciples are filled with the Holy Ghost and speak as the Spirit gives utterance (Acts 2:1–4).
- Acts 1:8 expressly connects the coming of the Holy Ghost with power to be witnesses of Jesus.
- Peter interprets the Pentecost event in relation to the exalted Jesus and the Father's promise (Acts 2:32–33).

**Strongly supported:**
- John's breathing language intentionally evokes the Scriptural breath/creation field, especially Genesis 2:7, while Ezekiel 37 remains a relevant restoration parallel.
- The Spirit saying is integrated with the sending and witness of the disciples, not detached from their commission.
- John 20 and Acts 2 must both retain their full textual weight.

**Not yet established:**
- That John 20:22 is merely symbolic.
- That John 20:22 and Acts 2 are identical in timing, extent, or function.
- That John 20:22 gives only a partial measure of the Spirit.
- That Pentecost is only a public display of a gift already fully received.
- That one passage cancels or corrects the other.
- That the breathing is a physical transfer of a material substance.
- That any one scholarly model has been demonstrated conclusively.

### 19.7 Triple Constraint assessment of this scholarly pass

- **Biblical compliance — PASS within scope:** the explicit actions and statements in John and Acts are retained; scholarly models are not inserted into the biblical wording.
- **Scientific coherence — PASS within scope:** this is a textual, grammatical, and historical-theological inquiry. No biological or physical mechanism is claimed.
- **GUA-W consistency — PASS:** God remains the Source; Christ's action and the Spirit's work are not reduced to an impersonal process; the disciples receive, are sent, and witness. No new layer, Gear, or mechanism is proposed.

**Disposition:** The interpretive question remains UNRESOLVED, but its shape is now more precise. Further work must test the major models against the complete Johannine sequence, Luke 24, Acts 1–2, the relevant Old Testament promises, and the historical reception of these texts. The investigation must keep extent/timing separate from function and must not treat “actual versus symbolic” as the only available distinction.

The article surveys the interpretive questions and range of scholarly proposals; it expressly does not defend one final interpretation.

## 20. Luke 24 and Acts 1 — the promised endowment remains future after the Resurrection

Luke supplies a second account of the period between the Resurrection and Pentecost. It prevents the investigation from treating John 20:22 as though it were the only record of Jesus' instruction concerning the Spirit after His Resurrection.

### 20.1 Luke 24:36–49 — the risen Lord opens understanding, commissions, and promises

Luke records the risen Jesus appearing among the disciples. He offers peace, shows His hands and feet, invites them to handle Him, and eats before them (Luke 24:36–43). He then opens their understanding so they may understand the Scriptures (v. 45), declares the necessity of the Messiah's suffering and Resurrection, and commissions the proclamation of repentance and remission of sins in His name among all nations (vv. 46–48).

The passage concludes:

> “And, behold, I send the promise of my Father upon you: but tarry ye in the city of Jerusalem, until ye be endued with power from on high.” (Luke 24:49, KJV)

This is explicit future-oriented language. The disciples are to remain in Jerusalem until they are clothed/endued with power from on high. Luke does not say in this verse that the disciples have already received this promised endowment.

**What Luke establishes:** the risen Jesus promises a coming endowment of power, commands the disciples to wait for it, and connects it to their commissioned proclamation.

**What Luke 24:49 does not explicitly state:** that no work or reception of the Holy Ghost could have occurred before this coming endowment. That negative proposition is not in the verse.

### 20.2 Acts 1:1–8 — Luke restates and clarifies the waiting period

Acts opens by referring to the former treatise and to what Jesus began both to do and teach. It then records that Jesus showed Himself alive after His Passion by many infallible proofs, appearing over forty days and speaking of the kingdom of God (Acts 1:1–3).

Jesus commands the disciples not to depart from Jerusalem, but to wait for the promise of the Father. He distinguishes John's water baptism from their coming baptism with the Holy Ghost (vv. 4–5). The disciples ask about the restoration of the kingdom to Israel; Jesus redirects them from knowledge of times and seasons reserved to the Father's authority to the promised reception of power:

> “But ye shall receive power, after that the Holy Ghost is come upon you: and ye shall be witnesses unto me...” (Acts 1:8, KJV)

The stated purpose is witness extending from Jerusalem to Judaea, Samaria, and the uttermost part of the earth.

Acts 1 therefore confirms that, after the Resurrection and during the forty-day period, the promised baptism/coming of the Holy Ghost and the associated power for witness are still awaited. This is not a minor detail. It must be given full weight alongside John 20:22.

### 20.3 Acts 2:1–4, 32–33 — the event and Peter's interpretation

At Pentecost the disciples are together. A sound from heaven as of a rushing mighty wind fills the house; cloven tongues like as of fire appear; they are filled with the Holy Ghost and speak with other tongues as the Spirit gives utterance (Acts 2:1–4).

Peter's explanation is essential. He proclaims Jesus' Resurrection, declares that Jesus has been exalted by the right hand of God, and says:

> “Therefore being by the right hand of God exalted, and having received of the Father the promise of the Holy Ghost, he hath shed forth this, which ye now see and hear.” (Acts 2:33, KJV)

The passage identifies the exalted Jesus as the one who pours forth what the gathered witnesses see and hear. Pentecost is not presented as an autonomous human achievement or as the disciples generating spiritual power. It is the action of the exalted Christ in relation to the Father's promise.

### 20.4 The chronology that must be preserved

The Lukan account gives this sequence:

1. Jesus rises and appears to His disciples.
2. He opens their understanding of the Scriptures and commissions the proclamation (Luke 24:36–48).
3. He promises the Father's endowment and commands them to wait in Jerusalem (Luke 24:49).
4. Acts records appearances over forty days and further instruction concerning the kingdom (Acts 1:1–3).
5. Jesus commands them to wait for the Father's promise and promises power when the Holy Ghost comes upon them (Acts 1:4–8).
6. Jesus ascends (Acts 1:9–11).
7. At Pentecost the disciples are filled with the Holy Ghost and speak as the Spirit gives utterance (Acts 2:1–4).
8. Peter identifies the outpouring as the exalted Jesus' shedding forth of the Father's promise (Acts 2:32–33).

John 20:19–23, meanwhile, places the breathing and command to receive the Holy Ghost on the Resurrection day. The Lukan sequence does not mention this breathing scene. The difference in what each Gospel records must not be turned automatically into a contradiction; neither may the omission be used to make the accounts say the same thing in identical terms.

### 20.5 What this does to the competing interpretations

**Model A — John 20:22 is entirely anticipatory, with the promised reception occurring only at Pentecost.**

This model can account for Luke 24:49 and Acts 1:4–8, which place the promised endowment in the future. Its burden is to explain why John narrates Jesus breathing upon the disciples and commanding them, in the imperative, to receive the Holy Ghost. It must account for the force of John's action and words without reducing them to an empty gesture.

**Model B — John 20:22 is an actual reception, while Pentecost is a distinct endowment or empowerment for public witness.**

This model can preserve the direct wording of John 20:22 and the future-oriented promise in Luke 24:49 and Acts 1:4–8 by distinguishing the gift/reception in John from the promised power for witness in Luke–Acts. Its burden is to establish from the texts that these are distinguishable functions or stages, rather than simply assuming that distinction to solve the chronology.

**Model C — John 20:22 is an actual but limited or preparatory reception, with Pentecost bringing a fuller or distinct manifestation.**

This model attempts to account for both events as related stages. Its burden is to identify textual evidence for “limited,” “partial,” or “fuller.” Neither John 20:22 nor Acts 2 uses those exact comparative terms to define the relation between the two events. Such language must therefore remain an interpretive hypothesis unless further evidence establishes it.

**Model D — one continuous divine purpose is expressed through distinct events and functions.**

This proposal may allow the Resurrection-day scene, the waiting period, the Ascension, and Pentecost to be related without making them identical. But “one purpose” cannot serve as a substitute for explaining what each passage says happened, when it happened, and what function is expressly attributed to it. The proposal is a framework for testing, not yet a conclusion.

### 20.6 A critical distinction: reception, endowment, and witness

The texts use different expressions in different contexts:

- John 20:22: Jesus breathes upon the disciples and commands, “Receive ye the Holy Ghost.”
- Luke 24:49: Jesus promises that He will send the Father's promise upon them and commands them to wait until they are endued with power from on high.
- Acts 1:5: Jesus promises their baptism with the Holy Ghost.
- Acts 1:8: Jesus promises power when the Holy Ghost comes upon them, for the purpose of witness.
- Acts 2:4: they are filled with the Holy Ghost and speak as the Spirit gives utterance.
- Acts 2:33: Peter attributes the outpouring to the exalted Jesus, who has received the Father's promise.

These expressions are related, but the text does not authorize treating every verb as a technical synonym with an identical temporal and functional meaning. Nor does vocabulary variation alone prove separate gifts. Context, narrative sequence, and the speakers' explanations must govern.

### 20.7 Interim finding

**Explicitly established:**
- John records a Resurrection-day breathing and command to receive the Holy Ghost.
- Luke records a future promise of endowment with power, with a command to wait.
- Acts records that the promised coming and power occur after the Ascension, at Pentecost.
- Peter identifies the exalted Jesus as the one who pours forth the Father's promise.
- The Lukan account expressly connects the coming power with witness to Jesus.

**Strongly supported:**
- John 20:22 and Pentecost must not be collapsed into one undifferentiated event.
- Luke–Acts gives a distinct future-oriented promise that remains outstanding during the post-Resurrection waiting period.
- The risen and exalted Christ is the active giver; the disciples are recipients and witnesses.

**Not established yet:**
- That John 20:22 is only symbolic.
- That John 20:22 is a complete and final fulfillment of every promise concerning the Spirit.
- That the disciples received only a partial measure in John 20.
- That Luke 24:49 explicitly denies any earlier reception of the Spirit.
- That the difference between John's and Luke's accounts is a contradiction.
- That the precise relation is exhausted by the labels “indwelling” and “empowerment.”

### 20.8 Triple Constraint assessment

- **Biblical compliance — PASS:** the distinct statements and sequences in John, Luke, and Acts are preserved. No passage is suppressed to force harmonization.
- **Scientific coherence — PASS within scope:** this is a canonical and historical-textual comparison. No unsupported physical or biological mechanism is proposed.
- **GUA-W consistency — PASS:** the Father promises; the risen and exalted Christ sends and pours forth; the Holy Ghost gives power and utterance; the disciples receive and witness. The derivative witnesses do not become the Source.

**Disposition:** U-008 remains UNRESOLVED, but the Lukan evidence places a strong constraint on every proposed reading. Any adequate account must explain both the direct Resurrection-day command in John 20:22 and the still-future endowment promised in Luke 24:49 and Acts 1:4–8. The distinction between an earlier reception and a later promised endowment is a serious candidate for investigation, but its exact terms and theological meaning must be demonstrated rather than assumed.

## 21. John 7:39 — the Spirit's promised reception and the glorification of Jesus

The next controlling text is John 7:37–39. The narrator explicitly interprets Jesus' promise of living water as a reference to the Spirit whom believers were to receive, and connects that reception to Jesus' glorification. This passage must be read alongside John 16:7, John 17:1–5, John 19:30–34, John 20:22, and Acts 2:32–33.

### 21.1 The immediate setting: living water and the promised Spirit

At the feast, Jesus calls the thirsty to come to Him and drink (John 7:37). He speaks of rivers of living water flowing from the believer (v. 38). The narrator then states that Jesus spoke concerning the Spirit, whom believers were to receive, “for the Holy Ghost was not yet given; because that Jesus was not yet glorified” (v. 39, KJV).

The narrator's explanation is decisive for identifying the living water with the Spirit. It is not necessary to guess whether the image is merely physical water or a general metaphor for blessing: the text itself identifies the subject.

The direction of the promise is also clear: believers are to receive the Spirit, and the narrator relates this reception to the glorification of Jesus. The verse does not, by itself, specify every detail of when or how that reception occurs.

### 21.2 A translation and textual observation: “given” is supplied in the KJV

The KJV prints “given” in italics, indicating that the word is supplied to make the sense clear in English. The commonly printed Greek wording is compact: “for the Spirit was not yet, because Jesus was not yet glorified” (οὔπω γὰρ ἦν πνεῦμα, ὅτι Ἰησοῦς οὐδέπω ἐδοξάσθη).

Some later witnesses add wording equivalent to “given,” while the shorter wording is broadly represented in the early Greek textual tradition. The NET textual note explains that “given” is supplied in translation to prevent the mistaken conclusion that the Spirit did not exist before Jesus' glorification. This is a translation clarification, not a claim that the Spirit had no existence or activity before the Cross.

The Gospel itself has already described the Spirit descending and remaining upon Jesus (John 1:32–33), and Jesus has spoken of birth from the Spirit (3:5–8). Therefore John 7:39 cannot reasonably mean that the Spirit did not exist or had never acted. The explanatory focus is the promised reception associated with Jesus' glorification.

**Guardrail:** Do not build a doctrine on the English italics alone; do not claim that the Holy Spirit did not exist or work before Jesus was glorified; do not make the supplied word “given” disappear from consideration. Attend to the Greek wording, the KJV translation, and the Gospel's own prior Spirit references together.

### 21.3 What does “glorified” mean in John's Gospel?

John's use of glory language is not confined to one isolated instant. The narrative develops Jesus' “hour” and glorification through several connected passages:

- **John 12:23–24:** Jesus declares that the hour has come for the Son of man to be glorified, then speaks of the grain of wheat falling into the ground and dying in order to bring forth much fruit.
- **John 12:27–33:** Jesus speaks of the hour, the Father's name being glorified, and being “lifted up”; the narrator identifies this lifting up with the kind of death Jesus would die.
- **John 13:1, 31–32:** Jesus knows His hour has come to depart out of this world unto the Father; after Judas departs, Jesus speaks of the Son of man being glorified and God being glorified in Him.
- **John 16:5–7:** Jesus says He is going to the One who sent Him and that His departure is connected with the coming of the Comforter.
- **John 17:1–5:** Jesus asks the Father to glorify the Son so that the Son may glorify the Father, and speaks of the glory He had with the Father before the world was.
- **John 19:30:** Jesus declares “It is finished,” bows His head, and gives up the ghost.
- **John 20:** the risen Jesus appears to His disciples.

The strongest textual conclusion is that the Cross is central to Johannine glorification, but the full movement is related to Jesus' return to the Father. The Cross is not a disposable preliminary to glory: John presents the lifting up in death itself as glorifying. At the same time, John 13:1 and 16:5–7 place His departure to the Father and the sending of the Comforter within the same movement. John 17:1–5 joins the hour, the Cross-shaped mission, and the glory with the Father.

It is therefore too narrow to say that “glorified” means only the moment of Resurrection, or only the moment of Ascension, without explaining John's Cross-centered use of the term. It is also too narrow to isolate the crucifixion from the return to the Father and the sending of the Spirit.

### 21.4 The Cross and John 19:30 — does Jesus give the Spirit at His death?

John 19:30 says that Jesus bowed His head and “gave up the ghost” (παρέδωκεν τὸ πνεῦμα). Because the same Gospel has connected the Spirit with living water and glorification (John 7:37–39), some interpreters have proposed that John's wording at the Cross carries a double meaning: Jesus truly dies, while the language also anticipates or signifies the giving of the Spirit.

This proposal must be examined, not assumed.

Peter-Ben Smit's study, “The Gift of the Spirit in John 19:30? A Reconsideration of παραδωκεν τὸ πνεῦμα,” argues that the expression primarily describes Jesus' complete death, while allowing that it may carry a proleptic reference to what unfolds in the final chapters. John Christopher Thomas's 2021 survey also records the debate and cautions that arguments for a direct bestowal of the Spirit at this exact point may exceed the evidence.

The immediate textual sequence is:
1. Jesus declares the work finished (John 19:30).
2. He bows His head and gives up the ghost (v. 30).
3. A soldier pierces His side, and blood and water come forth (v. 34).
4. John gives eyewitness testimony to the event (vv. 35–37).
5. The risen Jesus later breathes upon the disciples and commands them to receive the Holy Ghost (20:22).

John 19:30 unquestionably records Jesus' death. Whether its wording also signals the Spirit's gift is debated. John 19:34's blood and water may be read in relation to the living-water promise, but a direct identification of the water with the Spirit at that precise moment must be demonstrated rather than presumed. The narrator's explicit identification of living water with the Spirit occurs in John 7:39; the narrator does not explicitly repeat that identification in John 19:34.

**Guardrail:** Do not treat “gave up the ghost” as proof that the Spirit was imparted to the disciples at the Cross. Do not rule out every literary or theological echo simply because the immediate sense is Jesus' death. Keep primary meaning and possible proleptic resonance distinct.

### 21.5 John 16:7 is a key bridge between glorification and the Spirit's coming

Jesus says that it is expedient for the disciples that He go away; if He does not go away, the Comforter will not come to them, but if He departs, He will send Him (John 16:7).

This statement strongly links the Spirit's coming to Jesus' departure. It supports the future orientation of John 7:39 and aligns with Luke 24:49 and Acts 1:4–8, where the disciples are told to wait for the Father's promise and the coming power.

Yet John 16:7 does not explain John 20:22 by itself. We must not assume that “go away” refers only to the instant of death, nor may we detach it from the Cross and Resurrection that form the passage's surrounding context. The relation between departure, return to the Father, glorification, and the Spirit's coming requires the whole narrative.

### 21.6 The strongest synthesis currently supported

The passages now provide a more coherent sequence of textual claims:

- **John 7:37–39:** Jesus promises living water; the narrator identifies it as the Spirit believers were to receive in relation to Jesus' glorification.
- **John 12:23–33:** Jesus' hour of glorification arrives through the Cross; His being lifted up is identified with His death.
- **John 13:1; 16:5–7:** His departure to the Father is linked with the coming of the Comforter.
- **John 17:1–5:** Jesus asks the Father to glorify Him in the hour and speaks of the glory He had with the Father before the world.
- **John 19:30–37:** Jesus completes His work and truly dies; the side-piercing and blood/water testimony follow. A direct Spirit-bestowal interpretation of 19:30 remains debated.
- **John 20:19–23:** the risen Jesus breathes upon the disciples and commands them to receive the Holy Ghost.
- **Luke 24:49; Acts 1:4–8:** the disciples are still commanded to wait for the Father's promised endowment and power for witness.
- **Acts 2:32–33:** Peter identifies Pentecost's outpouring with the exalted Jesus' shedding forth of the Father's promise.

This supports a connected divine purpose across Cross, Resurrection, return to the Father, and the Spirit's coming. It does not yet settle whether John 20:22 is an actual reception distinct from Pentecost, an anticipatory sign, or a moment within a larger staged bestowal. A unified purpose does not make the events identical; distinct events do not require unrelated purposes.

### 21.7 Triple Constraint assessment

- **Biblical compliance — PASS within scope:** John 7:39 is read with its narrator's explanation; earlier Spirit activity is not denied; the Cross remains central to John's glorification language; John 19:30 is not made to prove more than it explicitly states; John 20 and Acts 2 retain their distinct wording and chronology.
- **Scientific coherence — PASS within scope:** this is a textual, grammatical, and historical-theological investigation. No unsupported physical mechanism for the Spirit's action is proposed.
- **GUA-W consistency — PASS:** the Father is the Source; the Son completes the mission, returns to the Father, and sends/pours forth the promised Spirit; believers receive and witness. No derivative witness is made the originating cause.

**Disposition:** John 7:39 and 16:7 place Jesus' glorification/departure in a necessary relation to the promised reception of the Spirit. The Cross is central to this Johannine glorification, and the return to the Father is part of the narrative movement. John 19:30's primary reference is Jesus' death; a secondary Spirit-gift resonance remains debated. U-008 remains UNRESOLVED pending a full synthesis of John's narrative with Luke–Acts and the historical evidence.

## 22. John 1:32–34; 3:5–8; 3:34 — the Spirit descends, gives birth, and is given without measure

The Johannine sequence must now be examined from its beginning. John does not introduce the Spirit for the first time in John 20. The Gospel first presents the Spirit descending and remaining upon Jesus, then records Jesus' teaching about birth from the Spirit, and identifies Jesus as the one who baptizes with the Holy Ghost. These passages establish essential controls for interpreting the Resurrection-day command.

### 22.1 John 1:32–34 — the Spirit descends and remains upon Jesus

John the Baptist testifies that he saw the Spirit descend from heaven like a dove and remain upon Jesus. The testimony is repeated in the divine sign given to John: the one upon whom the Spirit descends and remains is the one who baptizes with the Holy Ghost.

The Greek verb μένω (*menō*, remain/abide) is important throughout the Fourth Gospel. Here the text does not merely report a momentary descent; it expressly says the Spirit remained upon Jesus. The Baptist's testimony identifies Jesus and His distinctive mission.

The narrative does not describe Jesus as receiving the Spirit because He previously lacked the Spirit, nor does it explain the descent as the Spirit's first existence or activity. The passage gives a sign identifying Jesus as the Spirit-baptizer. Interpretations of the event as messianic anointing or inauguration of public ministry are well represented in scholarship, but those theological descriptions should be kept distinct from the explicit wording of the testimony.

**Explicit:** the Spirit descends, remains upon Jesus, and identifies Him as the one who baptizes with the Holy Ghost.

**Supported inference:** the abiding Spirit marks Jesus' distinctive identity and mission as the Spirit-baptizer.

**Not established by this passage alone:** a complete account of the Spirit's eternal relation to the Son; the exact mechanics of the Spirit's descent; or the precise timing and nature of every later reception by believers.

### 22.2 John 3:5–8 — birth from above by water and Spirit

Jesus tells Nicodemus that a man must be born of water and of the Spirit to enter the kingdom of God (John 3:5). He contrasts flesh born of flesh with spirit born of Spirit (v. 6), commands Nicodemus not to marvel, and uses the wind to illustrate the Spirit's sovereign, perceptible yet unmastered action (vv. 7–8).

The immediate context begins with the necessity of being born ἄνωθεν (*anōthen*), a word capable of meaning “again” or “from above” (vv. 3–4). Jesus' answer in verses 5–8 explains the birth necessary to see and enter God's kingdom. The origin and agency are from above; the human recipient does not generate this birth.

The phrase “water and Spirit” has been interpreted in several ways: as a unified image of cleansing and renewal, with Ezekiel 36:25–27 as an important background; as a reference involving baptism; or through other proposed readings. The allusion to Ezekiel's promise of clean water, a new heart, and God's Spirit is a strong canonical candidate, but the exact force of “water” in John 3:5 remains debated. Do not declare the question settled by importing a later sacramental or denominational system.

John 3:8 adds a decisive limit on human control: the wind/Spirit blows where it wills; its sound is heard, but its origin and destination are not mastered by the observer. Jesus does not present the Spirit as an instrument controlled by the recipient. The illustration concerns the Spirit's freedom and the observable reality of His work.

**Explicit:** entrance into the kingdom requires birth of water and Spirit; flesh produces flesh; Spirit produces spirit; the Spirit's action is not controlled or comprehensively mapped by the human observer.

**Strongly supported:** the new birth is God's work from above, not a humanly manufactured state. Ezekiel 36:25–27 provides a substantial Scriptural parallel for cleansing and Spirit-renewal.

**Unresolved:** the precise relation of water to Spirit in John 3:5; the extent to which baptism is directly in view; and how the new birth relates temporally and functionally to John 20:22 and Acts 2.

### 22.3 John 3:34 — God gives the Spirit without measure

John 3:34 says that the one God has sent speaks the words of God, “for God giveth not the Spirit by measure unto him” (KJV). This verse is important in the sequence because it places the Spirit's giving in relation to the one sent by God.

A grammatical caution is necessary. The Greek clause's subject and the implied recipient have been discussed by interpreters; translations differ in how they express the final phrase. The KJV makes God the giver and the sent one the recipient. Other translations and commentators have discussed whether the statement is more general or whether the Spirit is given without measure by God to the Son. The verse must be interpreted in its immediate context (John 3:31–36), where the one from above speaks God's words and the Father has given all things into His hand.

The text strongly supports the unique fullness of the Spirit's relation to the one sent from above, but avoid making a detailed doctrine rest on an English rendering without examining the Greek syntax and textual context.

### 22.4 John 1 and John 3 together — the Giver and the new birth

The two passages must not be collapsed:

- John 1:32–34 identifies Jesus as the one upon whom the Spirit remains and as the one who baptizes with the Holy Ghost.
- John 3:5–8 describes the necessity and divine agency of birth from the Spirit for entrance into God's kingdom.
- John 3:34 relates the Spirit's giving to the one sent by God, with a grammatical question that requires care.

A distinction is visible between the one identified as the Spirit-baptizer and the people who must be born of the Spirit. Jesus' identity and mission are not interchangeable with the recipient's new birth. The recipient does not become the source of the Spirit by receiving the Spirit.

This is a distinction of roles in the text, not yet a complete technical taxonomy of Spirit operations.

### 22.5 The sequence through John 7 and John 20

The Gospel's sequence now reads:

1. **John 1:32–34:** the Spirit descends and remains upon Jesus; Jesus is identified as the one who baptizes with the Holy Ghost.
2. **John 3:5–8:** birth from above by water and Spirit is necessary for entrance into God's kingdom; the Spirit acts freely.
3. **John 3:34:** the Spirit's giving is related to the one sent by God; the exact grammatical force must be respected.
4. **John 7:37–39:** Jesus promises living water, and the narrator identifies it with the Spirit believers were to receive in relation to Jesus' glorification.
5. **John 14–16:** Jesus promises the Comforter and connects His coming with Jesus' departure.
6. **John 19:30:** Jesus truly dies; any additional Spirit-gift resonance remains debated.
7. **John 20:22:** the risen Jesus breathes upon the disciples and commands them to receive the Holy Ghost.
8. **Luke 24:49; Acts 1:4–8; 2:1–4, 32–33:** the promised endowment, power, and Pentecostal outpouring are narrated after the Resurrection and Ascension.

This is a real literary and theological progression. It does not mean that every passage describes the same event or the same function.

### 22.6 Three distinctions that must be preserved

**A. The Spirit remaining upon Jesus**

This is the Baptist's testimony identifying Jesus and His mission. Jesus is the one who baptizes with the Holy Ghost.

**B. Birth from the Spirit**

This is Jesus' teaching about the divine origin and necessity of new birth. The human recipient cannot manufacture it or control the Spirit.

**C. The risen Christ's command to receive the Holy Ghost**

This is a specific action and command in the Resurrection narrative, within the commission of John 20:19–23. Its exact relation to new birth, the promised Spirit of John 7, and Pentecost remains under examination.

These distinctions are textually warranted as distinct narrative statements. Whether they represent distinct operations, related aspects of one gift, or successive stages cannot yet be settled from these passages alone.

### 22.7 A candidate relationship — Jesus as the Spirit-baptizer

John 1:33 is especially important because it does not merely say that Jesus receives or bears the Spirit. It identifies Him as the one who baptizes with the Holy Ghost. John 20:22 later records Jesus Himself acting toward the disciples and commanding reception.

This creates a strong canonical connection: the one identified at the beginning of the Gospel as the Spirit-baptizer is the risen one who, at the end of the Gospel, breathes upon His disciples and commands them to receive the Holy Ghost.

**This connection is explicit at the level of the two passages' stated roles and actions.** It is not yet proof that John 20:22 exhausts the promised Spirit baptism, nor that Pentecost is merely a repetition of the same act. Acts 1–2 must retain its distinct chronology and description.

### 22.8 Triple Constraint assessment

- **Biblical compliance — PASS within scope:** John 1, 3, 7, and 20 are read as distinct contexts. Jesus' identity as Spirit-baptizer is not confused with the believer's new birth or the disciples' reception. Disputed readings of John 3:5 and 3:34 remain identified as disputed.
- **Scientific coherence — PASS within scope:** this is textual and theological analysis. The wind analogy in John 3:8 is not misrepresented as a physical theory of the Holy Spirit, and no material mechanism is invented.
- **GUA-W consistency — PASS:** God is the Source; Jesus is identified as the one sent and the Spirit-baptizer; the Spirit acts and gives life; human beings receive and witness. No recipient is made the originating cause.

**Disposition:** John 1:32–34, 3:5–8, and 3:34 establish necessary distinctions for the investigation. Jesus is identified as the one upon whom the Spirit remains and the one who baptizes with the Holy Ghost; new birth is from the Spirit and not generated by the recipient; the risen Jesus later commands reception. The relation among these statements, John 7:39, and Pentecost remains UNRESOLVED. No new GUA-W Gear is promoted by this pass.

### 22.10 Scholarly refinement — the sign, the Spirit-baptizer, and the disputed water

The consulted commentaries strengthen several points while also identifying limits that must remain visible.

1. **The sign in John 1 is primarily revelatory.** Köstenberger notes that the Fourth Gospel does not narrate Jesus' baptism itself; it presents the Baptist's testimony about the Spirit's descent and remaining as the divine sign identifying Jesus to the Baptist. This means the narrative emphasis is not a technical account of the mechanics of Jesus' baptism, but the identification and mission of Jesus. <https://www.thetbs.org/study-materials/wp-content/uploads/2023/03/JohnBaker-Exegetical-Commentary-by-Andreas-Kostenberger-z-lib.org_.pdf>

2. **The Spirit-baptizer reading has a scholarly counter-position.** John 1:33 naturally identifies Jesus as the one who baptizes with the Holy Spirit. However, Cornelis Bennema has argued that the text may point to the effects of Jesus' Spirit-endowed ministry rather than a direct act of bestowing the Spirit. A published response disputes that reading. The verse's wording is clear; the exact account of how Jesus' Spirit-baptizing activity is realized in the Gospel's narrative remains a matter for argument. <https://www.thegospelcoalition.org/themelios/article/the-christocentric-and-christotelic-nature-of-johannine-pneumatology/>

3. **John 3:5 must not be prematurely narrowed.** John Christopher Thomas's survey notes that “water and Spirit” can be read as a unified “from above” birth image, while water also has other associations in the Gospel, including baptism. Ezekiel 36 remains a strong canonical parallel, but the exact reference of “water” is not settled by simply naming one background. <https://brill.com/view/journals/pneu/43/3-4/article-p442_14.pdf>

4. **John 3:34 is a grammatical caution point.** The KJV expresses the phrase as God giving the Spirit “unto him.” The verse is commonly read as describing the Spirit's unmeasured relation to the one sent by God, but the Greek clause is compact and translations differ in how they supply the implied recipient. The context (3:31–36) should govern; avoid treating the English supplied words as if each were separately explicit in the Greek.

5. **The Gospel presents a coherent Spirit-witness to Jesus.** John 1:32–34, 14:26, 15:26, and 16:13 place the Spirit's work in relation to identifying, testifying to, and glorifying Jesus. This reinforces the Christ-centered direction of the narrative without collapsing the Spirit's distinct agency into a mere metaphor.

**Refined disposition:** The explicit narrative distinctions remain. The interpretation of John 1:33's Spirit-baptizing action, John 3:5's water, and John 3:34's syntax requires continued examination. These qualifications do not erase the Gospel's clear identification of Jesus as the one associated with the Spirit's baptism, nor the necessity of birth from the Spirit.

## 23. Ezekiel 36:25–27 and 37:1–14 — cleansing, renewed heart, breath, and life

The Johannine Spirit investigation now reaches two closely related prophetic passages. Ezekiel 36:25–27 promises cleansing, a new heart, a new spirit, and God's own Spirit within the people. Ezekiel 37:1–14 presents a vision in which dry bones are assembled, receive flesh, and then live when breath/Spirit enters them. The vision is explicitly interpreted as the restoration of the house of Israel from its death-like condition in exile.

These passages must be read in their own historical and literary setting before they are compared with Genesis 2:7 and John 3 and 20.

### 23.1 Ezekiel 36:16–38 — the setting is Israel's defilement, exile, and restoration

The promise in verses 25–27 is not an isolated formula about an individual's private spiritual experience. Ezekiel 36:16–21 describes Israel's defilement of the land through bloodshed and idolatry, followed by dispersion among the nations. The LORD says that He acts for His holy name, which Israel had profaned among the nations (vv. 22–23).

The restoration promise then includes:
- gathering Israel from the nations and bringing them into their own land (v. 24);
- cleansing them from uncleanness and idols (v. 25);
- giving a new heart and putting a new spirit within them (v. 26);
- putting God's Spirit within them and causing them to walk in His statutes (v. 27);
- restoring covenant belonging and life in the land (v. 28);
- multiplying provision and fruitfulness (vv. 29–30);
- remembrance, shame, and recognition of the LORD's action (vv. 31–32);
- rebuilding and cultivation of the desolate land (vv. 33–36).

The stated divine motive is crucial: “not for your sakes do I this” (v. 32), but for the LORD's holy name (vv. 22, 32). The promise originates in God's purpose and action, not in Israel's self-repair.

**Explicit:** this is a corporate restoration oracle addressed to Israel, grounded in the LORD's holy name and involving cleansing, inward renewal, restored obedience, land, and covenant relationship.

**Strongly supported:** the promised inward renewal is inseparable from the LORD's larger restoration of His people. It is not merely a private feeling or an abstract spiritual experience.

**Not established:** that every detail in the oracle should be transferred without qualification to an individual believer or to a single later event. Later canonical use must be examined rather than assumed.

### 23.2 Ezekiel 36:25 — clean water and cleansing

The LORD promises to sprinkle clean water upon the people so that they will be clean, specifically cleansing them from filthiness and idols.

The image recalls purification language elsewhere in Scripture (compare Numbers 19:17–19; Psalm 51:7; Zechariah 13:1). Ezekiel's immediate wording explains the purpose: cleansing from defilement. The water is not presented as an autonomous force or as an action Israel performs upon itself. The LORD is the subject: “I will sprinkle”; “I will cleanse.”

This is a significant control for John 3:5. Jesus' phrase “born of water and of the Spirit” can reasonably be read against Ezekiel's promised cleansing and Spirit-renewal. The allusion is strong, especially because Nicodemus is addressed as a teacher of Israel (John 3:10). But John does not explicitly quote Ezekiel 36 in John 3:5, so the connection should be recorded as a strong canonical interpretation, not as a verbatim citation.

### 23.3 Ezekiel 36:26–27 — new heart, new spirit, and “my Spirit”

The text distinguishes closely related expressions:
- “a new heart”;
- “a new spirit”;
- “my spirit.”

The LORD promises to remove the heart of stone and give a heart of flesh. He will put a new spirit within the people, and He will put His Spirit within them, causing them to walk in His statutes.

The relation between “a new spirit” in verse 26 and “my spirit” in verse 27 has been discussed by scholars. Some interpretations distinguish the renewed human disposition from God's own Spirit; others stress their close relation or analogical correspondence. The wording of verse 27, “my spirit,” must not be casually reduced to a human faculty. At the same time, the precise theological account of the relation between the two expressions should not be asserted beyond what the passage establishes.

The practical result is explicit: the LORD's action produces a changed walk in obedience (v. 27). The promise is not simply an inward sensation. It has a stated outcome in the people's life.

**Textual distinction:** “new spirit” and “my Spirit” occur in adjacent but distinguishable clauses. Their relationship is important; their expressions should not be silently collapsed.

### 23.4 Ezekiel 37:1–10 — the bones are assembled, but there is no breath in them

The hand of the LORD brings Ezekiel out in the Spirit and sets him down in a valley full of bones (37:1). The bones are very many and very dry (v. 2). God asks whether they can live; Ezekiel answers, “O Lord GOD, thou knowest” (v. 3).

The LORD commands Ezekiel to prophesy to the bones. The word announces that God will cause breath to enter them and that they will live (vv. 4–6).

Ezekiel prophesies as commanded. There is a noise and shaking; the bones come together, sinews and flesh appear, and skin covers them (vv. 7–8). Yet the text makes a decisive observation:

> “but there was no breath in them.” (Ezekiel 37:8, KJV)

The visible assembly is not yet living. The text does not permit the observer to equate the appearance of a complete bodily structure with the presence of life.

Then the LORD commands Ezekiel to prophesy to the breath:

> “Come from the four winds, O breath, and breathe upon these slain, that they may live.” (v. 9)

Ezekiel prophesies as commanded. Breath enters them; they live and stand upon their feet, an exceeding great army (v. 10).

This is a remarkable textual sequence:
1. Bones are present.
2. Bones are assembled.
3. Sinews, flesh, and skin appear.
4. No breath is yet in them.
5. The commanded prophetic word is spoken to the breath.
6. Breath enters.
7. They live and stand.

The text itself distinguishes bodily assembly from the entry of breath and the resulting life. It does not say that the assembled bones generated their own breath.

### 23.5 Ezekiel 37:11–14 — the LORD interprets His own vision

The interpretation is not left to speculation. The LORD says:

> “Son of man, these bones are the whole house of Israel...” (v. 11)

The people say their bones are dried, their hope is lost, and they are cut off. The LORD promises to open their graves, bring them into the land of Israel, put His Spirit within them, and cause them to live (vv. 12–14).

This divine interpretation governs the vision. The primary referent is the restoration of Israel from its death-like exile and hopelessness. It is not permissible to detach the bones from the stated interpretation and claim that Ezekiel is directly describing an unrelated event.

At the same time, the language of opening graves, life from death, and the gift of God's Spirit gives the passage a wider canonical significance. Later Scripture may draw upon this pattern, but each use must be established from its own text.

### 23.6 The Hebrew word רוּחַ (*rûaḥ*) — breath, wind, and spirit

One feature of Ezekiel 37 is especially important. The Hebrew noun רוּחַ (*rûaḥ*) can mean wind, breath, or spirit, with the context determining the sense. In Ezekiel 37:9–14 the word moves through a deliberate range of associations:
- the breath that enters the slain;
- the winds from which the breath is summoned;
- the Spirit of the LORD promised within the people.

English translations sometimes choose “breath,” “wind,” or “spirit” at different points. This helps readability, but can obscure the Hebrew wordplay and conceptual continuity.

The repeated word does not mean that every occurrence has precisely the same referent. In verse 9, the four winds are addressed; in verse 14, the LORD promises “my spirit” within the people. The passage relates these meanings without making their contextual distinctions disappear.

The point is not that Hebrew has one word for three unrelated things. Nor is it that every use of *rûaḥ* must mean the Holy Spirit. The point is that Ezekiel's prophetic vision intentionally joins wind, breath, life, and God's Spirit in one literary movement.

### 23.7 The connection to Genesis 2:7 — a deliberate creation echo

Genesis 2:7 records that the LORD God formed man of the dust of the ground and breathed into his nostrils the breath of life; man became a living soul.

Ezekiel 37:9 uses the verb נָפַח (*nāphaḥ*, to breathe/blow), the same Hebrew verb used in Genesis 2:7. Both passages describe a divine life-giving action involving breath. Ezekiel's vision also depicts a formed bodily structure that remains lifeless until breath enters.

This creates a strong literary and theological connection:
- Genesis 2:7: the LORD forms the man from the dust and breathes the breath of life; the man becomes a living being.
- Ezekiel 37: bones are assembled and covered with flesh, but there is no breath; breath enters at God's command, and the slain live.

The difference must also be preserved. Genesis 2:7 describes the creation of the first man. Ezekiel 37 explicitly interprets the vision as the restoration of the whole house of Israel. Ezekiel employs creation language to portray national restoration as a new-life act of God. The parallel is not identity of historical event; it is a deliberate reuse of creation imagery to declare God's power to bring life where death and hopelessness prevail.

### 23.8 The connection to John 3:5–8 — cleansing and Spirit-renewal

John 3:5 speaks of birth of water and Spirit. Ezekiel 36:25–27 places cleansing water, a new heart, a new spirit, and God's Spirit within the people in one restoration promise.

This is a strong background for Jesus' words. John 3:10 sharpens the point: Jesus expects Nicodemus, a teacher of Israel, to understand the matters being discussed. Ezekiel's promise belongs to Israel's prophetic Scriptures and is therefore a relevant text for the conversation.

John 3:8's wind/Spirit wordplay also resonates with the range of *rûaḥ* in Ezekiel 37. The Greek πνεῦμα (*pneuma*) likewise can mean spirit, wind, or breath depending on context. This does not prove that John 3:8 directly quotes Ezekiel 37, but it makes the canonical comparison meaningful.

**Finding:** Ezekiel 36 is a strong candidate for the cleansing-and-renewal background of John 3:5. Ezekiel 37 provides a related creation-and-life background for the Gospel's Spirit language. Direct literary dependence in every detail is not established by vocabulary alone.

### 23.9 The connection to John 20:19–23 — the risen Jesus breathes

John 20:22 records:

> “And when he had said this, he breathed on them, and saith unto them, Receive ye the Holy Ghost:” (KJV)

The Greek verb translated “breathed” is ἐμφυσάω (*emphysaō*). The Septuagint uses this same Greek verb in Genesis 2:7 for God's breathing into the man, and in Ezekiel 37:9 for the breath entering the slain. This is a particularly strong lexical connection, not merely a general resemblance between two passages about air or life.

John's use of this uncommon verb makes Genesis 2:7 and Ezekiel 37:9 highly relevant intertexts for John 20:22. The risen Jesus breathes upon the disciples and commands them to receive the Holy Ghost. The scene can therefore be read within the scriptural pattern of divine life-giving breath and restoration.

A crucial distinction remains: in Genesis, the LORD God breathes into the first man; in Ezekiel, the prophet is commanded to prophesy to the breath, and the breath enters the slain; in John, Jesus Himself breathes upon the disciples and commands reception of the Holy Ghost. The narrative actions are related by language and theme, but the actors, recipients, and immediate contexts differ.

The lexical connection strongly supports a new-creation resonance in John 20:22. It does not, by itself, settle whether John 20:22 is the complete fulfillment of every promise concerning the Spirit or how it relates temporally to Pentecost.

### 23.10 A further connection — Ezekiel 37 and the restoration of one people

Ezekiel 37 does not end with the dry bones. Verses 15–28 describe the joining of the two sticks, the reunification of Israel and Judah, one king, covenant peace, and God's sanctuary among His people.

This continuation matters. The breath-and-life vision belongs to a larger restoration movement:
- life from the death-like condition of exile (vv. 1–14);
- reunification of the divided people (vv. 15–23);
- one shepherd and king (v. 24);
- covenant of peace and lasting sanctuary (vv. 25–28).

John's Gospel contains its own language of gathering into one: Jesus will gather the scattered children of God into one (John 11:51–52), and He speaks of one flock and one shepherd (John 10:16). The thematic correspondence with Ezekiel 37:15–28 is notable. It should be investigated as a possible additional Ezekiel connection, while avoiding a claim that every detail is a direct quotation.

This means the dry-bones vision cannot be treated as an isolated breath illustration. Its setting includes the restoration and unity of God's people under His appointed shepherd.

### 23.11 What the combined sequence establishes — and what it does not

**Explicit in the texts:**
- Genesis 2:7: God forms man and breathes the breath of life; man becomes a living soul.
- Ezekiel 36:25–27: God promises cleansing, a new heart, a new spirit, and His Spirit within Israel, producing obedience.
- Ezekiel 37:1–14: the bones are assembled but remain breathless until breath enters; God interprets the vision as the restoration of the whole house of Israel.
- John 3:5–8: birth of water and Spirit is necessary; the Spirit's action is not controlled by the human observer.
- John 20:22: the risen Jesus breathes upon the disciples and commands them to receive the Holy Ghost.

**Strongly supported connections:**
- Ezekiel 36:25–27 is a major canonical background for John 3:5.
- Ezekiel 37:9 intentionally echoes Genesis 2:7 through the shared Hebrew verb for breathing.
- John 20:22 evokes Genesis 2:7 and Ezekiel 37:9 through the shared Greek verb ἐμφυσάω in the Septuagint.
- John 20:22 carries a strong new-creation/life-giving resonance.
- Ezekiel 36 and 37 belong together: the promise of God's Spirit within the people is dramatized in the vision of the breath entering the slain.

**Not established yet:**
- That Ezekiel 37 is exclusively a prediction of Pentecost.
- That John 20:22 and Acts 2 describe an identical event.
- That John 20:22 must be only symbolic because of its new-creation imagery.
- That every use of Hebrew *rûaḥ* or Greek *pneuma* has the same referent.
- That Ezekiel's corporate restoration oracle can be reduced to an individual conversion formula.
- That the lexical echo alone proves every detail of John's intended theology.

### 23.12 Triple Constraint assessment

- **Biblical compliance — PASS within scope:** Ezekiel 36–37 is first read in its own setting of Israel's defilement, exile, restoration, and covenant hope. The divine interpretation of the bones is retained. Genesis 2:7 and John 20:22 are compared through the shared breath language without making the events identical.
- **Scientific coherence — PASS within scope:** this is literary, lexical, and canonical analysis. The vision is not misrepresented as a biological experiment or a physical mechanism. No scientific claim about the material transfer of a measurable substance is inferred from the prophetic imagery.
- **GUA-W consistency — PASS:** the LORD is the originating actor; the word is spoken under His command; the breath/Spirit gives life; the restored people live and are placed in the land. The bones do not assemble or animate themselves, and the prophet does not claim authorship of the life-giving act.

**Disposition:** Ezekiel 36–37 provides a substantial prophetic bridge between Genesis 2:7 and the Johannine passages. The strongest connection is the shared breath verb linking Genesis 2:7, Ezekiel 37:9, and John 20:22 in the Septuagint/Greek New Testament. Ezekiel 36 also provides a strong background for John 3:5. The new-creation reading of John 20:22 is strongly supported, but its precise relation to Pentecost remains unresolved. U-008 remains UNRESOLVED. No new GUA-W Gear is promoted by this pass.

## 24. Ezekiel 37:15–28 — one people, one king, one shepherd, and God's dwelling

The dry-bones vision (37:1–14) is followed immediately by a second enacted sign: two sticks are joined into one in Ezekiel's hand (37:15–23). The LORD then interprets the sign and expands the promise into one reunited people under one king and shepherd, living in covenant peace with God's sanctuary among them (37:24–28). This continuation prevents the breath vision from being isolated from its stated restoration horizon.

### 24.1 The two sticks — the LORD interprets the sign

Ezekiel is commanded to take one stick for Judah and the children of Israel associated with him, and another for Joseph/Ephraim and the house of Israel associated with him (vv. 16–17). He joins them into one stick in his hand. When the people ask what this means, the LORD supplies the interpretation (vv. 18–20).

The two sticks represent the divided kingdoms and their people. The LORD promises to gather the children of Israel from the nations, bring them into their own land, and make them one nation under one king (vv. 21–22). They will no longer be divided into two kingdoms.

The passage therefore moves from life-restoration imagery to national reunification. The sign is not an abstract illustration of two objects becoming one; it is explicitly interpreted as the LORD's action to reunite His scattered people.

**Explicit:** two sticks signify the divided houses; the LORD will gather, reunite, and establish them as one people in the land under one king.

**Not established:** that the two sticks directly represent two individuals, two churches, or two abstract systems. Such applications require independent canonical warrant.

### 24.2 Cleansing and covenant belonging — Ezekiel 37:23

The LORD promises to save the people from their backslidings and cleanse them. They will be His people, and He will be their God.

This links the second half of Ezekiel 37 back to Ezekiel 36:25–27. Cleansing, the new heart, God's Spirit, and restored obedience in chapter 36 are not detached from the reunification and covenant life of chapter 37. The chapters together describe the LORD's comprehensive restoration of His people.

### 24.3 One shepherd and one king — Ezekiel 37:24–25

The LORD declares:

> “And David my servant shall be king over them; and they all shall have one shepherd...” (Ezekiel 37:24, KJV)

The passage identifies one ruler, described as “David my servant,” who will shepherd the reunited people. In the prophetic setting, this is a future Davidic ruler; the text does not name the historical David as returning to reign in his former lifetime. Later canonical interpretation connects the promised Davidic shepherd-king with the Messiah, but the movement from Ezekiel's oracle to Jesus must be demonstrated from the New Testament rather than assumed.

The people will walk in God's judgments, observe His statutes, and dwell in the land promised to Jacob (vv. 24–25). The shepherd's rule is connected with covenant obedience and enduring habitation.

### 24.4 Covenant of peace and sanctuary — Ezekiel 37:26–28

The LORD promises a covenant of peace, establishes and multiplies the people, and sets His sanctuary in their midst for evermore. His tabernacle will be with them; He will be their God, and they will be His people. The nations will know that the LORD sanctifies Israel when His sanctuary is among them.

The restoration goal is therefore larger than the return of breath to the slain. It includes a reunited people, a Davidic shepherd-king, covenant peace, God's continuing presence, and public recognition among the nations.

This is the stated horizon of Ezekiel 37:1–28. The breath vision in verses 1–14 and the sticks/king/sanctuary vision in verses 15–28 belong to one extended restoration oracle.

### 24.5 John 10:14–18 — Jesus, the good shepherd

Jesus declares Himself the good shepherd who knows His sheep and lays down His life for them (John 10:14–15). He also says:

> “And other sheep I have, which are not of this fold: them also I must bring, and they shall hear my voice; and there shall be one fold, and one shepherd.” (John 10:16, KJV)

The verbal and thematic correspondence with Ezekiel 37:24 is substantial: one shepherd gathers a people into unity. John 10 adds the inclusion of “other sheep” who are not of the existing fold. The unity comes through the shepherd's bringing and the sheep's hearing His voice.

The comparison is not merely a shared shepherd metaphor. Ezekiel's promised reunification under one shepherd and John's one flock under Jesus belong to a coherent canonical pattern. Still, John 10:16 does not quote Ezekiel 37:24 by name, and its “other sheep” should be interpreted within John's Gospel and its wider canonical setting.

John 10:17–18 adds that Jesus lays down His life and takes it again, acting under the command received from the Father. The shepherd's gathering mission is thus bound to Jesus' death and resurrection in John's own account.

### 24.6 John 11:45–53 — gathering the scattered children of God

After the raising of Lazarus, the chief priests and Pharisees convene. Caiaphas declares that it is expedient for one man to die for the people rather than the whole nation perish (John 11:49–50). The narrator then explains that Caiaphas spoke prophetically, not of himself: Jesus would die for the nation, and not for that nation only, but also to gather together in one the children of God that were scattered abroad (vv. 51–52).

The narrator's explanation is especially important. It gives Jesus' death a gathering purpose that extends beyond the immediate nation. The language of gathering scattered people resonates with Ezekiel's promises to gather Israel from the nations (Ezekiel 37:21), while the resulting unity resonates with the one people under one shepherd (37:22, 24).

The correspondence is strong at the level of theme and canonical pattern. It should not be claimed that John 11:52 is a direct quotation of Ezekiel 37; the wording and context differ. John's explicit claim is that Jesus' death serves the gathering of God's scattered children into one.

### 24.7 The shepherd, the gathering, and the breath vision — relation without collapse

The sequence across Ezekiel and John can now be stated carefully:

- **Ezekiel 37:1–14:** the LORD restores life to the death-like house of Israel; the breath enters and the people live.
- **Ezekiel 37:15–23:** the LORD reunites the divided and scattered people as one nation.
- **Ezekiel 37:24–28:** one Davidic shepherd-king rules; covenant peace and God's sanctuary remain among the people.
- **John 10:14–18:** Jesus identifies Himself as the good shepherd, lays down His life, takes it again, and brings the other sheep into one flock under one shepherd.
- **John 11:51–52:** the narrator explains that Jesus dies not only for the nation but to gather the scattered children of God into one.

This produces a coherent Christological reading: Jesus' death and resurrection are connected with shepherding, gathering, and unity. Ezekiel's restoration promise supplies a substantial prophetic pattern for these Johannine themes.

The distinction of contexts remains. Ezekiel speaks in the setting of Israel's exile and promised restoration, including the land and Davidic rule. John presents Jesus' mission and the gathering of God's scattered children. The New Testament's application broadens the horizon, but it does not authorize erasing Ezekiel's original referent.

### 24.8 Does this settle the identity of “David my servant”?

Ezekiel 37:24–25's Davidic ruler is naturally read in the wider biblical expectation of a future Davidic shepherd-king (compare 2 Samuel 7:12–16; Psalm 89:3–4, 20–29; Isaiah 9:6–7; Jeremiah 23:5–6; 30:9; 33:14–17; Ezekiel 34:23–24). John 10's presentation of Jesus as the good shepherd and John 11's account of His gathering mission support a Christian canonical identification of Jesus with this promised shepherd-king.

However, Ezekiel 37 itself does not name Jesus of Nazareth. The identification is a canonical and Christological conclusion drawn by reading the prophetic promise alongside the New Testament witness. Keep the source text and later identification distinguishable.

### 24.9 What this adds to the John 20:22 investigation

The breath/Spirit question is now set within a larger restoration purpose. Ezekiel 37 does not end with the breath entering the slain. It proceeds to one people, one shepherd-king, covenant peace, and God's dwelling among His people.

John's Gospel similarly connects:
- Jesus' identity as shepherd (John 10);
- His death for the nation and gathering of scattered children (John 11);
- His death and resurrection (John 10:17–18; 19–20);
- His breathing upon the disciples and command to receive the Holy Ghost (John 20:22).

This strengthens the reading of John 20 within the larger new-creation and gathering mission of the risen Christ. It does not establish that the disciples in John 20:22 are themselves the entirety of the gathered people, nor does it settle the chronology or relation of this act to Pentecost.

**Disposition:** Ezekiel 37:15–28 provides the larger restoration horizon for the breath vision: one reunited people, one Davidic shepherd-king, covenant peace, and God's sanctuary among them. John 10:14–18 and 11:51–52 strongly correspond to the one-shepherd and gathering themes.
