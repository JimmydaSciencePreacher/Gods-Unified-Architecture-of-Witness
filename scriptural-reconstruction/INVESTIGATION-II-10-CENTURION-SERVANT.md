# Investigation II.10 — The Centurion's Servant: Authority and the Word
**Primary texts:** Matthew 8:5–13; Luke 7:1–10 (KJV)  
**Related texts:** Psalm 107:20; John 4:46–54; John 5:19–30; John 1:1–3, 14  
**Status:** Comparative textual study recorded; medical mechanism not described.

## Question
What does the centurion understand about authority, and how does Jesus respond to the request for healing at a distance?

## Sequence
- A centurion's servant is grievously tormented / dear to him and sick, ready to die.
- In Matthew, the centurion personally comes and beseeches Jesus. In Luke, he sends Jewish elders, then friends, to speak on his behalf.
- The centurion says he is not worthy that Jesus should come under his roof.
- He asks Jesus to “speak the word only,” believing his servant will be healed.
- He explains authority through his own experience: as a man under authority, he gives commands to soldiers and servants and they obey.
- Jesus marvels and says He has not found so great faith, no, not in Israel.
- The messengers return and find the servant whole.

## Differences to preserve
Matthew narrates the centurion as speaking directly; Luke describes intermediaries and includes the elders' testimony that the centurion loves their nation and built them a synagogue. This may be a narrative representation of speech through agents, but the case study should retain both accounts' forms rather than silently rewriting either one.

## What the text states
The centurion believes Jesus' word is sufficient without His bodily arrival. Jesus commends the centurion's faith. The servant is found healed at the time of the encounter.

## Witness and agency
The centurion requests; elders and friends convey the request in Luke; Jesus speaks; the servant is healed; messengers verify the result. The centurion's military analogy concerns delegated authority in his own sphere. Jesus does not say His authority is merely the same as the centurion's; rather, He commends the centurion's recognition of authority.

## Canonical context
Psalm 107:20 speaks of God sending His word and healing. John 4:46–54 is a close comparison: Jesus speaks at a distance and the recovery is temporally connected to His word. The centurion's account adds an explicit analogy of authority and command. John 1:1–3 and Hebrews 1:1–3 provide the wider canonical identity context of the Word and the Son.

## Limits
The accounts do not provide a diagnosis, clinical measurements, or a physiological mechanism. They do not establish a universal law of remote healing or a general theory of authority in physical systems.

## Triple Constraint
- **Biblical compliance — PASS:** the accounts' differences and the centurion's words are preserved.
- **Scientific coherence — PASS for restraint; medical mechanism UNRESOLVED:** no clinical dataset is supplied.
- **GUA-W consistency — PASS for governing relationship; technical mapping UNRESOLVED:** the centurion recognizes authority; he does not become its source. The healing is attributed to Jesus.

## Finding
The centurion's request is remarkable because he does not insist on Jesus' physical presence. He recognizes that Jesus can command and the servant will be healed. The narrative makes the authority of Jesus, rather than the distance or the messenger chain, the decisive focus.

**The centurion understands delegated command. He asks for the Word. The servant is healed.**
