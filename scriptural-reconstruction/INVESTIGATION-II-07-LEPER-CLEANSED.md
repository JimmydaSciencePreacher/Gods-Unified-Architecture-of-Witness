# Investigation II.7 — The Leper Is Cleansed
**Primary texts:** Matthew 8:1–4; Mark 1:40–45; Luke 5:12–16 (KJV)  
**Related texts:** Leviticus 13–14; 2 Kings 5:1–14; Matthew 11:2–6; John 1:1–3, 14  
**Status:** Comparative textual study recorded; medical identification of the ancient condition remains limited.

## Question
What happens when a man described as full of leprosy approaches Jesus, and how do cleansing, touch, command, priestly testimony, and public report function in the accounts?

## Sequence
- A man with leprosy comes to Jesus. Luke describes him as “full of leprosy.”
- The man worships/beseeches Jesus and says, “Lord, if thou wilt, thou canst make me clean.”
- Jesus puts forth His hand and touches him, saying, “I will; be thou clean.”
- The leprosy immediately departs and the man is cleansed.
- Jesus instructs him to tell no man, but to show himself to the priest and offer the gift Moses commanded for a testimony.
- Mark records that the man spreads the matter widely despite the instruction, affecting Jesus' ability to enter openly into the city.

## Differences to preserve
Matthew emphasizes the man's worship and Jesus' command to show himself to the priest. Mark records the man's approach and Jesus' compassion in many textual translations; the KJV says Jesus was “moved with compassion” in Mark 1:41. Luke identifies the man as full of leprosy and notes that great multitudes gather to hear Jesus and be healed. Each account contributes details.

## What the text states
The man believes Jesus has the ability to cleanse him, while expressing the matter of willingness. Jesus touches and speaks; cleansing occurs immediately. Jesus directs the man toward the Mosaic priestly procedure.

## Witness and agency
The man approaches and asks. Jesus wills, touches, and commands. The man is cleansed. The priest is to inspect and receive the prescribed offering as a testimony. The public later hears the report. The priestly witness is not the source of cleansing.

## Canonical context
Leviticus 13–14 sets out priestly examination and procedures concerning leprosy/skin disease and cleansing. The Gospel instruction to show himself to the priest and offer what Moses commanded is therefore textually significant: Jesus does not present the man's restoration as a reason to erase the Mosaic testimony. Second Kings 5 provides another cleansing narrative, but its details and agency differ; Naaman is instructed through Elisha and must wash in Jordan. Do not conflate the accounts.

The ancient biblical term translated “leprosy” should not automatically be equated with one precise modern diagnosis, including Hansen's disease, in every passage.

## Limits
The Gospel accounts do not provide modern microbiology, diagnostic criteria, or laboratory confirmation. The text describes a dramatic and immediate cleansing; it does not give a medical mechanism.

## Triple Constraint
- **Biblical compliance — PASS:** the command, touch, cleansing, and priestly instruction are explicit.
- **Scientific coherence — PASS for careful terminology; modern diagnosis/mechanism UNRESOLVED:** avoid anachronistic diagnosis.
- **GUA-W consistency — PASS for governing relationship; technical mapping UNRESOLVED:** Jesus is the acting authority; the man and priest are witnesses in distinct roles.

## Finding
The man distinguishes ability from willingness: “thou canst” and “if thou wilt.” Jesus answers both with His own will and command. The cleansing is followed by an instructed public/priestly testimony, not by the healed man becoming the source of the work.

**The man asks. Jesus wills and speaks. The man is cleansed. The priest is to witness.**
