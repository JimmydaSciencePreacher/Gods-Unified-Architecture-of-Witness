

## 14. Foundational review — Genesis 1–2 as the beginning for examining the human will

**Governing correction:** The Answer precedes the question. The question exists within Creation and leads the Witness back to what the Author has already given. Accordingly, this examination does not begin with a modern philosophical definition of free will. It begins with Genesis 1–2, especially Genesis 2:1–5, and then examines the human action narrated in Genesis 3.

### A. What is established before the human transgression

- **Genesis 1:1** identifies God as Creator of heaven and earth. The created order does not originate in mankind.
- **Genesis 2:1–3** declares the heavens, earth, and their host finished; God ends His work, rests, blesses the seventh day, and sanctifies it. Completion belongs to God's work, not to a human decision.
- **Genesis 2:4–6** introduces the generations of heaven and earth and records the conditions concerning field plants, rain, the absence of a man to till, and the mist watering the ground. These verses must be read in their precise scope; verse 5 does not say that no vegetation of any kind existed anywhere.
- **Genesis 2:7** attributes man's formation from the dust and the breathing of the breath of life to the LORD God; man becomes a living soul. Human life is received, not self-authored.
- **Genesis 2:15** records man's placement in the Garden for appointed work: to dress and keep it.
- **Genesis 2:16–17** records God's command, generous permission, specific prohibition, and stated consequence. The governing word precedes the human response.

Genesis 2:1–5 is therefore the foundational return to the completed Creation and its originating order. The direct command and boundary relevant to the Fall are stated in 2:16–17. These passages are related, but their distinct contributions must not be collapsed.

### B. What Genesis 3 shows the human will doing

The narrative does not give an abstract dictionary definition of “will.” It shows human willing in operation through distinguishable actions and responses:

1. **Reception of communication:** the woman hears the serpent's question and engages its competing account (3:1–5).
2. **Recall/report of the command:** she states what God said, though her report differs from the wording recorded in 2:16–17 (3:2–3).
3. **Perception:** she sees the tree (3:6).
4. **Evaluation:** she regards it as good for food, pleasant to the eyes, and desirable for wisdom (3:6).
5. **Desire:** the text expressly describes attraction/desirability (3:6).
6. **Action:** she takes and eats, gives to her husband, and he eats (3:6).
7. **Heeding:** God later identifies Adam's hearkening to his wife's voice in connection with his eating contrary to the command (3:17).
8. **Response to consequence:** the humans know they are naked, make coverings, hide, fear, and answer God's questions (3:7–13).

These are related dimensions of human willing and action, but they are not synonyms. Seeing is not evaluating; evaluation is not desire; desire is not taking; hearing is not necessarily heeding; and action is not authority to establish the governing standard.

### C. What the account establishes about capacity and authority

- Human beings perceive, evaluate, desire, heed, choose, and act. Genesis 3 records these as real human operations.
- The text does not portray the woman or man as physically compelled to eat. It records their own actions and later holds them accountable.
- The serpent's competing testimony challenges God's word; the humans' evaluation and action occur after that challenge. The narrative therefore does not reduce the Fall to the bare existence of choice.
- The human capacity to evaluate does not establish authority to create the order or redefine the Creator's command.
- Genesis 3:22 acknowledges changed knowledge of good and evil. It does not state that God's authority was transferred or that mankind became the Author or independent source of the standard.
- Genesis 2:7 attributes man's formation and life to God. Genesis 2:16–17 attributes the command and boundary to God. Genesis 3 records the human response; it does not transfer those originating acts or authority to the human will.

### D. What Scripture attributes to God in this beginning account

God creates (Genesis 1:1); completes and sanctifies (2:1–3); forms and gives the breath of life (2:7); plants and places (2:8, 15); appoints man's work (2:15); grants permission and gives the prohibition (2:16–17); speaks and judges (3:9–19); and determines the conditions of continued access to the tree of life (3:22–24). The human will operates within a reality and command it did not originate.

### E. Finding and bounded conclusion

**Foundational finding:** In Genesis 2–3, human will is not presented as an independent authorship of reality or authority. The narrative displays a creaturely capacity to receive or heed communication, perceive, evaluate, desire, choose, and act. Those operations are real and morally accountable, but they do not make the human the Creator, the source of life, or the author of the governing command.

**What it can do in the account:** perceive, assess, desire, heed, choose, act, and respond.

**What it is not shown to do:** create itself, originate the created order, issue the Creator's command, or acquire authority to redefine the standard by which the action is judged.

**What the text attributes to God:** creation, completion, man's formation and life, provision, appointed purpose, command, boundary, evaluation, judgment, and the guarding of access to life.

**Limit:** This is a Scriptural account of the will's operation in the beginning narrative, not an exhaustive philosophical or physiological definition of every faculty of human willing. Do not mistake that limit for absence of an answer in the beginning. Later canonical passages may illuminate and confirm this foundation; they must not replace it or reverse its order.

**U-006 disposition:** NARROWED TO A FOUNDATIONAL ANSWER. The beginning account establishes the principal operations of human willing and the distinction between creaturely capacity and the Author's authority. The demand for an exhaustive abstract definition is not established by Genesis 1–3 and must not be used to keep the whole question perpetually unresolved.
