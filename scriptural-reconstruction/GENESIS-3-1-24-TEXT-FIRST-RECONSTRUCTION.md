

## 14. Foundational review — Genesis 1–2 as the beginning for examining the human will

**Governing correction:** The Answer precedes the question. The question exists within Creation and leads the Witness back to what the Author has already given. Accordingly, this examination does not begin with a modern philosophical definition of free will. It begins with Genesis 1–2, especially Genesis 2:1–5, and then examines the human action narrated in Genesis 3.

### A. What is established before the human transgression

- **Genesis 1:1** identifies God as Creator of heaven and earth. The created order does not originate in mankind.
- **Genesis 2:1–3** declares the heavens, earth, and their host finished; God ends His work, rests, blesses the seventh day, and sanctifies it. Completion belongs to God's work, not to a human decision.
- **Genesis 2:4–6** introduces the generations of heaven and earth and records the conditions concerning field plants, rain, the absence of a man to till, and the mist watering the ground. These verses must be read in their precise scope; verse 5 does not say that no vegetation of any kind existed anywhere.
- **Genesis 2:7** attributes man's formation from the dust and the breathing of the breath of life to the LORD God; man becomes a living soul. Human life is received, not self-authored.
- **Genesis 2:15** records man's placement in the Garden for appointed work: to dress and keep it.
- **Genesis 2:16–17** records God's command, generous permission, specific prohibition, and stated consequence. The governing word precedes the human response.

Genesis 2:1–5 is therefore the foundational return to the completed Creation and its originating order. The direct command and boundary relevant to the Fall are stated in 2:16–17. These passages are related, but their distinct contributions must not be collapsed.

### B. What Genesis 3 shows the human will doing

The narrative does not give an abstract dictionary definition of “will.” It shows human willing in operation through distinguishable actions and responses:

1. **Reception of communication:** the woman hears the serpent's question and engages its competing account (3:1–5).
2. **Recall/report of the command:** she states what God said, though her report differs from the wording recorded in 2:16–17 (3:2–3).
3. **Perception:** she sees the tree (3:6).
4. **Evaluation:** she regards it as good for food, pleasant to the eyes, and desirable for wisdom (3:6).
5. **Desire:** the text expressly describes attraction/desirability (3:6).
6. **Action:** she takes and eats, gives to her husband, and he eats (3:6).
7. **Heeding:** God later identifies Adam's hearkening to his wife's voice in connection with his eating contrary to the command (3:17).
8. **Response to consequence:** the humans know they are naked, make coverings, hide, fear, and answer God's questions (3:7–13).

These are related dimensions of human willing and action, but they are not synonyms. Seeing is not evaluating; evaluation is not desire; desire is not taking; hearing is not necessarily heeding; and action is not authority to establish the governing standard.

### C. What the account establishes about capacity and authority

- Human beings perceive, evaluate, desire, heed, choose, and act. Genesis 3 records these as real human operations.
- The text does not portray the woman or man as physically compelled to eat. It records their own actions and later holds them accountable.
- The serpent's competing testimony challenges God's word; the humans' evaluation and action occur after that challenge. The narrative therefore does not reduce the Fall to the bare existence of choice.
- The human capacity to evaluate does not establish authority to create the order or redefine the Creator's command.
- Genesis 3:22 acknowledges changed knowledge of good and evil. It does not state that God's authority was transferred or that mankind became the Author or independent source of the standard.
- Genesis 2:7 attributes man's formation and life to God. Genesis 2:16–17 attributes the command and boundary to God. Genesis 3 records the human response; it does not transfer those originating acts or authority to the human will.

### D. What Scripture attributes to God in this beginning account

God creates (Genesis 1:1); completes and sanctifies (2:1–3); forms and gives the breath of life (2:7); plants and places (2:8, 15); appoints man's work (2:15); grants permission and gives the prohibition (2:16–17); speaks and judges (3:9–19); and determines the conditions of continued access to the tree of life (3:22–24). The human will operates within a reality and command it did not originate.

### E. Finding and bounded conclusion

**Foundational finding:** In Genesis 2–3, human will is not presented as an independent authorship of reality or authority. The narrative displays a creaturely capacity to receive or heed communication, perceive, evaluate, desire, choose, and act. Those operations are real and morally accountable, but they do not make the human the Creator, the source of life, or the author of the governing command.

**What it can do in the account:** perceive, assess, desire, heed, choose, act, and respond.

**What it is not shown to do:** create itself, originate the created order, issue the Creator's command, or acquire authority to redefine the standard by which the action is judged.

**What the text attributes to God:** creation, completion, man's formation and life, provision, appointed purpose, command, boundary, evaluation, judgment, and the guarding of access to life.

**Limit:** This is a Scriptural account of the will's operation in the beginning narrative, not an exhaustive philosophical or physiological definition of every faculty of human willing. Do not mistake that limit for absence of an answer in the beginning. Later canonical passages may illuminate and confirm this foundation; they must not replace it or reverse its order.

**U-006 disposition:** NARROWED TO A FOUNDATIONAL ANSWER. The beginning account establishes the principal operations of human willing and the distinction between creaturely capacity and the Author's authority. The demand for an exhaustive abstract definition is not established by Genesis 1–3 and must not be used to keep the whole question perpetually unresolved.


## 15. Genesis 3:8–24 — divine questioning, human response, judgment, provision, and guarded access

This section continues the same beginning-first examination. Genesis 3:8–24 is not an appendix to the question of human willing: it shows what the humans do after eating, how they respond when addressed by God, and how God acts in judgment and in the continuing order of life.

### A. Genesis 3:8–13 — hiding, calling, questioning, and answers

- **3:8:** The man and woman hear the voice of the LORD God and hide among the trees. Their response to God's presence is fear-driven concealment, not an ability to remove themselves from His presence or authority.
- **3:9:** The LORD God calls to the man, “Where art thou?” The question initiates the encounter; God is the One who calls. The text does not suggest that God lacks knowledge or is seeking information for His own correction.
- **3:10:** Adam reports that he heard God's voice, was afraid because he was naked, and hid. He identifies fear and nakedness as his stated reasons.
- **3:11:** God asks who told him he was naked and whether he had eaten from the prohibited tree. The question directs attention to the prior command and the human act.
- **3:12:** Adam answers by referring to “the woman whom thou gavest to be with me” and says she gave him the fruit. His answer identifies another person's action and points back to God's gift, but it does not deny that he ate.
- **3:13:** The woman says the serpent beguiled her and she ate. Her answer identifies deception and her own eating.

**Finding:** The humans retain the capacity to hear, fear, hide, answer, explain, and attribute responsibility. The responses also reveal evasion and displacement of attention: Adam points to the woman and to God's giving; the woman points to the serpent's beguiling. Neither answer changes the recorded acts of eating. The narrative preserves both the influence of another's communication and the actors' own participation.

**Boundary:** The text records the serpent's beguiling and the humans' answers. It does not say that deception erased their agency or that their agency made the serpent's deception unreal. Do not flatten these distinct factors into one.

### B. Genesis 3:14–19 — God's judgments and the consequences of disobedience

- **3:14–15:** God addresses the serpent with a curse and declares enmity between the serpent and the woman, and between their respective seeds. The passage establishes hostility and an ongoing conflict in the terms stated. Later canonical interpretation may illuminate the promised seed; the immediate text must first be allowed to say what it says.
- **3:16:** God addresses the woman concerning multiplied sorrow/conception and childbirth, her relation to her husband, and his rule. This is a divine pronouncement following the transgression. Do not convert the verse into a command authorizing every form of human domination, nor erase the verse's actual wording.
- **3:17:** God identifies Adam's hearkening to his wife's voice and eating from the tree God had commanded him not to eat. The ground is cursed for his sake; sorrowful labor and its produce are described.
- **3:18:** Thorns and thistles, and the eating of the field's herb, describe the altered conditions of man's labor.
- **3:19:** Man eats bread in the sweat of his face until he returns to the ground from which he was taken; the verse explicitly recalls his formation from dust and declares his return to dust.

**Finding:** God names the acts and assigns judgment. The human will is not the authority that determines the consequence of its own act. Adam's heeding is expressly identified by God as part of the account of his transgression; the judgment also reaches the ground and the conditions of human labor. The text distinguishes the serpent, the woman, the man, and the ground rather than presenting one undifferentiated consequence.

**Boundary:** These verses describe judgments and consequences. They do not authorize an interpreter to infer that every instance of suffering, childbirth difficulty, marital conflict, or agricultural hardship can be mapped one-to-one to a particular personal sin.

### C. Genesis 3:20–21 — naming and God's provision

- **3:20:** Adam calls his wife's name Eve, with the stated reason that she was the mother of all living. The naming occurs after the judgment pronouncements and before the expulsion.
- **3:21:** The LORD God makes coats of skins and clothes them. The text explicitly attributes this provision to God.

**Finding:** Even after the transgression and judgment, the narrative records continued human life and a concrete provision made by God. The text says God made garments of skins. It does not explicitly describe a sacrifice, name an animal, or explain a ritual mechanism here; those claims must not be inserted as though stated in this verse.

### D. Genesis 3:22–24 — mortality, expulsion, and guarded access to life

- **3:22:** The LORD God speaks of the man having become “as one of us, to know good and evil,” and states the concern that he might take also of the tree of life, eat, and live for ever.
- **3:23:** The LORD God sends the man forth from the Garden of Eden to till the ground from which he was taken.
- **3:24:** God drives out the man and places cherubims and a flaming sword east of the Garden to keep the way of the tree of life.

**Finding:** The passage explicitly presents God as the One who determines the man's continued access to the Garden and the tree of life. The man does not secure immortality by his own initiative. The guarded way is a divine action and boundary. This follows the earlier pattern: God gives life, establishes the command, judges the transgression, provides clothing, and governs access to the tree of life.

**Boundary:** Genesis 3:22–24 states the action and stated concern about eating from the tree of life and living forever. Do not add an unstated theory about the tree's biological mechanism or claim that the passage transfers divine authority to mankind. “As one of us” must be interpreted within the verse and the wider canon; it is not a declaration that man became God.

### E. Integrated finding for Genesis 3:1–24

The whole chapter shows more than a single “choice.” It records a sequence of distinguishable human operations and responses: hearing competing speech; reporting a command; seeing; evaluating; desiring; taking; eating; giving; heeding; recognizing; covering; hiding; fearing; answering; attributing; and continuing to act within consequences. It also records God's initiating and governing actions: speaking the command, calling the humans, questioning, identifying acts, pronouncing judgment, providing clothing, sending the man from the Garden, and guarding the way to the tree of life.

**The human will acts, but it does not become the Author.** Human action is real and accountable; it does not originate Creation, life, the command, the judgment, or the boundary governing access to life. The chapter does not teach that human capacity is unreal, nor that human capacity is sovereign.

### F. U-006 refinement

The Genesis 3 narrative supplies a substantial beginning-account answer to the question of human willing. It distinguishes capacities and operations, demonstrates accountability, and establishes the Creator/creature boundary. The remaining limitation is not that Genesis 3 says nothing about the will; it is that the narrative does not present a formal exhaustive taxonomy of every human faculty or answer every later doctrinal question about the source and operation of saving faith.

The latter questions must be taken back to the Beginning first and then compared with the relevant canonical witnesses. They must not be smuggled into Genesis 3 or treated as though its narrative account were silent.


## 16. Genesis 3:15 — the divinely declared conflict and the canonical witness to victory

### A. Begin with what Genesis 3:15 actually says

KJV: “And I will put enmity between thee and the woman, and between thy seed and her seed; it shall bruise thy head, and thou shalt bruise his heel.”

The verse is spoken by God to the serpent within the judgments of Genesis 3:14–19. Its explicit features are:

1. **Divine initiative:** “I will put.” The enmity is established by God's declaration; it is not first authored by the woman, mankind, or the serpent.
2. **Two parties:** the serpent and the woman.
3. **Two seeds/offspring:** the serpent's seed and the woman's seed. The Hebrew noun *zeraʿ* can refer to seed or offspring and can function collectively; the verse's wording must not be forced into an exclusively individual or exclusively collective sense before the wider context is examined.
4. **Conflict and injury:** the verse describes the serpent's head being bruised and the woman's seed's heel being bruised. The wording establishes conflict and injury, with an evident asymmetry in the body parts named. The verse itself does not narrate the later event, name Jesus, specify a chronology, or explain the mechanics of the victory.

The KJV's “it” in “it shall bruise thy head” should be retained when quoting the KJV. It is not sound to make the English pronoun alone settle every question about the identity and number of the seed. The noun “seed” and the verse's poetic parallelism must be read with the canonical witnesses.

### B. The immediate finding

Genesis 3:15 is not a human proposal or a recovery plan authored by Adam and Eve. God declares what He Himself will put in place: enmity between the serpent and the woman, and between their respective offspring. The conflict and its outcome are announced by the Author after the transgression. The humans do not originate this declaration, and the serpent does not control its terms.

The verse is commonly called the *protoevangelium* (“first Gospel”) in Christian interpretation. That label is a theological description of the verse's canonical significance, not a word appearing in the KJV text. The label must not replace examination of the actual wording.

### C. Canonical witnesses — what later Scripture clarifies

**1. Revelation 12:1–17 — the dragon is identified as the old serpent**

- Revelation 12:9 explicitly identifies the great dragon as “that old serpent, called the Devil, and Satan, which deceiveth the whole world.”
- Revelation 12:1–5 describes a woman, a male child destined to rule the nations with a rod of iron, and the child's being caught up to God and His throne.
- Revelation 12:17 describes the dragon's war against the woman's remaining seed/offspring, identified there by their keeping the commandments of God and having the testimony of Jesus Christ.

**Contribution:** Revelation explicitly identifies the serpent/dragon with Satan and presents a woman, her child, and her remaining offspring in a conflict with him. This is a strong canonical echo of the woman/serpent/seed conflict in Genesis 3:15. The imagery is apocalyptic; the exact identity and scope of the woman and the precise relation of each group in Revelation 12 must be handled in that book's context rather than flattened into a one-to-one identification without argument.

**2. Romans 16:20 — God crushes Satan**

KJV: “And the God of peace shall bruise Satan under your feet shortly.”

**Contribution:** This is the clearest later verbal and thematic correspondence to Genesis 3:15: Satan is named, and the language of bruising is applied to his defeat. The subject is “the God of peace”; the victory is God's action, while “under your feet” includes the addressed believers in the statement. This does not make believers independent authors of Satan's defeat.

**3. Hebrews 2:14–15 — Christ's death defeats the devil's power**

The passage says that the children are partakers of flesh and blood, and that Christ likewise took part of the same, that through death He might destroy the one having the power of death, that is, the devil, and deliver those held in bondage through fear of death.

**Contribution:** Hebrews identifies the incarnation and death of Christ as central to the defeat of the devil's power and the deliverance of those in bondage. It supplies explicit Christological content to the canonical account of victory over the devil. It does not quote Genesis 3:15 directly.

**4. 1 John 3:8 — the Son of God manifested to destroy the works of the devil**

The verse expressly says that the Son of God was manifested for this purpose: that He might destroy the works of the devil.

**Contribution:** The Son's manifestation is directly associated with undoing the devil's works. This supports the canonical reading of Christ's mission in relation to the serpent's opposition, without pretending that 1 John is a direct quotation of Genesis 3:15.

**5. Colossians 2:14–15 — triumph through the Cross**

Paul describes the cancellation of the handwriting of ordinances against us and Christ's disarming of principalities and powers, making a public spectacle of them and triumphing over them in the Cross.

**Contribution:** This locates Christ's triumph over hostile powers in His Cross. It is a relevant canonical witness to victory over evil powers, though Genesis 3:15 is not directly quoted.

**6. Romans 5:12–21 and 1 Corinthians 15:21–22, 45–49 — Adam and Christ**

Romans 5 contrasts the entrance and reign of sin and death through one man with the grace and life that reign through Jesus Christ. First Corinthians 15 contrasts Adam, through whom death came, with Christ, the last Adam, and the resurrection life belonging to Him.

**Contribution:** These passages establish the Adam/Christ relationship and the movement from death in Adam to life in Christ. They illuminate why the Genesis 3 account matters to the Gospel. They do not, by themselves, identify the “seed” in Genesis 3:15; that would conflate Paul's explicit treatment of Adam/Christ with the distinct wording of the serpent/seed promise.

**7. Galatians 3:16 — the seed of Abraham**

Paul explicitly identifies the promised “seed” in the Abrahamic promise as Christ.

**Contribution and boundary:** This is an explicit identification of Christ as the Seed in the Abrahamic promise. It is important canonical evidence for the promised Seed theme, but its immediate subject is the promise to Abraham, not a direct grammatical exposition of Genesis 3:15. The two passages may be considered within the wider canonical story, but they must not be treated as if Paul explicitly said Galatians 3:16 was a quotation or direct explanation of Genesis 3:15.

### D. Canonical synthesis — what can be stated, and with what degree of certainty

The following claims are supported:

- Genesis 3:15 explicitly announces a conflict established by God's initiative between the serpent and the woman and between their respective offspring.
- Revelation 12 explicitly identifies the old serpent as the Devil and Satan and portrays the dragon in conflict with the woman, her child, and her remaining offspring.
- Romans 16:20 explicitly speaks of God bruising Satan, using language that strongly echoes Genesis 3:15.
- Hebrews 2:14–15 and 1 John 3:8 explicitly connect Christ's coming and work with defeating the devil and destroying his works.
- Colossians 2:14–15 locates Christ's triumph over hostile powers in the Cross.
- Romans 5 and 1 Corinthians 15 identify Christ as the last Adam and contrast death in Adam with life in Christ.
- Galatians 3:16 explicitly identifies Christ as the Seed in the Abrahamic promise, but does not directly settle the grammar of Genesis 3:15.

**Canonical conclusion:** The New Testament gives a clear Christ-centered account of God's victory over Satan through the Son's coming, death, and resurrection, and it explicitly promises God's crushing of Satan. Read within this canonical witness, Genesis 3:15 is rightly understood in Christian theology as the first announcement of the conflict that culminates in Christ's victory. However, Genesis 3:15 itself does not name Jesus or supply the full later revelation. The precise relationship between the woman's “seed” in Genesis 3:15 and the individual Messiah is a canonical-theological conclusion drawn from the whole witness, not a claim that the verse alone spells out every detail.

### E. GUA-W boundary and placement

This finding does not add Jesus Christ as a component of GUA-W, nor does it make GUA-W the Author or the Gospel. It records a canonical relationship within the Scriptural witness:

- **Author:** God declares the enmity and the promised outcome.
- **Word/Son:** the New Testament identifies the Son's coming and work in the defeat of the devil.
- **Creation/history:** the conflict unfolds within the created and historical order.
- **Witness:** Scripture records the promise and its later revelation; believers bear testimony but do not originate the victory.

The map remains distinct from the Author and from Christ. The investigation recognizes the relationship Scripture itself reveals; it does not construct or revise the completed GUA-W.

### F. Questions that remain properly bounded

1. The exact scope of “thy seed” and “her seed” in the immediate Genesis 3:15 context requires care; *zeraʿ* can be collective, and the verse's parallelism does not by itself settle every detail.
2. The precise identity and symbolism of the woman in Revelation 12 remain matters of interpretation; Revelation's explicit identification of the dragon as Satan is not uncertain.
3. The timing and stages of the victory are developed across the New Testament; Genesis 3:15 itself does not provide a detailed chronology.
4. The Abrahamic seed in Galatians 3:16 must not be conflated with a direct grammatical explanation of Genesis 3:15.

These are bounded interpretive questions, not evidence that the Answer is missing. The correct method remains: begin with the declaration in Genesis, then follow the canonical witnesses in their own contexts.
