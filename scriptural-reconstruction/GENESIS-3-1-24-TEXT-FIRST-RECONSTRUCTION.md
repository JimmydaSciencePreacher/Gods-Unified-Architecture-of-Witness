# Genesis 3:1–24 — Text-First Reconstruction, Unit 8

**Status:** Text-first reconstruction of Genesis 3:1–24; settled conclusions are distinguished from questions that remain open  
**Scope:** KJV Genesis 3:1–24, read in continuity with Genesis 2:15–25  
**Question:** What does Genesis 3 actually record about the serpent's communication, human perception and action, changed knowledge, divine judgment, and the guarding of life? What does the chapter record about human perception, desire, action, changed knowledge, and the Author's authority?  
**Method:** Preserve the KJV as the project's base text. Examine Hebrew forms where they materially clarify the wording. Distinguish direct textual statements, grammatical observations, interpretive proposals, and unanswered questions. Do not import a modern philosophical definition of free will into the passage.

## 1. Prior work and source discipline

This unit follows the text-first reconstruction of Genesis 2:18–25 and the consolidated Genesis 1–2 findings. It investigates the events in their Scriptural context and recognizes their relationship to the completed GUA-W. It does not create or add an architectural node.

The primary Scriptural text is KJV Genesis 3:1–24. The Hebrew text and grammar are consulted to clarify wording, not to replace the KJV. The NET Bible notes are used as a secondary linguistic and translation resource; their interpretive conclusions are not treated as the text itself. Where grammatical or interpretive options remain, they are recorded rather than silently resolved.

## 2. Genesis 3:1–5 — The serpent's communication

### Genesis 3:1 — Craftiness and the question

> Now the serpent was more subtil than any beast of the field which the LORD God had made. And he said unto the woman, Yea, hath God said, Ye shall not eat of every tree of the garden?

**Text states**
- The serpent is described as more subtle than the beasts of the field made by the LORD God.
- The serpent speaks to the woman.
- The serpent frames God's instruction as a question about eating from every tree.

**Hebrew observation**
- עָרוּם (*ʿārûm*) is translated “subtil” in the KJV; its range can include clever, shrewd, prudent, or crafty, with context determining the sense.
- There is a Hebrew wordplay between עֲרוּמִּים (*ʿărummîm*, “naked,” Genesis 2:25) and עָרוּם (*ʿārûm*, “shrewd/crafty,” Genesis 3:1). The words are related in sound but are not the same form or meaning.

**Textual caution**
The serpent's opening is elliptical in Hebrew. The NET notes that a phrase such as “Is it really true that God said…?” must be supplied to make the sense explicit. The narrative presents a question that reframes the command; it does not record the serpent asking for a neutral clarification.

### Genesis 3:2–3 — The woman's reply

> And the woman said unto the serpent, We may eat of the fruit of the trees of the garden:
> But of the fruit of the tree which is in the midst of the garden, God hath said, Ye shall not eat of it, neither shall ye touch it, lest ye die.

**Text states**
- The woman answers that they may eat from the garden's trees.
- She identifies a prohibition concerning the tree in the midst of the garden.
- She adds “neither shall ye touch it,” language not present in the recorded command of Genesis 2:17.
- She states the consequence as “lest ye die.”

**Comparison with Genesis 2:16–17**
- God says they may “freely eat” of every tree, with one stated exception.
- The woman's wording omits the emphatic “freely” and adds “neither shall ye touch it.”
- The KJV text therefore records a difference between the original command and the woman's retelling.

The difference is textual. The passage does not directly explain why the woman adds the touch prohibition or whether she received that wording from Adam. Those questions remain open.

### Genesis 3:4–5 — Contradiction and promise

> And the serpent said unto the woman, Ye shall not surely die:
> For God doth know that in the day ye eat thereof, then your eyes shall be opened, and ye shall be as gods, knowing good and evil.

**Text states**
- The serpent directly contradicts the stated death warning.
- The serpent attributes a motive to God: God knows that eating will open their eyes and make them “as gods, knowing good and evil.”
- The serpent's account links eating, opened eyes, and knowledge/likeness.

**Hebrew observation**
The root יָדַע (*yādaʿ*, H3045) occurs twice in verse 5:
- יֹדֵעַ (*yōḏēaʿ*) — Qal participle masculine singular, “knowing/knows,” with God as the grammatical subject of the first clause.
- יֹדְעֵי (*yōḏəʿê*) — Qal participle masculine plural construct, “knowing,” in the comparison clause.

The phrase כֵּאלֹהִים (*kēʾlōhîm*) may be rendered “like God” or “like divine beings/gods.” The KJV reads “as gods.” The function of the plural participle and the referent of *Elohim* in this comparison are disputed. The wording should not be flattened into one uncontested English interpretation.

**Interpretive boundary**
Verse 5 promises a change in awareness and a likeness associated with knowing good and evil. It does not use the phrase “autonomous will,” nor does it explicitly say that humanity will become the originating authority or author of the standard of good and evil.

## 3. Genesis 3:6–7 — Evaluation, action, and new awareness

### Genesis 3:6 — Seeing, evaluating, desiring, taking, eating

> And when the woman saw that the tree was good for food, and that it was pleasant to the eyes, and a tree to be desired to make one wise, she took of the fruit thereof, and did eat, and gave also unto her husband with her; and he did eat.

**Text states**
The verse narrates a sequence:
1. The woman sees the tree.
2. She regards it as good for food.
3. She regards it as pleasant/desirable to the eyes.
4. She regards it as desirable for making one wise.
5. She takes and eats.
6. She gives to her husband, who is described as with her, and he eats.

**Hebrew observations**
- רָאָה (*rāʾâ*) — see.
- טוֹב (*ṭôḇ*) — good.
- תַּאֲוָה (*taʾăwāh*) — desire/desirable.
- נֶחְמָד (*neḥmād*) — desirable/pleasant, from a different root than *taʾăwāh*.
- לְהַשְׂכִּיל (*ləhaśkîl*) — to make wise / gain insight.
- לָקַח (*lāqaḥ*) — take.
- אָכַל (*ʾākal*) — eat.

The two desire/desirability words overlap in sense but are distinct Hebrew forms. Their verbal roots also occur in the prohibition against coveting in Deuteronomy 5:21. This is relevant lexical context, but it does not establish that every instance of desire is itself sinful.

**Correspondence with Genesis 1**
Genesis 1 repeatedly records God seeing that what He made was good. Genesis 3:6 uses the verbs “see” and “good” in the woman's assessment of the tree. This is a lexical correspondence. It may invite literary comparison, but shared vocabulary alone does not prove that the woman's evaluation is a deliberate inversion of every Genesis 1 evaluation.

**Important limit**
The text records the woman's evaluation and action. It does not state that the capacity to see, assess, or desire is inherently evil. The issue in the immediate narrative is that the eating occurs contrary to God's prior command.

The text says Adam was “with her” when she gave him the fruit. It does not explicitly narrate whether he heard the serpent's earlier dialogue or what reasoning preceded his eating.

### Genesis 3:7 — Opened eyes and knowing

> And the eyes of them both were opened, and they knew that they were naked; and they sewed fig leaves together, and made themselves aprons.

**Hebrew observations**
- פָּקַח (*pāqaḥ*) — open, here in a Niphal form: the eyes undergo opening.
- יָדַע (*yādaʿ*) — know, here וַיֵּדְעוּ (*wayyēdəʿû*), Qal wayyiqtol, third-person masculine plural.
- עֵירֻמִּם (*ʿêrummîm*) — naked.

The verse distinguishes the opening of the eyes from the resulting knowledge. It states specifically that they knew they were naked. It does not state that they gained exhaustive knowledge or omniscience.

They then sew fig leaves and make coverings for themselves. The narrative moves from changed awareness to a human attempt at covering.

## 4. Genesis 3:8–13 — Hiding, questioning, and explanation

### Genesis 3:8–10 — Hearing, hiding, fear

> And they heard the voice of the LORD God walking in the garden in the cool of the day: and Adam and his wife hid themselves from the presence of the LORD God amongst the trees of the garden.
>
> And the LORD God called unto Adam, and said unto him, Where art thou?
>
> And he said, I heard thy voice in the garden, and I was afraid, because I was naked; and I hid myself.

**Text states**
- The man and woman hear the voice/sound associated with the LORD God in the garden.
- They hide from His presence.
- God calls to Adam and asks where he is.
- Adam reports hearing, fear, nakedness, and hiding.

**Hebrew observations**
- שָׁמַע (*šāmaʿ*) — hear/listen/heed. In 3:8 and 3:10 the context is hearing.
- קָרָא (*qārāʾ*) — call; with the accompanying construction it may carry the sense of summoning.
- יָרֵא (*yārēʾ*) — fear/be afraid.
- חָבָא (*ḥāḇāʾ*) — hide; the forms in verses 8 and 10 convey hiding oneself.

**Translation caution**
The expression in verse 8 traditionally rendered “in the cool of the day” includes רוּחַ (*rûaḥ*), which can mean wind or spirit, and the participle describing the LORD God is debated. The KJV wording is retained as the base text, but the exact scene should not be used to construct an architectural claim without further study.

The question “Where art thou?” does not require the conclusion that God lacks knowledge of Adam's location. The narrative records a divine call and question; any more specific account of God's motive must be argued from wider context.

### Genesis 3:11 — The source of the awareness is questioned

> And he said, Who told thee that thou wast naked? Hast thou eaten of the tree, whereof I commanded thee that thou shouldest not eat?

**Hebrew observation**
The phrase “Who told thee?” uses a form of נָגַד (*nāgaḏ*), “make known, report, declare”: הִגִּיד (*higgîḏ*), Hiphil perfect, third-person masculine singular.

**Textual finding**
God asks two questions:
1. Who made known to Adam that he was naked?
2. Did Adam eat from the tree God had commanded him not to eat?

The first question makes the source/provenance of Adam's new awareness narratively salient. The second returns directly to the command and the prohibited action.

Adam does not provide a direct answer naming an informant in verse 12. The text therefore raises the source question without recording an explicit identification by Adam.

The question should not be made to prove more than it says. It does not identify the serpent as the direct speaker who told Adam that he was naked; it asks who told him, and Adam instead explains his fear and hiding before referring to the woman.

### Genesis 3:12 — Adam's explanation

> And the man said, The woman whom thou gavest to be with me, she gave me of the tree, and I did eat.

**Text states**
- Adam identifies the woman.
- He describes her as the one God gave to be with him.
- He says she gave him from the tree.
- He acknowledges, “I did eat.”

The Hebrew construction places “the woman” in an emphatic position and resumes her as the subject of the verb “gave.” The relative clause “whom thou gavest to be with me” is inserted into the statement. A NET grammatical note reads this as foregrounding the woman and shifting blame toward God for giving her to Adam. That is a grammatical/interpretive observation, not a direct quotation of Adam's unspoken motive.

**Distinctions**
- Adam acknowledges his own action.
- He identifies the woman's action preceding his.
- He invokes God's giving of the woman in his explanation.

The text does not say Adam was physically compelled to eat. It also does not give a full account of his internal reasoning.

### Genesis 3:13 — The woman identifies deception

> And the LORD God said unto the woman, What is this that thou hast done? And the woman said, The serpent beguiled me, and I did eat.

The verb translated “beguiled” is from נָשָׁא (*nāšāʾ*, H5377), here in the Hiphil perfect with a first-person singular object suffix: “he deceived/misled me.” Its wider use includes misleading someone into false confidence or false expectation.

The woman identifies the serpent's deception and then acknowledges, “I did eat.”

**Distinction**
The narrative records both an influence upon the woman's understanding and her own action. It does not say the serpent physically forced her to eat; nor does the woman's statement erase the deception she identifies.

## 5. Genesis 3:14–19 — Divine judgment and altered conditions

### Genesis 3:14–15 — The serpent and enmity

God addresses the serpent and pronounces a curse. In verse 15 He says He will put enmity between the serpent and the woman, and between their respective seed/offspring.

The Hebrew זֶרַע (*zeraʿ*, seed/offspring) is grammatically singular but can function as a collective noun. The verse describes conflict involving head and heel. The Hebrew verb used for the blow is repeated in both clauses; English translations vary between “bruise,” “strike,” and “attack.”

The verse has received important later theological interpretations, including Christian readings that connect the woman's seed with Christ. Those wider canonical readings should be examined in a separate study alongside the immediate Hebrew grammar. This text-first unit neither rejects nor finalizes those interpretations.

### Genesis 3:16 — The woman

The verse describes increased pain/hardship associated with childbirth and states that the woman's desire will be toward her husband, followed by “he shall rule over thee.”

The Hebrew תְּשׁוּקָה (*təšûqāh*, desire) is disputed in this context. Interpretations include sexual desire and a desire connected with control or dominance. Genesis 4:7 is often brought into the discussion. The lexical and contextual evidence does not warrant treating one interpretation as settled here.

The verse describes a changed relational condition after the transgression. It should not be turned into an unrestricted moral endorsement of domination.

### Genesis 3:17 — Adam heeds another voice

> And unto Adam he said, Because thou hast hearkened unto the voice of thy wife, and hast eaten of the tree, of which I commanded thee, saying, Thou shalt not eat of it: cursed is the ground for thy sake; in sorrow shalt thou eat of it all the days of thy life;

This verse is particularly relevant to the investigation of will.

The verb שָׁמַע (*šāmaʿ*, H8085) occurs in the Qal perfect, second-person masculine singular: שָׁמַעְתָּ (*šāmaʿtā*), “you heard/listened.” In the idiom “listen/hearken to the voice of,” the expression often means to heed or obey.

God connects three things:
- Adam hearkened to his wife's voice.
- Adam ate from the tree.
- God had commanded Adam not to eat from it.

This is stronger and more precise than saying only that Adam “made a choice.” The text links the voice he heeded, the action he performed, and the prior command he violated.

**Limit:** The verse does not condemn hearing one's wife in general. The stated context is Adam's heeding her voice in connection with eating what God had prohibited.

The verse also begins the pronouncement concerning the ground. Man's work with the ground existed before the Fall (Genesis 2:15); the conditions of that work are now described as painful and frustrated.

### Genesis 3:18–19 — Thorns, toil, and return to dust

The ground will bring forth thorns and thistles; Adam will eat by the sweat of his face until he returns to the ground from which he was taken.

This returns the narrative to Genesis 2:7, where God formed man from the dust of the ground. The human being's material origin and mortality are explicitly recalled.

The text describes altered conditions of human life and work. It does not provide a scientific mechanism for the change, and this unit proposes none.

## 6. Genesis 3:20–21 — Naming and provision

### Genesis 3:20 — Eve named

> And Adam called his wife's name Eve; because she was the mother of all living.

Adam names his wife חַוָּה (*Ḥawwāh*, Eve), with the explanation that she is the mother of all living. The explanation contains a sound association with the Hebrew life word family חַי (*ḥay*, living) / חָיָה (*ḥāyāh*, live).

This follows the pronouncement of mortality in verse 19. The juxtaposition of “dust” and “all living” is textual and noteworthy, but the text does not explicitly state Adam's motive or give a full explanation of his theological understanding when naming her.

As in Genesis 2:19–23, naming is not the same action as creating. Adam names; the text attributes the woman's existence to God's prior making of her in Genesis 2:22.

### Genesis 3:21 — God makes garments and clothes them

> Unto Adam also and to his wife did the LORD God make coats of skins, and clothed them.

The text attributes both making the garments and clothing the humans to the LORD God. This contrasts with Genesis 3:7, where the humans sew fig leaves and make coverings for themselves.

**Limit:** The verse does not say how the skins were obtained. It does not explicitly mention an animal's death, a sacrifice, shed blood, or an atonement ritual. Those may be theological connections explored from wider Scripture, but they are not stated in this verse.

The direct observation is that God provides garments and clothes them.

## 7. Genesis 3:22 — God acknowledges changed knowledge

> And the LORD God said, Behold, the man is become as one of us, to know good and evil: and now, lest he put forth his hand, and take also of the tree of life, and eat, and live for ever:

This verse must be read in direct comparison with Genesis 3:5.

**Hebrew observations**
- הֵן (*hēn*) — behold / now; introduces the foundational assertion.
- הָיָה (*hāyâ*) — be/become; here the clause is rendered in the KJV “is become.”
- כְּאַחַד מִמֶּנּוּ (*kəʾaḥad mimmennû*) — “as one of us.”
- לָדַעַת (*lādaʿat*) — Qal infinitive construct of יָדַע (*yādaʿ*), “to know.”
- טוֹב וָרָע (*ṭôḇ wā-rāʿ*) — good and evil.

The infinitive “to know” explains the respect in which the man is said to have become like one of “us.” The plural referent “us” is not identified within the verse; interpretations of the divine plural require wider Scriptural and historical study.

### Comparison: Genesis 3:5 and 3:22

| Feature | Genesis 3:5 | Genesis 3:22 |
|---|---|---|
| Speaker | Serpent | LORD God |
| Comparison | “as gods” in the KJV | “as one of us” |
| Knowledge | “knowing good and evil” | “to know good and evil” |
| Root | יָדַע (*yādaʿ*) | יָדַע (*yādaʿ*) |
| Narrative function | Prediction/promise by the serpent | Divine acknowledgement of a changed condition |

The forms differ:
- 3:5 uses יֹדֵעַ (*yōḏēaʿ*) for “God knows,” and יֹדְעֵי (*yōḏəʿê*) for “knowing.”
- 3:22 uses לָדַעַת (*lādaʿat*), an infinitive construct.

The correspondence is real. God acknowledges that man has become like one of “us” with respect to knowing good and evil. The verse does not say that man has become God in every respect, become the Creator, or received God's authority to define the standard of good and evil.

**Unresolved distinction**
“Knowing good and evil” may involve discernment, evaluative judgment, experiential knowledge, moral knowledge, or another related sense. Usage elsewhere in Scripture can illuminate the expression, but it must be examined passage by passage. The phrase alone does not define every dimension of the changed knowledge. The separate authority question is resolved: the text records no transfer to humanity of God's authority or of authority to establish the standard.

### The tree of life

The verse continues with the possibility that man might stretch out his hand, take also from the tree of life, eat, and live forever. The tree of life is distinguished from the tree of the knowledge of good and evil.

The verse's final clause is elliptical in Hebrew. Verse 23 supplies the narrated action: God sends the man out of the garden. The narrative therefore records a restriction on further access to the tree of life, not a transfer of God's authority to humanity.

## 8. Genesis 3:23–24 — Sent out; the way to life guarded

### Genesis 3:23 — Sent from the garden

> Therefore the LORD God sent him forth from the garden of Eden, to till the ground from whence he was taken.

The verb שָׁלַח (*šālaḥ*, send) describes God's sending the man out. The man is sent to work the ground from which he was taken.

Work itself was already present in Genesis 2:15. The change is not that work first begins after the Fall; rather, Genesis 3:17–19 has described the altered conditions under which the ground will be worked.

### Genesis 3:24 — The way to the tree of life guarded

> So he drove out the man; and he placed at the east of the garden of Eden Cherubims, and a flaming sword which turned every way, to keep the way of the tree of life.

The verse describes the man being driven out and cherubim and a flaming sword being placed to guard the way to the tree of life.

**Connection with Genesis 2:15**
- Genesis 2:15 uses שָׁמַר (*šāmar*, keep/guard) for the man's appointed keeping of the garden.
- Genesis 3:24 uses the same root for keeping/guarding the way to the tree of life.

The repeated root is a textual correspondence. The objects differ: the garden in Genesis 2:15 and the way to the tree of life in Genesis 3:24. This does not, by itself, prove that Adam failed a specific guarding duty by allowing the serpent into the garden; Genesis does not explicitly say that.

There is also a possible wordplay between the use of שָׁלַח (*šālaḥ*) for stretching out the hand in 3:22 and God's sending the man out in 3:23. This is a grammatical/literary observation, not an independent architectural law.

## 10. Findings, interpretations, and proposals kept separate

### Direct textual observations
1. God gives a command in Genesis 2:16–17 before the serpent speaks.
2. The serpent questions and contradicts that command and presents an account of God's motive.
3. The woman's retelling differs from the original command in two identifiable ways: it omits “freely” and adds “neither shall ye touch it.”
4. The woman sees, evaluates, desires, takes, and eats.
5. The man eats; in Genesis 3:17 God identifies his heeding of his wife's voice in connection with that action.
6. Their eyes are opened and they know they are naked.
7. They hide; Adam reports fear.
8. God asks who told Adam he was naked, and separately asks whether he ate from the prohibited tree.
9. The humans acknowledge eating and identify preceding influences in their explanations.
10. God says the man has become like one of “us” in knowing good and evil.
11. God sends the man out and the way to the tree of life is guarded.

### Interpretations that remain provisional
1. The serpent's strategy may be described as introducing a competing account of God's Word, character, and motive. The narrative shows his words and their sequence, but it does not disclose every aspect of his private intention.
2. The woman's evaluation may be studied as a movement from receiving a command to assessing the tree under the serpent's competing account. The text does not explicitly formulate a theory of independent moral authority.
3. Adam's heeding of his wife's voice is identified as part of his transgression. This is more specific than describing the Fall as an undifferentiated “choice.”
4. The question “Who told thee?” foregrounds the source of Adam's changed awareness, but the narrative does not record Adam identifying an informant.
5. The knowledge acknowledged in 3:22 is real within the narrative. Its precise nature and scope remain under investigation; it does not confer God's authority or transfer authority to establish the standard.

### Architectural proposals not admitted as canon
- The Fall may involve a disruption in the human relation to God's Word as the governing reference for evaluation and action.
- Perception, evaluation, desire, heeding, and action are distinguishable stages worth examining.
- The distinction between the capacity to evaluate and authority to establish the standard of evaluation may be important to a future account of human will.
- The contrast between human self-covering (3:7) and God's clothing (3:21), and between human access and guarded access to life (3:22–24), may be relevant to wider theological reconstruction.

These are proposals/questions only. None is promoted to a settled GUA-W node by this document.

## 12. Questions carried forward

1. What does “knowing good and evil” mean across its other Scriptural occurrences, especially Deuteronomy 1:39, 2 Samuel 19:35–36, and 1 Kings 3:9?
2. Does Genesis 4:6–7 distinguish desire, sin's desire, human response, and ruling in a way that clarifies Genesis 3?
3. What exactly is meant by the woman's “desire” in Genesis 3:16, given the disputed meaning of תְּשׁוּקָה (*təšûqāh*)?
4. What does the narrative imply, and not imply, about the source of Adam's awareness in Genesis 3:7–11?
5. What is the relation between the serpent's promise in 3:5 and God's acknowledgement in 3:22? Which parts of the prediction are confirmed, and which must be read in context?
6. What is the identity of “one of us” in 3:22? The verse alone does not resolve the referent.
7. How should Genesis 3:15 be read in immediate Hebrew context and in later canonical interpretation?
8. What changed in the human condition after the Fall, and what remained unchanged about God's authorship and authority?
9. Can “autonomous will” be defined from Scripture without importing modern philosophical assumptions?
10. Are capacity to perceive, evaluation, desire, choice, heeding, action, and authority to establish a standard distinct concepts? Which passages define each?
11. Does the repeated root שָׁמַר (*šāmar*) in Genesis 2:15 and 3:24 establish only a verbal correspondence, or does wider Scripture support a more specific relationship?
12. Which observations can later be compared with scientific disciplines, and which are strictly theological/textual questions?

## 13. Provisional conclusion

Genesis 3 records a sequence in which the serpent questions and contradicts God's command, the humans act contrary to the command, their awareness changes, they hide, and God confronts them. The chapter distinguishes perception, evaluation, desire, heeding, action, knowledge, and divine judgment in its narration.

Genesis 3:22 confirms that the man has become like one of “us” in knowing good and evil. It does not state that humanity became the Author, acquired God's authority, or became the independent source of the standard of good and evil. The precise meaning and scope of the acquired knowledge remain open. The separate question of authority is resolved: no transfer of God's authority or authority to establish the standard is stated.

**Overall finding:** Genesis 3 records a competing representation of the Author's command, human evaluation and action contrary to that command, changed awareness, accountability, judgment, and altered human/environmental conditions. The conclusion that no authority to establish the standard was transferred is resolved. The full definition and operation of human will, and the precise nature and scope of the changed knowledge in 3:22, remain separate questions. These findings are recognized in relation to the completed GUA-W; they do not create a new architecture.
