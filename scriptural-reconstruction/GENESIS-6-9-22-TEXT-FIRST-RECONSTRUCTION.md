# Genesis 6:9–22 — Text-First Reconstruction

## 1. Scope and method

This reconstruction examines Genesis 6:9–22 in the KJV, following the passage's own sequence: Noah's description, the condition of the earth, God's announcement and command, the ark's specified construction, the covenant statement, the preservation of living creatures, provision of food, and Noah's obedience.

The method distinguishes:
- what the text directly states;
- what the Hebrew wording contributes;
- what later passages clarify;
- what remains unresolved.

The passage is not treated as a modern engineering specification, a complete zoological catalogue, or a scientific account independent of the larger narrative. No new GUA-W layer, operator, runtime stage, State term, or universal law is proposed.

## 2. KJV source text — passage outline

Genesis 6:9–22 records these movements:

1. **Verses 9–10 — Noah's generations and household:** Noah is described as just, perfect in his generations, and walking with God; three sons are named.
2. **Verses 11–12 — The earth's condition:** The earth is corrupt before God, filled with violence; God looks upon it and sees corruption in all flesh's way upon the earth.
3. **Verses 13–16 — Announcement and ark command:** God declares the end of all flesh, identifies violence as filling the earth, commands Noah to make an ark, and gives construction details.
4. **Verse 17 — The flood announced:** God says He will bring a flood of waters to destroy all flesh in which is the breath of life under heaven.
5. **Verse 18 — Covenant and entry:** God declares that He will establish His covenant with Noah; Noah, his sons, his wife, and his sons' wives are to enter the ark.
6. **Verses 19–20 — Living creatures:** Noah is to bring pairs of every sort of living creature into the ark, male and female, to preserve them.
7. **Verse 21 — Food:** Noah is commanded to gather food for his household and the animals.
8. **Verse 22 — Obedience:** Noah does according to all that God commanded him.

## 3. Noah's description (6:9–10)

The passage begins, “These are the generations of Noah.” This formula connects the account to the genealogical language already encountered in Genesis 5, while shifting the focus from the line of descent to Noah's character and the events surrounding him.

The KJV describes Noah as:
- “a just man”;
- “perfect in his generations”;
- one who “walked with God.”

### 3.1 “Just” and “perfect”

The Hebrew word translated “just” is צַדִּיק (*ṣaddîq*), commonly carrying the sense of righteous or just. The word translated “perfect” is תָּמִים (*tāmîm*), which can describe wholeness, integrity, or being without defect, according to context.

The phrase “perfect in his generations” has been read in more than one way. It may describe Noah's integrity among the people of his time; some have proposed that it refers to the purity of his genealogy or descent. The wording does not explicitly state a genetic theory. The ordinary moral sense of integrity has strong contextual relevance because the next verses contrast Noah with the corruption and violence filling the earth. Any narrower claim about ancestry or genetics requires evidence beyond the phrase itself.

“Perfect” should not automatically be expanded into the claim that Noah was absolutely sinless. Genesis later records Noah's failures (Genesis 9:20–24). The text's description here is compatible with integrity and righteousness without making Noah the Author, the source of grace, or a sinless being.

### 3.2 “Walked with God”

The phrase recalls Enoch in Genesis 5:22, 24, who also “walked with God.” The shared wording creates a clear textual connection between the two men. In both cases, the phrase describes a life in relation to God; it is not merely physical locomotion.

The text does not say that Noah's walk with God caused God to become indebted to him. Genesis 6:8 has already said Noah found grace in the LORD's eyes. The sequence should be preserved: grace is stated in verse 8; Noah's character is described in verses 9–10. The passage does not present grace as a wage Noah earned.

### 3.3 Noah's sons

Shem, Ham, and Japheth are named. Genesis 5:32 had already named these sons and stated Noah's age. Genesis 6:10 repeats their names as the narrative turns toward the household that will enter the ark.

## 4. The earth corrupt and filled with violence (6:11–12)

The text states that “the earth also was corrupt before God, and the earth was filled with violence.” God looks upon the earth and sees that it is corrupt, “for all flesh had corrupted his way upon the earth.”

Three observations should be distinguished:

1. **Corruption:** The Hebrew root שָׁחַת (*šāḥaṯ*) appears repeatedly in verses 11–12 and again in verse 13. It can convey ruin, corruption, or destruction. The repetition ties the condition of the earth to the judgment God announces.
2. **Violence:** The word חָמָס (*ḥāmās*) means violence or wrongdoing. The earth is described as filled with it. The text does not reduce the problem to a private failing by one individual; it describes a condition pervasive across the earth.
3. **“All flesh”:** The phrase כָּל־בָּשָׂר (*kol-bāśār*) appears in the account and is used in relation to the corruption and the coming judgment. Its scope is clarified by the passage's references to human beings and living creatures, but the moral statement “all flesh had corrupted his way” should not be expanded into a detailed claim about the moral agency of every animal species.

The repeated “corrupt” wording forms a strong verbal connection: the earth is corrupt before God; God sees that it is corrupt; all flesh has corrupted its way; and God declares that He will destroy them with the earth. The judgment responds to the condition the text has just identified.

The passage does not present God as discovering the condition through limited observation. “God looked upon the earth” is the narrative's presentation of divine assessment, not a statement that the Creator had previously lacked knowledge.

## 5. God's declaration and command to Noah (6:13–16)

### 5.1 The announced end (6:13)

God tells Noah that “the end of all flesh is come before me,” because the earth is filled with violence through them, and that He will destroy them with the earth.

The text identifies:
- the Author of the announcement: God;
- the condition named: violence filling the earth;
- the declared judgment: the end of all flesh;
- the means and instructions that follow: the ark and the flood.

The passage does not portray Noah as devising a rescue plan independently and then persuading God to adopt it. God announces the judgment and gives Noah the command and specifications.

### 5.2 “Make thee an ark” (6:14)

God commands Noah to make an ark of “gopher wood.” The Hebrew term גֹּפֶר (*gōpher*) is rare and its exact wood species is uncertain. It should not be confidently identified as a particular modern tree without additional evidence.

The ark is to be made with rooms or compartments (קִנִּים, *qinnîm*) and pitched within and without with pitch. The exact construction materials and methods cannot all be recovered from this verse alone. The text does clearly specify a vessel, compartments, and a sealing treatment on the inside and outside.

### 5.3 Dimensions (6:15)

The stated dimensions are:
- length: 300 cubits;
- breadth: 50 cubits;
- height: 30 cubits.

The unit is the cubit. Because cubit standards varied historically, modern conversions depend on which cubit length is assumed. The text's primary datum is the ratio and the stated cubit measurements; any conversion to feet or metres should name its assumed cubit rather than present a single modern measurement as certain.

The dimensions are a real specification in the narrative. They do not, by themselves, provide every structural detail needed to reconstruct the vessel as a modern naval engineering plan.

### 5.4 The opening, door, and levels (6:16)

The KJV describes a window, a cubit in measure, a door in the side, and lower, second, and third stories.

The Hebrew word translated “window” is צֹהַר (*ṣōhar*), whose precise architectural referent has been debated; proposals include an opening, light aperture, or other feature. The text gives its measure as one cubit but does not provide a modern architectural drawing.

The door is specified as being in the side. The ark is to have lower, second, and third levels. These are explicit features; additional details about ramps, internal partitions, ventilation systems, or loading arrangements should not be asserted as though Genesis 6 states them.

## 6. The flood and the covenant (6:17–18)

### 6.1 The flood announced (6:17)

God says, “I, even I, do bring a flood of waters upon the earth,” to destroy all flesh in which is the breath of life from under heaven; everything in the earth is to die.

The Hebrew phrase מַבּוּל (*mabbûl*) is the term used for the Flood. The verse identifies God's action and the scope of the announced judgment in the narrative. The phrase “breath of life” connects with the life-language already used in Genesis 2 and the creation account.

The text does not describe the physical mechanism by which the waters are gathered or distributed in this verse. That account is developed in Genesis 7–8. We should not insert a scientific mechanism into this passage before examining the later narrative.

### 6.2 “But with thee will I establish my covenant” (6:18)

The covenant statement is central:

> “But with thee will I establish my covenant; and thou shalt come into the ark, thou, and thy sons, and thy wife, and thy sons' wives with thee.” (KJV)

The Hebrew word בְּרִית (*berît*) is translated “covenant.” God is the speaker and the One who says He will establish the covenant with Noah.

The verse does not say Noah authors the covenant or sets its terms. It is God's declared commitment. The immediate practical consequence named here is that Noah and his household are to enter the ark.

The phrase “with thee” identifies Noah as the person addressed. It does not require us to infer that every member of his household has the same stated description as Noah in verse 9. The text distinguishes Noah's description from the household members named for preservation.

## 7. The living creatures and food (6:19–21)

### 7.1 Pairs of every sort (6:19–20)

God commands Noah to bring into the ark two of every sort of living thing, male and female, to keep them alive with him. The categories named include birds, cattle, and every creeping thing of the ground. They are to come to Noah to be kept alive.

The Hebrew wording uses “two” and “male and female.” The purpose stated is preservation of life. The text does not supply a modern taxonomic inventory, total species count, or detailed ecological classification. “After their kind” reflects the category language used in Genesis 1 and should be read within the narrative's own classification vocabulary.

Genesis 7:2–3 later gives additional instructions concerning clean animals and birds, including a different number. That later passage must be read alongside Genesis 6 rather than treated as though Genesis 6:19–20 were the only instruction in the complete Flood account. The detailed relationship between the general pair instruction here and the later clean-animal numbers should be examined in the sequence of Genesis 7.

### 7.2 Food provision (6:21)

Noah is commanded to take food of every sort that is eaten and gather it to himself. The text gives the reason: it shall be for food for Noah and for the animals.

This is a practical provision included within the divine command. It does not describe the full logistics, quantities, storage methods, preservation technology, or feeding schedule. Those details are not supplied in these verses.

## 8. Noah's response (6:22)

The passage closes:

> “Thus did Noah; according to all that God commanded him, so did he.” (KJV)

This is the text's explicit statement of Noah's obedience. The sequence is clear:
- God announces what He will do.
- God commands Noah what he is to do.
- God specifies the ark and preservation instructions.
- Noah does according to all that God commanded.

The verse does not say Noah originated the plan, selected the covenant, or independently determined the ark's purpose. Noah's action is a response to God's word.

This distinction should not be weakened in either direction. Noah is not the Author of the command; nevertheless, the text expressly records Noah's real obedience. God's initiative and the creature's response are distinct, not mutually exclusive.

## 9. Internal connections across Genesis 1–6

### 9.1 Creation, life, and preservation

Genesis 1–2 records God's creation and ordering of life. Genesis 6:17 speaks of the breath of life and announces judgment; Genesis 6:19–21 commands the preservation of living creatures and provision of food. The passage places judgment and preservation within the same divine address.

### 9.2 Enoch and Noah — “walked with God”

Genesis 5:22, 24 says Enoch walked with God. Genesis 6:9 says the same of Noah. The phrase links the two men across the genealogy. Enoch's taking and Noah's preservation through the ark are different narrative outcomes; the shared description does not make their circumstances identical.

### 9.3 Corruption and the ground

Genesis 3:17–19 connects human toil with the cursed ground. Genesis 5:29 records Lamech's hope concerning work and toil because of that ground. Genesis 6:11–13 describes the earth filled with violence and corruption. The language of “earth” and the continuing human condition connect the passages, but the text does not say the Flood is merely a remedy for agricultural hardship. The stated reason in Genesis 6 is corruption and violence.

### 9.4 God's word and Noah's action

Genesis 6:13–21 records God's declaration and instructions; Genesis 6:22 records Noah's compliance. This is a clear narrative distinction between command and response. It should not be collapsed into either “Noah created the plan” or “Noah did nothing.” The text records divine initiative and actual creaturely obedience.

### 9.5 Covenant and preservation

The covenant is announced before the ark entry and before the Flood narrative is carried forward. God declares the covenant; Noah is instructed to enter the ark with his household. The later passages will show the covenant's further content and sign. Genesis 6:18 should not be made to contain every detail that Genesis 9 later supplies.

## 11. Triple Constraint review

### Biblical compliance
The reconstruction follows Genesis 6:9–22 in the KJV, distinguishes the stated descriptions from disputed interpretations, and keeps later details from Genesis 7 and 9 in their proper place.

### Scientific coherence
The text's cubit measurements are preserved without pretending the cubit has one universally certain modern conversion. The reconstruction does not claim that the passage supplies all naval engineering, zoological, ecological, or hydrological details. Such questions require distinct evidence and should not be answered by inventing details absent from the text.

### GUA-W consistency
God is the Author of the announcement, command, covenant, and preservation instruction. Noah's obedience is a real creaturely response, not an originating cause. No new architecture component is introduced.

**Disposition:** The direct narrative sequence is admissible. The precise identity of some construction materials and features, the full logistics of preservation, and the relationship between the pair instruction in Genesis 6 and the clean-animal numbers in Genesis 7 remain open for careful follow-up.

## 12. Open questions

1. What is the precise contextual force of “perfect in his generations” in Genesis 6:9? What evidence supports moral integrity, genealogical purity, or another nuance?
2. How does the repeated corruption root שָׁחַת (*šāḥaṯ*) structure verses 11–13?
3. What is the scope of “all flesh” in each occurrence, and how does the context distinguish moral corruption from the creatures included in the judgment?
4. What wood is meant by גֹּפֶר (*gōpher*)? Can the species be identified with confidence?
5. What is the precise architectural referent of צֹהַר (*ṣōhar*) in verse 16?
6. What cubit standard is appropriate for any proposed modern conversion, and what uncertainty must be retained?
7. How do Genesis 6:19–20 and Genesis 7:2–3 fit together in the sequence of instructions?
8. What does the covenant statement in Genesis 6:18 establish at this point, and what further content is supplied only later?
9. What does Noah's obedience in verse 22 establish about response to divine command without confusing response with originating purpose?
10. What details about the ark's construction, animal kinds, food quantities, and flood mechanism are not supplied here and must remain open?

## 13. Working conclusion

Genesis 6:9–22 presents Noah as a just man, whole in his generations, and one who walked with God. It then describes the earth as corrupt and filled with violence. God announces judgment, commands Noah to build an ark with specified dimensions and features, declares His covenant, instructs the preservation of Noah's household and living creatures, and commands the gathering of food. The passage closes by recording that Noah did according to all that God commanded him.

The governing distinctions are clear within the narrative: God identifies the condition, declares what He will do, gives the command, establishes the covenant, and specifies the means of preservation. Noah receives the word and obeys. The response is real, but it does not become the source of the command or covenant.

The next investigation should proceed into Genesis 7, where the entry into the ark, the clean and unclean animal instructions, the onset and duration of the Flood, and the fulfillment of God's word are narrated. The instructions in Genesis 6 should be compared with their execution in Genesis 7 without assuming the two chapters repeat every detail in identical form.
