# Investigation II.6 — The Storm Is Calmed
**Primary texts:** Matthew 8:23–27; Mark 4:35–41; Luke 8:22–25 (KJV)  
**Related texts:** Psalm 65:7; Psalm 89:9; Psalm 107:23–30; Job 38:8–11; John 1:1–3; Hebrews 1:1–3  
**Status:** Comparative textual study recorded; meteorological mechanism not described.

## Question
What do the three Gospel accounts report when Jesus rebukes the wind and the sea, and what does the disciples' response reveal about their recognition of Him?

## Sequence
1. Jesus and His disciples enter a ship and cross the sea (Matthew, Mark, Luke).
2. A great storm arises; the ship is covered or in danger from the waves.
3. Jesus is asleep in the vessel (Matthew, Mark, Luke).
4. The disciples awaken Him, expressing fear for their lives.
5. Jesus rebukes the winds/sea and addresses the disciples' fear and lack of faith.
6. A great calm follows.
7. The disciples marvel and ask what manner of man this is, that even winds and sea obey Him.

## Differences to preserve
Mark records Jesus' words, “Peace, be still,” and the disciples' question, “Who then is this, that even the wind and the sea obey him?” Matthew records the disciples' question about what manner of man He is. Luke records that He rebukes the raging water and that they marvel. Do not harmonize away each writer's wording.

## What the text states
The storm is real within the narrative; the disciples fear; Jesus speaks with authority; the wind and sea become calm. The accounts explicitly connect the event with the disciples' faith and their question about Jesus' identity.

## Witness and agency
The disciples experience the danger and call to Jesus. Jesus rebukes the storm. The calm is not attributed to the disciples' skill or command. Their response shows that witnessing an extraordinary event does not mean they fully understand the One who performs it.

## Canonical context
The Old Testament repeatedly portrays God as sovereign over the sea and waters (for example, Psalm 89:9; 107:23–30; Job 38:8–11). These passages provide a meaningful canonical background. The Gospel accounts' presentation of Jesus exercising authority over the sea should be read alongside John's identification of the Word through whom all things were made (John 1:1–3) and Hebrews' description of the Son upholding all things (Hebrews 1:1–3). This is a canonical theological relationship, not a claim that the Gospel writers explicitly quote each of these passages here.

## Limits
No account provides meteorological measurements or a physical mechanism for the sudden calm. The narrative does not establish a repeatable weather-control technique or a general physical law.

## Triple Constraint
- **Biblical compliance — PASS:** the shared sequence and differences among the accounts are preserved.
- **Scientific coherence — PASS for restraint; mechanism UNRESOLVED:** no meteorological data or causal mechanism is supplied.
- **GUA-W consistency — PASS for the governing relationship; technical mapping UNRESOLVED:** Christ's authority and the disciples' derivative witness are preserved; no new architectural stage is added.

## Finding
The sign reveals authority over the sea and confronts the disciples with the identity question: “What manner of man is this?” The narrative does not invite the reader to stop at the weather event; it directs attention to the One whom wind and sea obey.

**The storm is witnessed. The Word speaks. The sea is calm. The disciples ask who He is.**
