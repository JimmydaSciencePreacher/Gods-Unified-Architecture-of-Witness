# Genesis 4:9–10 — Text-First Reconstruction, Unit 11

**Status:** INITIAL TEXTUAL FINDINGS; interpretive conclusions remain bounded; no architectural law admitted  
**Date:** 2026-11-16  
**Scope:** KJV Genesis 4:9–10, read with Genesis 2:15; 3:8–13; 4:1–8  
**Questions:** What does Cain say he knows? What does “keeper” mean in Hebrew? How does this relate to Genesis 2:15? What does the voice of Abel's blood communicate in the narrative?

## 1. KJV base text

Genesis 4:9:
> And the LORD said unto Cain, Where is Abel thy brother? And he said, I know not: Am I my brother's keeper?

Genesis 4:10:
> And he said, What hast thou done? the voice of thy brother's blood crieth unto me from the ground.

## 3. Genesis 4:9 — Word-level observations

- וַיֹּאמֶר (*wayyōʾmer*) — “and [the LORD] said”; Qal wayyiqtol, third-person masculine singular, from אמר (*ʾāmar*), say.
- יְהוָה (*YHWH*) — the LORD; subject.
- אֶל־קַיִן (*ʾel-Qayin*) — to Cain.
- אֵי (*ʾê*) — where? An interrogative asking location or whereabouts.
- הֶבֶל (*Heḇel*) — Abel.
- אָחִיךָ (*ʾāḥîḵā*) — your brother.
- וַיֹּאמֶר (*wayyōʾmer*) — and he said; Cain is the subject in the response.
- לֹא (*lōʾ*) — not.
- יָדַעְתִּי (*yādaʿtî*) — Qal perfect, first-person common singular, from ידע (*yādaʿ*): I knew / I know. The first-person suffix identifies Cain as the speaker: “I do not know.”
- הֲ (*ha-*) — interrogative particle, introducing a question.
- שֹׁמֵר (*šōmēr*) — Qal active participle, masculine singular, from שמר (*šāmar*): keeping, guarding, watching, tending.
- אָחִי (*ʾāḥî*) — my brother.
- אָנֹכִי (*ʾānōḵî*) — I; independent first-person pronoun, placed at the end for emphasis.

The literal shape of Cain's final question is:
> “The keeper/guardian of my brother, am I?”

The KJV's “Am I my brother's keeper?” is a faithful idiomatic rendering.

### 3.1 “I know not” — ידע (*yādaʿ*)

The verb יָדַעְתִּי (*yādaʿtî*) comes from the same root H3045, *yādaʿ*, already examined in:
- Genesis 3:5 — the serpent's statement about knowing good and evil.
- Genesis 3:7 — the humans knew they were naked.
- Genesis 3:22 — the LORD acknowledges that man has become like one of us, to know good and evil.
- Genesis 4:9 — Cain says, “I know not.”

The root is the same, but the grammatical forms and objects/contexts differ. Genesis 4:9 uses a first-person singular Qal perfect with an explicit negation: “I did not know / I know not.”

The immediate context matters. Cain has just killed Abel (4:8). He is the person who acted against Abel, and God asks where Abel is. Cain's denial is therefore not a neutral example of someone lacking information. Within the narrative sequence, Cain knows what happened to Abel because he himself killed him.

**Direct observation:** Cain's statement “I know not” conflicts with the information supplied by the preceding narrative. The text presents a false denial, not an innocent absence of knowledge.

**Boundary:** The verb ידע itself does not mean “lie,” “evade responsibility,” or “refuse moral knowledge.” Those are contextual interpretations of Cain's utterance, not the lexical meaning of the root.

### 3.2 “Am I my brother's keeper?” — שמר (*šāmar*)

The word שֹׁמֵר (*šōmēr*) is an active participle from שָׁמַר (*šāmar*), a verb with a broad range including keep, guard, watch, preserve, and observe.

The question is not simply “Am I my brother's brother?” Cain already knows their relationship. It asks whether he is the one responsible for keeping or guarding his brother.

The emphatic pronoun אָנֹכִי (*ʾānōḵî*, “I”) occurs at the end:
> “The keeper of my brother, am I?”

The rhetorical form can function as a repudiation of responsibility. The NET notes interpret it as a defiant response denying responsibility for his brother. That is a contextual reading, not the lexical definition of שֹׁמֵר.

## 4. Direct connection: Genesis 2:15 and Genesis 4:9

Genesis 2:15 KJV:
> “…to dress it and to keep it.”

Genesis 2:15 uses the same Hebrew root שמר (*šāmar*) translated “keep.” Adam is placed in the garden to work/serve it and keep/guard it.

Genesis 4:9 KJV:
> “Am I my brother's keeper?”

Cain uses the same root, now as the participle שֹׁמֵר (*šōmēr*), “keeper/guardian.”

| Passage | Hebrew form | Referent / object | Narrative setting |
|---|---|---|---|
| Genesis 2:15 | שָׁמַר (*šāmar*) | Adam keeping the garden | Assigned garden responsibility |
| Genesis 4:9 | שֹׁמֵר (*šōmēr*) | Cain asked about keeping his brother | Cain's denial after killing Abel |

This is a genuine lexical connection.

It raises a significant question: Is the text developing a broader responsibility motif in which “keeping” is not merely possession or control, but a duty of care/guardianship toward what has been entrusted?

The word alone does not establish that full theological definition. Its contexts must be compared.

**Important restraint:** Genesis 2:15 assigns Adam the task of keeping the garden. Genesis 4:9 does not explicitly say Cain was previously assigned a formal office as Abel's guardian. The echo is lexical and thematic, not an explicit transfer of Adam's garden commission to Cain.

## 5. The repeated question pattern: Genesis 3:9 and Genesis 4:9

Genesis 3:9:
> “And the LORD God called unto Adam, and said unto him, Where art thou?”

Genesis 4:9:
> “And the LORD said unto Cain, Where is Abel thy brother?”

Both narratives use a divine question beginning with “where” after a human act contrary to God's instruction:
- Genesis 3:9 — God asks Adam where he is.
- Genesis 4:9 — God asks Cain where Abel is.

In Genesis 3, Adam responds by describing fear and hiding (3:10), then refers to the woman (3:12).
In Genesis 4, Cain says he does not know and asks whether he is his brother's keeper.

The parallel is not exact:
- The question in Genesis 3 concerns Adam's whereabouts.
- The question in Genesis 4 concerns Abel's whereabouts.
- Adam acknowledges fear and hiding.
- Cain denies knowledge and challenges responsibility.

**Observation:** The repeated divine “where” question brings the human response into the open. It does not imply that God lacks knowledge of the events.

## 6. Genesis 4:10 — The voice of Abel's blood

### Word-level observations

- וַיֹּאמֶר (*wayyōʾmer*) — and He said.
- מֶה (*meh*) — what?
- עָשִׂיתָ (*ʿāśîtā*) — Qal perfect, second-person masculine singular, from עשה (*ʿāśāh*): you did / you have done.
- קוֹל (*qōl*) — voice, sound, report.
- דְּמֵי (*dəmê*) — construct plural form of דָּם (*dām*), blood; literally “bloods of.”
- אָחִיךָ (*ʾāḥîḵā*) — your brother.
- צֹעֲקִים (*ṣōʿăqîm*) — Qal active participle, masculine plural, from צעק (*ṣāʿaq*): crying out, calling for help.
- אֵלַי (*ʾēlay*) — to me.
- מִן־הָאֲדָמָה (*min-hāʾădāmāh*) — from the ground/soil.

The Hebrew phrase is literally “the voice of the bloods of your brother [is] crying to me from the ground.” The noun דְּמֵי (*dəmê*) is plural in form, and the participle צֹעֲקִים (*ṣōʿăqîm*) is also masculine plural. The KJV renders the sense idiomatically as “the voice of thy brother's blood crieth.”

### 6.1 Blood and voice

The text personifies Abel's shed blood as crying out to God. The word “voice” is explicit in Hebrew: קוֹל (*qōl*).

The NET note interprets the blood as evidence that testifies against Cain, like an eyewitness in a court. This is a helpful interpretive analogy, but it is not the lexical meaning of “blood” or “voice.”

The text establishes:
- Abel has been killed.
- His blood is associated with the ground.
- The blood's “voice” cries to God.
- God hears and confronts Cain.

The ground is not an irrelevant setting. Cain's action against Abel has a consequence that is represented as a cry rising from the ground to God.

### 6.2 The plural “bloods”

The Hebrew דְּמֵי (*dəmê*) is morphologically plural, though English versions commonly render it as singular “blood.”

Interpretations include:
- a plural of intensity or poetic force;
- Abel's blood and the life represented by it;
- in later Jewish interpretation, Abel's potential descendants as well as Abel himself.

The later interpretive tradition should not be confused with the minimum meaning established by the Hebrew form. The plural is observable; the precise reason for it is interpretive.

## 7. What the passage establishes

Genesis 4:9–10 records a progression:
1. God asks Cain where Abel is.
2. Cain says he does not know.
3. Cain challenges whether he is his brother's keeper.
4. God asks what Cain has done.
5. Abel's blood is represented as crying to God from the ground.

The narrative moves from a question about a person, to Cain's denial, to the question of responsibility, to the evidence of the act.

This sequence makes the relationship between knowledge and responsibility especially important.

Cain's “I know not” is not a lack of information in the ordinary sense. The preceding narrative has already identified Cain as Abel's killer. His words conflict with the narrative's established facts.

Cain's “Am I my brother's keeper?” is not an answer to where Abel is. It challenges the responsibility implied by God's question.

## 8. Relevance to U-006 — Human will, knowledge, and responsibility

This passage adds several distinctions:
- **Knowledge:** Cain's utterance denies knowledge, while the narrative establishes his direct knowledge of the event through his own action.
- **Speech:** Cain's words are an act of response to God's question.
- **Responsibility:** Cain's keeper question challenges the premise that he bears responsibility toward his brother.
- **Action:** Cain's prior action has an observable consequence: Abel's blood cries from the ground.

This supports the need to distinguish actual knowledge from a person's claim about what they know, and responsibility from a person's willingness to acknowledge it.

It does not yet define will. Nor does it establish that the capacity to know automatically produces truthful acknowledgment or responsible action.

**U-006 remains UNRESOLVED.**

## 9. Relevance to U-007 — Knowledge versus authority

Genesis 4:9–10 does not use “know good and evil.” It does, however, show that a person may speak falsely about what he knows.

Cain's denial does not change what happened. His statement does not alter the reality of Abel's death, nor does it change God's knowledge or the evidence represented by Abel's blood.

This is relevant to the distinction between:
- reality;
- a person's knowledge of reality;
- a person's statement about reality;
- a person's authority (or lack of authority) to redefine reality.

The passage does not formulate that philosophical distinction explicitly. It narrates an instance in which Cain's words do not change the truth of what occurred.

**U-007 remains UNRESOLVED.**

3. How should the first-person denial “I know not” be distinguished from actual lack of knowledge?
4. What is the precise significance of the plural דְּמֵי (*dəmê*) in Genesis 4:10?
