# Pharaoh — Hardening, Resistance, Purpose, and the Author's Sovereignty

**Investigation date:** 2026-11-20  
**Status:** PARTIAL — the narrative's sequence, stated purposes, and alternating attribution of hardening are explicit; the exact metaphysical relation between divine action and Pharaoh's willing remains disputed.  
**Governing order:** Author → Word → Creation → Witness  
**Method:** Four-question method; Triple Constraint Law  
**Primary text:** KJV, Exodus 1–15; Romans 9

## 1. Governing question

Does the Author's hardening of Pharaoh eliminate Pharaoh's responsibility, or does Scripture reveal a relationship between Pharaoh's resistance and God's sovereign purpose?

Do not remove either side of the text:
- Pharaoh refuses, resists, makes concessions, reverses them, and is described as hardening his own heart.
- God announces beforehand that He will harden Pharaoh's heart, later acts as the grammatical subject of hardening, and declares the purpose of displaying His power and making His name known.

The study must not reduce God's action to mere observation. It must also not turn Pharaoh into an innocent agent who never resists or acts.

## 2. The four-question method

### Question 1 — What must AI ask to understand GUA-W?

1. What purpose and promise precede Moses' confrontation with Pharaoh?
2. What does God announce in Exodus 3:19–20, 4:21, and 7:3 before the plague sequence?
3. What does Pharaoh himself say and do in Exodus 5–10?
4. Which passages attribute the hardening to Pharaoh, which to God, and which describe the heart's condition without an explicit personal agent?
5. What are the Hebrew verbs, their forms, and their ranges of meaning?
6. When does the narrative first explicitly report the LORD as the agent of hardening, and how does that relate to the prior divine announcements?
7. What purpose does God state in Exodus 7:3–5, 9:13–17, and 14:4, 17–18?
8. What does Pharaoh confess, concede, and then reverse?
9. How does Romans 9:14–18 use Pharaoh, and what larger argument is Paul making?
10. What conclusions are explicit, what interpretations remain debated, and what must remain unresolved?

### Question 2 — What is GUA-W revealing that helps AI understand?

The governing order remains Author → Word → Creation → Witness. God originates the purpose of Israel's deliverance and declares His authority. Pharaoh does not originate the purpose, the covenant promise, or the signs. Yet Pharaoh is represented as an acting ruler whose refusals, oppression, words, and decisions matter in the narrative.

The preceding investigations distinguish prior provision, warning, capability, action, and responsibility. Pharaoh adds the difficult case in which Scripture attributes an action both to the human agent and to God, in different statements and at different points in the narrative.

This is an investigation of existing GUA-W distinctions, not a new component.

### Question 3 — What am I learning about what I am witnessing?

The text gives a sequence, not a single isolated sentence. God announces what He will do before Moses' confrontation; Pharaoh refuses; signs and judgments occur; Pharaoh sometimes acknowledges wrongdoing or asks for relief; he repeatedly reverses concessions; and God is explicitly named as the one hardening Pharaoh's heart in later scenes.

### Question 4 — What am I learning about the Witness through what I am witnessing?

A derivative ruler can exercise real authority and make consequential decisions without becoming the Author. God's sovereign purpose is not defeated by Pharaoh's resistance. Pharaoh's resistance is not thereby transformed into obedience or morally approved action.

The narrative's explicit attribution of hardening to God must be retained. So must the narrative's explicit portrayal of Pharaoh as an oppressor and refuser. The exact manner in which divine hardening and Pharaoh's willing are related requires careful interpretation.

## 3. The purpose precedes the confrontation — Exodus 1–4

### 3.1 The situation in Egypt — Exodus 1

Israel's multiplication is followed by a new Pharaoh who does not regard Joseph's history. Pharaoh fears Israel, imposes taskmasters, increases their burdens, and orders the killing of Hebrew male children. The oppression is not introduced as a morally neutral political disagreement.

### 3.2 God hears and remembers — Exodus 2:23–25

The children of Israel groan because of bondage. Their cry comes up to God. The text says God hears, remembers His covenant with Abraham, Isaac, and Jacob, looks upon the children of Israel, and has respect unto them.

### 3.3 The Author states the purpose — Exodus 3:7–10

God says He has seen the affliction, heard the cry, knows their sorrows, and has come down to deliver them and bring them to a good land. He then sends Moses to Pharaoh.

This is the originating purpose of the confrontation: deliverance in fulfilment of God's covenant concern. Pharaoh is not the author of this purpose.

### 3.4 Foreknowledge and announced refusal — Exodus 3:18–20

God tells Moses that the elders of Israel will hear him, but that the king of Egypt will not let the people go except by a mighty hand. God then declares that He will stretch out His hand and smite Egypt with wonders.

**Explicit:** God's knowledge of Pharaoh's refusal and God's announced action precede Moses' confrontation.

**Limit:** This statement alone does not explain every detail of the relation between foreknowledge, decree, and Pharaoh's will.

### 3.5 The first hardening announcement — Exodus 4:21

God tells Moses that when he returns to Egypt and performs the wonders, God will harden/strengthen Pharaoh's heart so that he will not let the people go.

This is not a later theological inference: the announcement is in the text before the confrontation.

### 3.6 The second announcement — Exodus 7:1–5

God says He will harden Pharaoh's heart and multiply signs and wonders in Egypt. Verse 5 states the purpose: the Egyptians will know that He is the LORD when He stretches out His hand upon Egypt and brings Israel out.

**Finding:** The announced hardening is placed within God's stated deliverance and revelation purpose. It is not an independent purpose detached from the Exodus.

## 4. Pharaoh's own refusal — Exodus 5

Moses and Aaron deliver God's command: "Let my people go." Pharaoh answers in Exodus 5:2:

> Who is the LORD, that I should obey his voice to let Israel go? I know not the LORD, neither will I let Israel go.

Pharaoh then increases the burden on the Hebrew labourers (5:6–18). Israel's officers complain, and Moses brings the matter to God (5:22–23).

**Explicit:** Pharaoh refuses the command and imposes harsher labour. His conduct is presented as his own speech and action.

**Important:** The narrative's first confrontation already includes the announced purpose of God and Pharaoh's actual refusal. It is not legitimate to read Pharaoh as a passive object who has no actions or stated intention.

## 5. The three Hebrew verbs

The English word "harden" translates several Hebrew roots in Exodus 4–14. They overlap in the narrative, but should not be treated as though their lexical forms were identical.

| Hebrew root | Basic lexical range | Examples in Exodus |
|---|---|---|
| חזק — ḥāzaq (chazaq) | Be strong; make strong; strengthen | 4:21; 7:13, 22; 9:12; 10:20, 27; 11:10; 14:4, 8, 17 |
| כבד — kāvēd / kābad (kabed) | Be heavy; make heavy; weighty | 7:14; 8:15, 32; 9:7, 34; 10:1 |
| קשה — qāšâ (qashah) | Be hard; make difficult; be severe | 7:3; 13:15 |

The Hebrew word לֵב / לֵבָב (lev / levav, heart) can denote the inner person, including thought, understanding, intention, decision, courage, and will. It is not limited to modern English notions of emotion.

### 5.1 A necessary translation qualification — Exodus 7:13

The KJV reads, "And he hardened Pharaoh's heart." Some Hebrew analyses understand the form here intransitively: Pharaoh's heart "was strong" or "became strong." The subject and grammatical construction matter. The following passages more explicitly attribute actions to Pharaoh himself.

Do not build the whole argument on the English pronoun "he" in this one verse. The wider narrative provides clearer examples of both Pharaoh and God as agents.

### 5.2 Do not overstate lexical conclusions

Some scholars argue that ḥāzaq can mean God strengthened Pharaoh's resolve; others understand the divine forms as directly causing the hardening. The lexical range alone does not settle the theological question. Context, grammatical stem, subject, sequence, and canonical comparison all matter.

### 6.1 What the sequence establishes

1. God's announcement precedes the confrontation (4:21; 7:3).
2. Pharaoh's refusal is explicit from 5:2.
3. Pharaoh's own hardening is explicitly stated several times, especially 8:15, 8:32, and 9:34.
4. The LORD is explicitly named as agent in 9:12 and repeatedly thereafter.
5. Pharaoh's temporary confession and requests for relief do not produce lasting obedience.
6. The hardening theme continues into the pursuit at the Red Sea.

### 6.2 A careful point about "first"

Exodus 4:21 and 7:3 are prior divine announcements. Exodus 9:12 is commonly identified as the first explicit narrative statement during the plague sequence that says, "the LORD hardened the heart of Pharaoh." That does not mean God's prior announcements are irrelevant or that every scholar agrees divine action only begins at 9:12. Some readings understand the announcements as already describing God's causal involvement from the outset.

The distinction is between **prior announcement** and **later narrated occurrence**, not between a God who is uninvolved and one who suddenly becomes involved.

## 7. The purpose of the hardening — Exodus 9:13–17 and 10:1–2

Exodus 9:16 gives a stated purpose:

> And in very deed for this cause have I raised thee up, for to shew in thee my power; and that my name may be declared throughout all the earth.

The context is the plague of hail and the confrontation with Pharaoh. Exodus 10:1–2 says the signs are to be placed before Pharaoh and his servants so that Israel may tell subsequent generations what God did in Egypt and know that He is the LORD.

The stated purpose includes:
- displaying God's power;
- making God's name known beyond Egypt;
- giving Israel a remembered testimony of deliverance;
- demonstrating that Pharaoh's royal power does not supersede the LORD.

**Limit:** These statements identify God's purpose in this historical confrontation. They do not license a general claim that every instance of human stubbornness has the same revealed purpose.

## 8. The Exodus and the Red Sea — Exodus 12–14

Pharaoh finally sends Israel away after the death of the firstborn (12:29–33). The Egyptians urge Israel to depart quickly. Yet the narrative does not end with the release.

In Exodus 14:4 God says He will harden Pharaoh's heart so that Pharaoh will pursue Israel, and that God will be honoured upon Pharaoh and his host. Verse 8 says the LORD hardened Pharaoh's heart and Pharaoh pursued the children of Israel.

The purpose is again explicit: "the Egyptians shall know that I am the LORD" (14:4, 18).

The pursuing Egyptian force is then judged at the sea. Israel witnesses the deliverance and fears the LORD (14:30–31).

**Finding:** Pharaoh's resistance does not prevent the Author's deliverance. The Author's purpose includes the public revelation of His authority through judgment and rescue.

## 9. Romans 9:14–18 — Paul's use of Pharaoh

Paul quotes Exodus 9:16 in Romans 9:17:

> For the scripture saith unto Pharaoh, Even for this same purpose have I raised thee up, that I might shew my power in thee, and that my name might be declared throughout all the earth.

Paul then concludes in verse 18:

> Therefore hath he mercy on whom he will have mercy, and whom he will he hardeneth.

### 9.1 What Paul explicitly affirms

- God's mercy is not controlled by human entitlement.
- God has authority to show mercy.
- God has authority to harden.
- Pharaoh is an example within Paul's argument about God's purpose and sovereign action.

### 9.2 What Romans 9 must not be made to say without further argument

Romans 9:17–18 should not be isolated from 9:6–24 or the continuation of Paul's argument in Romans 10–11. Paul is addressing God's word, Israel, election, mercy, hardening, and the inclusion of Gentiles. The chapter is not merely a detached philosophical essay about Pharaoh's psychology.

Nor should Exodus be reduced to Romans 9 alone. Exodus supplies the narrative sequence and stated historical purpose to which Paul appeals.

### 9.3 Does Romans 9 erase Pharaoh's responsibility?

No such erasure is stated in Romans 9. The broader canonical account continues to describe Pharaoh's oppressive actions, refusals, and words. Paul's affirmation of God's sovereign hardening must be held alongside that narrative, not used to delete it.

**Limit:** Christian traditions differ over how Romans 9 relates divine hardening to individual election, judicial hardening, and human freedom. This study records Paul's explicit affirmation of sovereignty without claiming to settle every later doctrinal dispute.

## 10. Interpretive positions and limits

At least two serious interpretive approaches appear in published scholarship:

### Approach A — Direct divine causation

G. K. Beale's exegetical study argues that the divine hardening announcements in Exodus 4:21 and 7:3 describe God's active causal involvement, not merely permission. It reads the later events as the unfolding of that announced purpose. Beale also emphasizes the wider Exodus purpose of God's self-revelation and Israel's deliverance.

### Approach B — Strengthening Pharaoh's own resolve

Hans-Georg Wünch's study argues that ḥāzaq can be understood as strengthening Pharaoh's resolve, and that the divine action reinforces a decision Pharaoh himself has already made. This reading emphasizes the order in which Pharaoh's own resistance appears and argues that the narrative does not portray God as forcing Pharaoh to act against his will.

### What both approaches must account for

- God's prior announcement in 4:21 and 7:3.
- Pharaoh's explicit refusals and self-hardening.
- The first explicit narrated divine hardening in 9:12.
- The continued divine hardening after 9:12.
- The stated purpose in 9:16 and 10:1–2.
- Paul's use of Pharaoh in Romans 9:17–18.

**Unresolved:** The precise causal mechanics of God's action in Pharaoh's heart are not agreed upon. The Hebrew vocabulary and narrative sequence are central evidence, but interpreters differ about how they combine.

## 11. The central distinctions

| Distinction | Scriptural finding |
|---|---|
| Prior purpose | God intends Israel's deliverance before Pharaoh's confrontation (Exodus 2–4). |
| Prior knowledge | God says He knows Pharaoh will not release Israel at the first request (3:19). |
| Prior announcement | God announces hardening before the plague sequence (4:21; 7:3). |
| Human refusal | Pharaoh refuses God's command (5:2). |
| Human action | Pharaoh repeatedly hardens his heart or reverses concessions (8:15, 32; 9:34). |
| Divine action | The LORD is explicitly named as hardening Pharaoh (9:12; 10:20, 27; 11:10; 14:8). |
| Stated purpose | God's power and name are to be made known (7:5; 9:16; 10:1–2; 14:4, 18). |
| Judgment | Pharaoh and Egypt are judged; Israel is delivered (12–14). |

These are related but not interchangeable statements. Foreknowledge, announcement, hardening, refusal, purpose, and judgment must not be collapsed into one word.

## 12. Direct answer to the governing question

**Does the Author's hardening of Pharaoh eliminate Pharaoh's responsibility?**

The narrative does not eliminate Pharaoh's responsibility. Pharaoh oppresses, refuses, speaks, negotiates, confesses, reverses, and pursues. Scripture repeatedly attributes actions to Pharaoh himself.

**Does Pharaoh's responsibility eliminate the Author's sovereignty?**

No. God announces His action beforehand, is explicitly named as the agent of hardening, states His purpose, and brings Israel out of Egypt. Romans 9 expressly uses Pharaoh to affirm God's sovereign mercy and hardening.

**What can be concluded without exceeding the text?**

Scripture places God's sovereign action and Pharaoh's own resistance within the same narrative. The Author's purpose is accomplished; Pharaoh's resistance remains Pharaoh's resistance and is judged. The exact metaphysical relation between God's action and Pharaoh's willing is disputed and should not be falsely presented as settled by a single lexical observation.

## 13. GUA-W relationship

The governing principle remains:

**God alone is Original Cause. Everything else is derivative witness. The derivative cannot alter the Source.**

Pharaoh does not originate God's covenant purpose, the command to release Israel, or the signs. Pharaoh's royal authority does not make him the Author. His resistance does not cancel the Author's purpose.

At the same time, the text does not treat Pharaoh's words and actions as unreal. He is a responsible ruler whose conduct is judged.

The passage adds no new GUA-W component. It tests the already-established distinction between the Author's originating purpose and the derivative agent's real but non-original actions.

**Closing finding:** God announces His purpose and hardening before Moses confronts Pharaoh. Pharaoh refuses and repeatedly resists. God is later explicitly named as hardening Pharaoh's heart, and Scripture identifies the purpose: that God's power and name be known and Israel be delivered. Pharaoh's resistance does not defeat the Author's purpose; God's sovereignty does not erase the narrative's attribution of real actions and accountability to Pharaoh. The exact manner in which divine hardening and Pharaoh's willing relate remains a question for further canonical examination.
