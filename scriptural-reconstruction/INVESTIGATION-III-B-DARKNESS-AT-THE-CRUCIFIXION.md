# Investigation III.B — Darkness at the Crucifixion

**Primary texts:** Matthew 27:45; Mark 15:33; Luke 23:44–45 (KJV)  
**Related texts:** Amos 8:9–10; Joel 2:10, 31; Exodus 10:21–23; Isaiah 13:10; Ezekiel 32:7–8; Acts 2:20  
**Status:** Synoptic comparison and scientific boundary examination written; exact physical cause and geographic extent unresolved.

## 1. Investigative question

**What do the Gospel accounts actually report about the darkness during the Crucifixion, how do their descriptions compare, and what can astronomy and historical inquiry establish about possible explanations?**

The darkness is a distinct Passion sign. It must not be merged with the torn veil or earthquake, even though Matthew and Mark narrate these events in close sequence and Luke places the veil report beside the darkness.

## 2. The three Synoptic accounts

### Matthew 27:45 (KJV)

“Now from the sixth hour there was darkness over all the land unto the ninth hour.”

Matthew specifies the period from the sixth hour to the ninth hour. In the conventional daylight-hour reckoning, this is approximately noon until 3 p.m.

### Mark 15:33 (KJV)

“And when the sixth hour was come, there was darkness over the whole land until the ninth hour.”

Mark gives the same hour markers and a closely corresponding description of the darkness.

### Luke 23:44–45 (KJV)

“And it was about the sixth hour, and there was a darkness over all the earth until the ninth hour. And the sun was darkened, and the veil of the temple was rent in the midst.”

Luke gives the same approximate time interval, describes the darkness as over “all the earth” in the KJV, and adds that the sun was darkened. Luke places the veil-rending notice in the same sentence sequence.

### John

John 19 records the Crucifixion and death but does not mention this darkness. That omission is a fact about John's narrative; it does not, by itself, establish either that John rejected the report or that he independently confirmed it.

## 3. Shared testimony and textual limits

The three Synoptic Gospels report darkness from the sixth to the ninth hour. Their close verbal agreement should not be counted as three wholly independent eyewitness attestations without examining the literary relationship among the Synoptic accounts. Mark is commonly regarded in modern scholarship as a source used by Matthew and Luke, though the precise Synoptic relationships remain a scholarly question.

The KJV wording differs slightly:
- Matthew: “all the land”
- Mark: “the whole land”
- Luke: “all the earth”

The underlying Greek noun *gē* can refer to earth, land, country, or region depending on context. Therefore, the English phrase “all the earth” should not be used by itself to claim that every geographic location on the planet became dark. Nor should the wording be narrowed dogmatically to Jerusalem alone without contextual argument.

**TEXT STATES:** Darkness occurs during the sixth-to-ninth-hour interval. The Synoptic accounts do not supply a meteorological or astronomical cause.

## 4. Was it a solar eclipse?

A solar eclipse is sometimes proposed as an explanation. The astronomical constraint is important.

A solar eclipse requires the Moon to be between Earth and the Sun, which occurs at new moon. Passover falls at the full-moon portion of the lunar month. If the Crucifixion occurred at Passover as the Gospel chronology indicates, a solar eclipse cannot explain the reported midday darkness. Moreover, an ordinary solar eclipse does not produce three hours of darkness at one location.

This is a genuine scientific boundary, not a theological objection. It rules out an ordinary solar eclipse as a satisfactory explanation under the Passover chronology; it does not rule out every possible source of unusual darkness.

Ancient discussions sometimes associate the darkness with an eclipse, including later reports about Thallus and Phlegon. However, the surviving references are indirect and textually complicated. They do not provide a securely preserved contemporary astronomical record that independently establishes the Gospel event. Julius Africanus' discussion of Thallus is preserved through later transmission, and the relation between the fragments and the Crucifixion is disputed.

**Finding:** Do not write “the Crucifixion darkness was a solar eclipse.” The astronomical conditions make an ordinary solar eclipse incompatible with the Passover/full-moon setting and the stated duration.

## 5. Other proposed physical explanations

Various natural mechanisms have been suggested, including dense cloud cover, dust or sand-laden atmospheric conditions, and other forms of reduced sunlight.

These are possible categories of physical explanation, not established explanations for this event. No surviving contemporaneous meteorological observation identifies a dust storm, cloud system, volcanic haze, or other specific atmospheric cause at Jerusalem during the Crucifixion.

The Gospel accounts do not say that the darkness was caused by dust, clouds, an eclipse, or a particular atmospheric process. They report the darkness and its timing.

**Scientific boundary:** A natural mechanism may be physically conceivable without being historically evidenced in this particular case. Conversely, the absence of a known mechanism in surviving records does not by itself demonstrate that the reported event did not occur.

## 6. Scriptural context: prophetic and canonical relationships

The darkness has significant Scriptural parallels. These must be examined as canonical relationships, not treated as proof that the Gospel writers used a particular passage unless the textual evidence supports that conclusion.

### Amos 8:9–10

Amos records the LORD saying:

“And it shall come to pass in that day, saith the Lord GOD, that I will cause the sun to go down at noon, and I will darken the earth in the clear day:”

The passage continues with mourning “as the mourning of an only son.” The correspondence with darkness at noon and mourning is striking. Whether a Gospel writer consciously invokes Amos, and how directly, must be evaluated from literary context rather than assumed.

### Joel 2:10, 31

Joel describes the sun and moon darkened in the context of the day of the LORD. Acts 2:20 later quotes Joel's language concerning the sun being turned into darkness. This establishes a canonical vocabulary of cosmic signs; it does not independently identify the physical cause of the Crucifixion darkness.

### Exodus 10:21–23

The plague of darkness in Egypt supplies another canonical comparison: darkness is associated with divine judgment and the Exodus narrative. The three-hour darkness in the Synoptic Passion accounts is not the same duration as the three days in Exodus. The comparison is thematic and textual, not an identity of events.

### Isaiah 13:10 and Ezekiel 32:7–8

Both prophetic passages use darkened heavenly lights in judgment imagery. They establish that cosmic-darkness language is part of the prophetic vocabulary of Scripture.

**Canonical observation:** The Crucifixion darkness belongs to a Scriptural world in which the darkening of heavenly light can signify judgment, mourning, and the day of the LORD. The precise intertextual dependence of each Gospel account remains a matter for careful textual study.

## 7. Historical and scholarly assessment

Scholars have proposed several interpretive categories:
- an actual physical phenomenon;
- a miraculous or divinely caused darkness;
- apocalyptic or prophetic symbolism;
- a historical report expressed with theological and Scriptural significance;
- combinations in which a reported event is also interpreted through prophetic imagery.

These categories are not all mutually exclusive. A historical event can be narrated with theological meaning. But the investigator must not decide the cause solely by choosing a theological or naturalistic preference.

Richard Carrier's discussion of Thallus argues that the fragment commonly cited as independent confirmation is not secure evidence for the Crucifixion darkness. This is a critical scholarly position, not a consensus verdict that the Synoptic report is invented. Other scholarship examines the darkness primarily within Mark's Passion narrative and its Scriptural resonances, including Amos 8:9–10 and Exodus 10.

The proper conclusion is modest: the Synoptic accounts attest the darkness; the surviving external evidence does not provide an uncontested independent contemporary verification or a secure physical explanation.

## 8. Timing and narrative placement

All three Synoptics place the darkness from the sixth hour to the ninth hour. The accounts then proceed to Jesus' final cry and death, with differences in the details each evangelist records.

Luke 23:45 places the veil-rending statement beside the sun-darkening statement. Matthew 27:51 and Mark 15:38 narrate the veil after Jesus' death. The chronology and literary arrangement of the veil event are therefore to be examined in the separate veil investigation; this study does not silently harmonize the sequence.

The darkness itself should not be assigned an exact start or end relative to every saying from the cross beyond the stated sixth-to-ninth-hour interval.

## 9. Witness and registration

The Synoptic narratives present the darkness as an event occurring during the Crucifixion. They do not name an individual who first notices it, describe a measurement of light intensity, or record a meteorological investigation.

This matters for the Witness distinction:
- The Gospel text reports the event.
- The Gospel writers preserve its time and scope language.
- Later readers compare the report with astronomical, historical, and canonical evidence.
- Modern hypotheses about mechanism are interpretations, not additional eyewitness testimony.

The witness records what is given; the investigator must not turn a proposed explanation into the original event.

### Biblical compliance — PASS for the Synoptic report

Matthew 27:45, Mark 15:33, and Luke 23:44–45 are examined in context. Their shared time interval and different scope wording are preserved. John's omission is recorded without treating silence as denial. The veil is reserved for its own study.

## 12. Findings

1. Matthew, Mark, and Luke report darkness from the sixth to the ninth hour, approximately noon to 3 p.m.
2. Luke additionally says the sun was darkened; the Synoptics do not identify a specific physical cause.
3. The Greek term translated “land” or “earth” has a range of meanings; the wording alone does not settle whether the darkness was local, regional, or global.
4. John does not report the darkness; this is an omission in his account, not an explicit denial.
5. An ordinary solar eclipse is not a viable explanation under the Passover/full-moon chronology and the reported three-hour interval.
6. Dust, cloud, or other atmospheric mechanisms remain hypotheses without event-specific evidence.
7. Amos 8:9–10 provides a notable Scriptural parallel concerning the sun going down at noon and mourning; Exodus 10, Joel, Isaiah, and Ezekiel provide wider darkness/judgment imagery.
8. The surviving external references sometimes connected to the darkness do not constitute secure, uncontested contemporary independent confirmation.
9. The physical cause and geographic extent remain unresolved.
10. The veil-rending and earthquake/opened-graves reports remain separate investigations.

## 13. Unresolved questions

1. What is the most responsible interpretation of *gē* in each Synoptic context: land, region, or earth?
2. How directly does Mark's darkness language invoke Amos 8:9–10?
3. What is the literary relationship among Matthew, Mark, and Luke in this report, and how should it affect claims of independent attestation?
4. What can be established about the Thallus and Phlegon references from their surviving transmission?
5. Is any natural atmospheric mechanism historically evidenced for Jerusalem at the relevant time? Current evidence does not establish one.
6. How should the physical-event and prophetic-symbolic readings be related without falsely treating them as mutually exclusive?
7. What is the precise chronological relationship between the darkness, Jesus' final sayings, His death, and the veil-rending report in each Gospel?

## 14. Provisional disposition

**Synoptic darkness testimony:** WRITTEN.  
**Time interval:** SIXTH TO NINTH HOUR, approximately noon to 3 p.m.  
**Ordinary solar eclipse:** NOT A VIABLE EXPLANATION under Passover/full-moon chronology and stated duration.  
**Other physical mechanism:** UNRESOLVED; no event-specific evidence establishes one.  
**Geographic extent:** UNRESOLVED.  
**Prophetic/canonical relationship:** SIGNIFICANT; precise literary dependence remains open.  
**GUA-W consistency:** PASS at governing level; no new Gear established.

**The text records darkness at noon during the Crucifixion. The text gives the interval. Scripture supplies prophetic parallels. Astronomy rules out an ordinary solar eclipse under the Passover conditions. The exact physical cause and geographic extent remain unresolved.**
