# Genesis 1–3 — Knowledge, the Author, and the First Departure from the Ultimate WHY

**Study status:** Text-first explanatory investigation; recorded 2026-11-18  
**Scope:** KJV Genesis 1:1–3:24, with bounded canonical cross-references  
**Relationship to GUA-W:** Examines the relationship between the Author, the created order, human knowledge and appointed work, and the first recorded human departure from God's Word. It adds no layer, Gear, runtime stage, or component to the completed architecture.

## 1. The question under examination

If Theology addresses the ultimate WHY of the One Creation and the other sciences investigate HOW the created order operates, where does the separation of knowledge from its ultimate WHY begin?

The wording of the question requires care. Genesis 1–3 does **not** use the modern category *science*, describe academic disciplines, or narrate the historical separation of physics, biology, chemistry, mathematics, and Theology. It would be anachronistic to claim that Genesis 3 records the birth of modern scientific disciplines.

The narrower question that the text can answer is this:

**Where does Genesis first record human beings receiving, interpreting, and acting upon claims about Creation and the Creator in a way that departs from the Author's stated Word?**

That question can be examined directly in Genesis 1–3.

## 2. Before the departure: knowledge and human work within the Author's order

### Genesis 1:26–31 — mankind receives a place and commission

The text says God made mankind in His image and likeness, created them male and female, blessed them, and gave them a commission concerning fruitfulness, filling the earth, subduing it, and having dominion over the living creatures (Genesis 1:26–28). God also identifies provision for food (1:29–30) and sees everything He made as very good (1:31).

**Textual observation:** Human beings are given identity, blessing, provision, and work by God. Their activity is real, but it is received within an order they did not originate.

**Bounded interpretation:** The human commission includes active engagement with Creation. The beginning account does not portray knowledge, observation, cultivation, naming, or the exercise of creaturely responsibility as inherently opposed to God.

### Genesis 2:15–17 — appointed purpose and the governing Word

The LORD God places the man in the Garden “to dress it and to keep it” (2:15). He then gives the man permission concerning the trees and a specific prohibition with a stated consequence (2:16–17).

**Textual observation:** Appointed work, provision, permission, prohibition, and consequence are all stated before the serpent's speech in Genesis 3.

**Finding:** The human task is not to invent the purpose or the governing boundary. The man receives work and command from the Author. The Word precedes the human response.

### Genesis 2:19–20 — observation and naming

God forms the beasts and fowls, brings them to Adam, and Adam gives names to them (2:19–20).

**Textual observation:** Adam observes and names creatures whose formation the text attributes to God. The act of naming is not presented as the act that creates them.

**Finding:** The beginning account contains a real human act of recognition and classification within the Creator's order. Naming is a witness activity, not authorship.

This matters to the present question: the Bible's beginning does not condemn the investigation of Creation. It places human knowledge and work under the Creator's prior action and Word.

## 3. Genesis 3:1–5 — the first recorded challenge to the Author's Word

The serpent begins by questioning the command: “Yea, hath God said...?” (3:1). The woman responds with her account of the command (3:2–3). The serpent then contradicts the stated consequence (3:4) and offers an alternative account of what God knows and what the humans may become (3:5).

The sequence is explicit:

1. God has already spoken (Genesis 2:16–17).
2. The serpent introduces a question about that Word (3:1).
3. The serpent contradicts the consequence God stated (3:4).
4. The serpent offers a competing account of God's motive and the promised result (3:5).

**Textual finding:** The first recorded departure is not a scientific experiment or the pursuit of knowledge as such. It is the introduction and reception of a competing testimony about the Author's Word, including a direct contradiction of what God had said.

**Interpretive finding:** The issue is not that the creature asks a question. The issue is that the question becomes the vehicle for challenging the reliability and authority of the Author's prior Word. The witness is invited to judge God's statement by an alternative account supplied by the serpent.

The serpent's statement about becoming “as gods, knowing good and evil” (3:5) must be read in the narrative and not turned into a claim that all knowledge is evil. The text identifies the serpent's promise as part of the temptation; it does not condemn every act of learning or investigation.

## 4. Genesis 3:6 — perception, evaluation, desire, and action

The woman sees that the tree is good for food, pleasant to the eyes, and desirable to make one wise; she takes the fruit and eats, gives it to her husband, and he eats (3:6).

The verse records distinguishable operations:

- **Perception:** she sees the tree.
- **Evaluation:** it is regarded as good for food and pleasant to the eyes.
- **Desire:** it is desirable to make one wise.
- **Action:** she takes and eats, then gives to her husband, who also eats.

**Textual observation:** These evaluations and actions occur after the serpent's competing testimony and in the presence of God's prior prohibition.

**Bounded interpretation:** The sequence shows creaturely evaluation operating in response to a competing word. The humans act upon the serpent's account rather than remaining within the command already given by God.

The text does not use the modern phrase “independent epistemic authority.” That phrase can serve only as an analytical description of the departure: the creature acts as though the competing account may govern the response, despite the Author's prior Word. It must not be presented as a direct quotation or technical term from Genesis.

## 5. Genesis 3:7–24 — the departure does not make mankind the Author

After eating, the humans recognize their nakedness, make coverings, hide, fear, and answer God's questions (3:7–13). God identifies the acts, pronounces judgment, and describes consequences (3:14–19). Adam names his wife Eve (3:20); God makes coats of skins and clothes them (3:21). God then speaks concerning the knowledge of good and evil and the tree of life, sends the man from the Garden, and guards the way to the tree of life (3:22–24).

Several distinctions are essential:

- The humans' knowledge changes, but Genesis 3:22 does not say that God's authority is transferred to them.
- Human beings continue to perceive, speak, answer, name, and act; their creaturely capacities are not erased.
- The humans' actions do not make them the source of life, the command, the judgment, or the boundary governing access to the tree of life.
- God's provision and governance continue after the transgression.

**Finding:** Genesis 3 records a departure within Creation, not a transfer of authorship. The human witness acts against the Author's Word, but does not become the Author.

## 6. What, then, became separated?

The text supports a distinction between two claims:

**Not established:** “Genesis 3 is where science began,” “Genesis 3 condemns scientific investigation,” or “Genesis 3 narrates the separation of modern academic disciplines from Theology.” None of those statements appears in the passage.

**Supported as a theological interpretation:** Genesis 3 records the first human departure from receiving the Author's Word as the governing account of reality. A competing testimony about God and His command is entertained, evaluated, and acted upon. The created witness turns from the Author's stated Word and follows a rival account.

Therefore, the first separation relevant to the present inquiry is not the differentiation of knowledge into disciplines. Genesis 1–2 already contains differentiated works, appointed functions, human observation, naming, and responsibility. The rupture in Genesis 3 is relational and authoritative: the human response is no longer governed by trust and obedience to the Word already given.

This is the bounded sense in which knowledge becomes disconnected from its ultimate WHY. The witness does not cease to live in the Author's Creation or cease to encounter its order. Rather, the witness's interpretation and action depart from the Author's Word.

## 7. Canonical witnesses: the later description of this departure

These passages are brought in after the Genesis account, as canonical witnesses. They do not replace the beginning narrative.

### Romans 1:18–25 — suppressing truth and exchanging the Creator's glory

Paul describes people who hold or suppress the truth in unrighteousness, says that what may be known of God is manifest, and describes the failure to glorify God or give thanks. He then speaks of vain reasoning, darkened hearts, and the exchange of the glory of the incorruptible God for images, serving the creature rather than the Creator.

**Contribution:** Romans 1 gives an explicit later canonical account of a human failure to honor God in relation to what is known, and of the exchange of the Creator for the creature. This is a strong witness for the theological category of knowledge detached from right recognition of the Creator.

**Boundary:** Romans 1 is not a history of modern science and does not say that every scientific finding or every scientist consciously rejects God. Its claims must be read in Paul's argument and not indiscriminately applied to every individual or disciplinary result.

### Proverbs 1:7 — the beginning of knowledge

“The fear of the LORD is the beginning of knowledge” (KJV).

**Contribution:** This proverb places the fear of the LORD at the beginning of knowledge. It supports the theological priority of rightly relating knowledge to the LORD.

**Boundary:** A proverb states wisdom teaching; it is not a technical taxonomy of scientific disciplines or a claim that empirical methods are unnecessary.

### Colossians 1:16–17 — all things by and for Christ

Paul says all things were created by Christ and for Him, and that by Him all things consist.

**Contribution:** This passage directly grounds the ultimate WHY in Christ's relation to all Creation. The created order is not self-originating and is not its own final end.

### Romans 11:36 — origin, means, and end

“For of him, and through him, and to him, are all things” (KJV).

**Contribution:** This is a concise canonical statement of the ultimate relation of all things to God. It supports the theological distinction between investigating created operations and answering why the whole exists and to whom it belongs.

## 8. Relationship to Theology as Queen of the Sciences

The Theology: Queen of the Sciences principle states that Theology addresses the ultimate WHY—the Author, originating Word, purpose, and end—while the differentiated sciences investigate HOW the created order operates within their proper domains.

Genesis 1–3 contributes the beginning-account foundation:

1. The Author creates and establishes the order (Genesis 1:1–31).
2. Human beings receive identity, provision, commission, and appointed work (Genesis 1:26–30; 2:15).
3. The Author gives the governing Word and boundary (Genesis 2:16–17).
4. Human beings observe and name what the Author has made (Genesis 2:19–20).
5. A rival testimony challenges the Word; the humans evaluate and act upon it (Genesis 3:1–6).
6. The departure brings judgment and changed conditions, but does not transfer authorship or ultimate authority to mankind (Genesis 3:7–24).

**GUA-W recognition:** The disciplines are not the source of the unity of Creation. They are differentiated human investigations within it. Their discoveries may accurately describe HOW aspects of Creation operate; the ultimate WHY remains grounded in the Author and His purpose.

The post-Fall condition calls for careful examination of human interpretations, assumptions, and claims of authority. It does not justify rejecting valid disciplinary findings merely because they are made by fallen human witnesses. Each finding must still be examined by the evidence and methods appropriate to its domain and by the Triple Constraint Law.

## 10. Conclusion

Genesis does not condemn the investigation of Creation. Before the Fall, Adam is given work, observes the creatures God has made, and names them. Human knowledge and activity are already present, but they are received and exercised within the Author's order.

The first recorded rupture is Genesis 3:1–6: the Author's prior Word is questioned, contradicted, replaced by a rival account, and followed by human evaluation and action. This is not the birth of science. It is the first recorded departure from the Author's Word in the human relationship to truth and authority.

The later canonical witness in Romans 1:18–25 describes the broader pattern of suppressing truth and exchanging the Creator for the creature. Proverbs 1:7, Colossians 1:16–17, and Romans 11:36 preserve the priority of the LORD and the ultimate relation of all things to Him.

**The sciences did not create the separation by discovering HOW. The danger arises when a witness treats a description of HOW as though it were the ultimate WHY—or treats the created order as though it were its own Author.**

GUA-W recognizes the unity that remains: one Author, one originating Word, one Creation, and many differentiated witnesses. The task is not to abolish the sciences, but to examine their findings faithfully and keep the ultimate WHY joined to the Author.
