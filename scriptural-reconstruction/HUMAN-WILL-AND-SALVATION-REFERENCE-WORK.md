# The Biblical Examination of the Human Will

**GUA-W Scripture Reference Work**  
**Status:** ACTIVE — first entry recorded; full corpus not yet audited  
**Governing question:** Does Scripture ever identify fallen man's independent will as the originating cause of his spiritual birth, faith, or salvation?

## 1. Scriptural starting point and governing posture

**This study begins at Revelation 22:18 (KJV).** The starting text is the warning against adding to the words of the prophecy of this book. This governs how the investigation handles Scripture: we must not add meanings, premises, or conclusions that the text does not give.

> **Revelation 22:18**
>
> 18 For I testify unto every man that heareth the words of the prophecy of this book, If any man shall add unto these things, God shall add unto him the plagues that are written in this book:

The immediate context must be preserved. Revelation 22:18–19 gives the paired warning concerning adding to and taking away from the words of the book of this prophecy. The scope of “this book” must be handled carefully in its literary setting; the verse must not be casually expanded into a claim that it directly settles every interpretive dispute or every question about the canon. Its force here is methodological: the study must neither add to Scripture nor take from it.

This warning governs every entry that follows. We will not insert “Free Will,” autonomous spiritual ability, or any other theological premise into a passage unless the passage and its context establish it. Nor will we remove the words that challenge a preferred doctrine. Claims must be tested by the whole relevant testimony of Scripture, with distinctions kept clear and conclusions limited to what the evidence warrants.

**Governing posture:** This is a direct, unsparing examination of claims made in the name of Scripture. No doctrine receives protection because it is popular, traditional, comforting, or preached with confidence. Equally, no passage may be exaggerated to make a stronger claim than its words and context support. The force of the conclusion must come from the Word itself.

## 2. Purpose and scope


This is a canonical, text-first reference work examining passages used to claim that human Free Will is a gift from God, especially where the claim moves from ordinary human choice or responsibility to an assertion that fallen man's independent will originates spiritual birth, faith, or salvation.

The investigation is not a collection of isolated proof-texts. Each passage must be read in its immediate literary, historical, grammatical, and canonical context. Arguments made from a passage are to be represented fairly before they are tested. Counter-texts must likewise be read in context, not deployed as competing proof-texts.

The work does not assume that every use of will, choose, desire, receive, believe, obey, or come has the same meaning. Nor does it assume that demonstrating human choice establishes autonomous spiritual ability, or that responsibility by itself proves ability.

## 3. Order of distinction before scriptural interpretation

Before quoting or interpreting a passage about the human will, the categories involved must be distinguished. These are not interchangeable terms, and one must not be silently substituted for another:

1. **Choice** — selecting, deciding, or responding among alternatives.
2. **Responsibility** — being morally or covenantally accountable for an action or omission.
3. **Ability** — possessing the capacity to perform the particular action under examination.
4. **Willingness** — desire, intention, readiness, or volition concerning that action.
5. **Divine initiative** — what God originates or performs, including calling, giving, drawing, opening, quickening, and working.
6. **Regeneration / spiritual birth** — the giving or origin of spiritual life.
7. **Faith** — believing or trusting; distinguish the act, its object, its source, and its relation to spiritual birth.
8. **Salvation** — God's saving purpose and accomplishment, and the distinct matters of reception, application, perseverance, and final completion.

### The order-of-distinction safeguard

**Logical distinction is not necessarily chronological sequence.** Listing or defining these categories in an analytical order does not claim that they occur in that temporal or causal order. For example, distinguishing faith from regeneration does not, by itself, establish which precedes the other. That question must be investigated from the relevant passages, their grammar, and their canonical context.

Likewise:
- Choice does not, by itself, establish ability.
- Responsibility does not, by itself, establish ability.
- Willingness does not, by itself, establish independent originating power.
- The presence of faith does not, by itself, identify faith's source.
- A command does not, by itself, settle the question of the hearer's capacity.
- A divine act and a human response must not be collapsed into one category.

These distinctions are an interpretive control, not a conclusion imposed upon the text. Each passage may establish, qualify, or challenge a category. The investigation must report what it actually says and leave unresolved what it does not settle.

## 4. Mandatory record for every passage entry

Every full entry must preserve these fields:

1. **Exact KJV passage** — quote the complete passage under examination, not merely a phrase detached from its sentence.
2. **Claim made from the passage** — state the strongest fair version of the argument used to support Free Will, including the inference being drawn.
3. **Immediate biblical context** — examine the surrounding verses, speaker, audience, literary unit, occasion, and argument.
4. **Counter-texts and their contexts** — identify relevant passages that qualify, challenge, or clarify the claim; read each in its own context.
5. **Original-language findings** — Hebrew, Aramaic, or Greek only where relevant; include lemma, morphology/syntax where material, semantic range, and limits. A Strong's gloss alone is not an argument.
6. **What the passage establishes** — classify the evidence separately for choice, responsibility, ability, willingness, divine initiative, regeneration/spiritual birth, faith, salvation, or another clearly named category.
7. **GUA-W Triple Constraint disposition** — separately assess Biblical compliance, Scientific coherence (using the appropriate methods of theological, linguistic, historical, or other relevant inquiry), and GUA-W consistency.
8. **Finding and limits** — distinguish explicit text, supported inference, interpretation, analogy, and unresolved matters. Record a status: PASS, PARTIAL, FAIL, UNRESOLVED, INTERPRETATION, or NOT YET INVESTIGATED.

No passage may be made to answer a question it does not address. A passage that establishes choice must not automatically be cited as proof of autonomous spiritual ability. A passage that establishes responsibility must not automatically be treated as proof of ability. A passage that describes belief must not automatically be treated as identifying the originating cause of regeneration.

## 5. Governing distinctions

- **Choice:** selection, decision, or response among alternatives.
- **Responsibility:** moral or covenantal accountability for an action or omission.
- **Ability:** capacity to perform the action in question; the domain and conditions of that capacity must be specified.
- **Willingness:** desire, intention, readiness, or volition.
- **Divine initiative:** an act, call, gift, drawing, giving, opening, quickening, or work attributed to God.
- **Spiritual birth/regeneration:** the origin of spiritual life, distinguished from its signs, effects, and accompanying responses.
- **Faith:** believing/trusting; distinguish its presence, object, expression, and source.
- **Salvation:** distinguish God's saving purpose and accomplishment from reception, application, perseverance, and final completion.

The term **independent will** is used here in a carefully limited sense: a human will alleged to originate spiritual birth, faith, or salvation independently of God's prior saving initiative. It does not mean that a person has no will, makes no choices, or bears no responsibility.

## 6. Triple Constraint application

Each entry is tested under all three constraints:

1. **Biblical compliance:** Does the claim preserve the exact KJV wording, the passage's context, and the testimony of Scripture as a whole, including the project's Genesis 1–2 foundation?
2. **Scientific coherence:** Are linguistic, grammatical, historical, and theological claims supported by appropriate evidence and methods? Are lexical possibilities distinguished from contextual meaning? Are claims no stronger than the evidence?
3. **GUA-W consistency:** Does the interpretation preserve Author → Word → Creation → Witness, God alone as Original Cause, and the distinction between divine initiative and derivative human response?

Failure under any one constraint prevents admission of a claim as an accepted GUA-W conclusion. A disputed reading may remain recorded as a competing interpretation or unresolved question.

## 7. Initial entry — HW-001: John 1:12–13

### A. Exact KJV passage

> **John 1:12–13**
>
> 12 But as many as received him, to them gave he power to become the sons of God, even to them that believe on his name:
>
> 13 Which were born, not of blood, nor of the will of the flesh, nor of the will of man, but of God.

### B. Argument commonly made

Verse 12 says that people received Christ and believed on His name. A common argument reasons that these are voluntary human acts; therefore, an independently originating human Free Will is the decisive cause of becoming God's child.

This argument must be represented carefully: the text does place receiving and believing in the description of those given authority/power to become the sons of God. The question is whether that response is thereby identified as the originating cause of the birth.

### C. Immediate context

John 1:1–14 opens with the Word's pre-existence, relation to God, and divine identity; all things are made by Him (vv. 1–3). John then introduces the witness of John the Baptist (vv. 6–8), the true Light coming into the world (vv. 9–11), the rejection by His own, and the reception by some (vv. 11–13). Verse 14 identifies the Word made flesh.

Verses 12 and 13 must be read together. Verse 12 describes receiving and believing and the authority/power given to those who believe. Verse 13 then describes the birth of those people and expressly identifies what that birth is not from and what it is from.

### D. Counter-text within the same immediate unit

John 1:13 is not a remote counter-text. It is the next sentence in the same unit. The KJV says those born were not born:
- of blood;
- of the will of the flesh;
- of the will of man;
- but of God.

The wording directly prevents the reader from assigning the originating source of this birth to human will. It does not erase the receiving and believing in verse 12. It distinguishes the response described in verse 12 from the source of the birth described in verse 13.

Further canonical counter-texts and corroborating passages to be examined in their own contexts include John 3:3–8; John 6:35–45, 63–65; Romans 9:6–24; Ephesians 2:1–10; James 1:16–18; 1 Peter 1:3, 23; and 1 John 5:1. These references are a research queue, not conclusions imported into this entry.

### E. Original Greek findings

Key expressions in the Greek text:

- **ἔλαβον (*elabon*)**, “received” — the verb used in John 1:12.
- **πιστεύουσιν (*pisteuousin*)**, “believe” — present active participle, describing those believing in His name.
- **ἐξουσία (*exousia*)**, “authority/right” — the noun translated “power” in the KJV in verse 12; it should not be confused automatically with inherent autonomous ability.
- **ἐγεννήθησαν (*egennēthēsan*)**, “were born” — aorist passive indicative, third person plural, in verse 13.
- **θελήματος (*thelēmatos*)**, “will/desire/intention” — genitive singular of θέλημα, occurring in the phrases “will of the flesh” and “will of man.”
- **ἐκ θεοῦ (*ek theou*)**, “of/from God” — identifies the source in the final clause of verse 13.

The passive form “were born” and the contrastive source phrase “but of God” are material. The syntax does not say that human beings cause themselves to be born of God. The verse explicitly negates human-will origin and affirms God as source.

Lexical caution: θέλημα can denote will, desire, or intention according to context. The conclusion here does not rest on an overextended dictionary definition; it rests on the sentence's explicit contrast and source construction.

### F. What the passage establishes

| Category | Finding | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| Choice/response | ESTABLISHED | Receiving Christ and believing on His name are stated in v. 12. |
| Responsibility | NOT SETTLED BY THIS TEXT ALONE | The passage is not a full treatment of moral accountability. |
| Human ability | NOT ESTABLISHED AS AUTONOMOUS SPIRITUAL ABILITY | Receiving/believing are described, but the text does not call independent human ability the source of birth. |
| Willingness | PRESENT BY IMPLICATION OF THE RESPONSE, NOT DEFINED AS ORIGIN | The text does not give a theory of human volition. |
| Divine initiative/source | ESTABLISHED FOR THE BIRTH | Those born are born “of God” (v. 13). |
| Spiritual birth | ESTABLISHED | The passage explicitly speaks of being born. |
| Human will as originating cause of spiritual birth | EXCLUDED BY THE TEXT'S WORDING | “Not ... of the will of the flesh, nor of the will of man, but of God.” |
| Faith's originating cause | NOT FULLY SETTLED BY THIS PASSAGE ALONE | Faith is named in v. 12; the precise causal relation between faith and regeneration must be examined across the canon. |
| Salvation as a whole | NOT EXHAUSTIVELY DEFINED HERE | The passage contributes to the question but does not alone describe every aspect of salvation. |

### G. GUA-W Triple Constraint disposition

- **Biblical compliance — PASS, within scope.** The claim that verse 12 describes receiving and believing is true. The stronger claim that human will originates the birth conflicts with verse 13.
- **Scientific coherence — PASS, within scope.** The grammatical and lexical observations above are limited to what the Greek construction supports. The passive birth verb and explicit source contrast are not being made to prove more than the sentence says.
- **GUA-W consistency — PASS, within scope.** The passage identifies God as the source of the birth and does not assign Original Cause to a derivative human response.

**Entry finding:** John 1:12–13 establishes receiving and believing as responses associated with those given authority to become God's children, while expressly identifying God—not the will of the flesh or the will of man—as the source of their birth. It does not establish fallen man's independent will as the originating cause of spiritual birth. It does not, by itself, settle every question concerning the order or causal relation of faith and regeneration.

**Status:** PASS for the narrow finding concerning the source of birth; PARTIAL for the wider question concerning the origin of faith and the complete order of salvation.

## 8. Entry — HW-002: Deuteronomy 30:11–20

### A. Exact KJV passage

> **Deuteronomy 30:11–20**
>
> 11 For this commandment which I command thee this day, it is not hidden from thee, neither is it far off.
>
> 12 It is not in heaven, that thou shouldest say, Who shall go up for us to heaven, and bring it unto us, that we may hear it, and do it?
>
> 13 Neither is it beyond the sea, that thou shouldest say, Who shall go over the sea for us, and bring it unto us, that we may hear it, and do it?
>
> 14 But the word is very nigh unto thee, in thy mouth, and in thy heart, that thou mayest do it.
>
> 15 See, I have set before thee this day life and good, and death and evil;
>
> 16 In that I command thee this day to love the LORD thy God, to walk in his ways, and to keep his commandments and his statutes and his judgments, that thou mayest live and multiply: and the LORD thy God shall bless thee in the land whither thou goest to possess it.
>
> 17 But if thine heart turn away, so that thou wilt not hear, but shalt be drawn away, and worship other gods, and serve them;
>
> 18 I denounce unto you this day, that ye shall surely perish, and that ye shall not prolong your days upon the land, whither thou passest over Jordan to go to possess it.
>
> 19 I call heaven and earth to record this day against you, that I have set before you life and death, blessing and cursing: therefore choose life, that both thou and thy seed may live:
>
> 20 That thou mayest love the LORD thy God, and that thou mayest obey his voice, and that thou mayest cleave unto him: for he is thy life, and the length of thy days: that thou mayest dwell in the land which the LORD sware unto thy fathers, to Abraham, to Isaac, and to Jacob, to give them.

### B. Argument commonly made

The argument is that Moses commands Israel to choose life (v. 19); therefore, human beings possess Free Will as a God-given, independent power to choose spiritual life and salvation. The command is sometimes treated as proof that the hearer has an autonomous ability to originate the spiritual response God requires.

The first part of the argument is accurate: Israel is commanded to choose. The further inference must be tested. The passage must identify the kind of life being set before Israel, the covenant setting of the command, and what the surrounding text says about the heart and God's own work.

### C. Immediate biblical context

Deuteronomy 30 does not begin at verse 11. Verses 1–10 describe Israel's future after the blessings and curses have come upon them and they return unto the LORD. The LORD promises to gather the dispersed people, bring them back to the land, and do them good. Crucially, verse 6 states:

> **Deuteronomy 30:6**
>
> And the LORD thy God will circumcise thine heart, and the heart of thy seed, to love the LORD thy God with all thine heart, and with all thy soul, that thou mayest live.

The text assigns the heart-circumcising action to the LORD. Its stated purpose is that Israel love the LORD with the whole heart and soul, unto life. Verse 8 then says, “And thou shalt return and obey the voice of the LORD, and do all his commandments.” The passage therefore places God's promised action toward the heart in the same restoration context as Israel's return and obedience.

Verses 11–14 explain that the commandment is not inaccessible or hidden. It is near, in Israel's mouth and heart, so that it may be done. Verses 15–20 set out the covenant alternatives: life and good, or death and evil; love, obedience, and cleaving to the LORD, or a heart turning away, refusal to hear, and idolatry. The stated consequence is explicitly tied to Israel's life in the land promised to the fathers (vv. 16, 18, 20).

The chapter's immediate subject is Israel's covenant relationship with the LORD, including restoration, obedience, blessing, curse, and life in the land. The word “life” must first be read in this covenant setting. The passage cannot simply be detached from that setting and treated as a direct technical definition of the origin of an individual's regeneration or eternal salvation.

### D. Counter-texts and contextual qualifications

**Deuteronomy 30:6** is the primary qualification within the same chapter. Israel is commanded to love and obey, but the LORD says that He will circumcise the heart so that Israel will love Him with all the heart and soul. This is an explicit divine action directed toward the heart and ordered toward the love commanded in verses 16 and 20.

This does not make the command to choose in verse 19 unreal, nor does it say that Israel does not make choices or bear covenant responsibility. It does prevent verse 19 from being isolated as though the chapter contained only an unaided human choice and no divine work upon the heart.

**Deuteronomy 10:12–16** supplies earlier covenant context: Israel is called to fear, walk, love, serve, and keep the LORD's commandments; verse 16 commands circumcision of the heart. Read with Deuteronomy 30:6, the heart is not treated as a merely external decision-point.

**Romans 10:6–10** quotes and applies language from Deuteronomy 30:12–14 to the word of faith preached concerning Christ. This is an important canonical development, but Paul's use must be examined in its own argument. It does not authorize silently replacing the original covenant setting of Deuteronomy 30:11–20 with a later doctrinal formula. Romans 10 deserves its own full entry before conclusions are drawn from the relationship.

Other relevant texts for later comparison include Jeremiah 31:31–34 and Ezekiel 36:24–28, where covenant renewal, a new heart, and God's action are described. They are corroborating research references, not substitutes for reading Deuteronomy in its own context.

### E. Original Hebrew findings

- **הַמִּצְוָה (*hammiṣwāh*)**, “the commandment” (v. 11): the definite noun identifies the command/instruction under discussion. The following clause says it is not hidden or far away.
- **נִפְלֵאת (*niphlēṯ*)**, from פלא (*p-l-ʾ*) (v. 11): a Niphal form describing something extraordinary, difficult, or beyond reach. In this context the point is that the command is not inaccessible or beyond Israel's reach; it is not a declaration that every inward spiritual capacity question has been settled.
- **בָּחַר (*bāḥar*)**, “choose” (v. 19): the verb is used in the exhortation commonly rendered “choose life.” It establishes a real covenant summons to choose between the alternatives Moses has set before Israel. The verb itself does not encode a philosophical doctrine of autonomous Free Will, nor does its occurrence alone define the source of spiritual regeneration.
- **לֵבָב (*lēbāb*)**, “heart” (vv. 6, 14, 17): the heart is the inward center of thought, disposition, and will in Hebrew usage. Verse 6 makes it the direct object of the LORD's promised circumcising action; verse 17 describes the heart turning away.
- **וּמָל (*ūmāl*)**, from מול (*m-w-l*) (v. 6): “and [He] will circumcise.” The subject is “the LORD thy God”; the heart is the object. The clause continues with **לְאַהֲבָה (*ləʾahăḇāh*)**, “to love,” expressing the purpose/result of this heart-circumcision. The grammatical subject of the circumcising action is God, not Israel.

The Hebrew findings support the plain contrast in the chapter: Israel is addressed with a genuine command and responsibility, while the LORD is identified as the one who will circumcise the heart in the restoration promise. Lexical data alone does not settle every theological question about the relationship between command, ability, divine action, and response.

### F. What the passage establishes

| Category | Finding | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| Choice/response | ESTABLISHED | Israel is commanded to choose life (v. 19). |
| Covenant responsibility | ESTABLISHED | Israel is warned of the consequences of turning away, refusing to hear, and serving other gods (vv. 17–18). |
| Human ability | PARTIAL / NOT FULLY DEFINED | The command is described as near and not inaccessible (vv. 11–14); this does not, by itself, define autonomous ability to regenerate or save oneself. |
| Willingness/disposition | ADDRESSED | The heart may turn away (v. 17); the LORD promises to circumcise the heart so that Israel will love Him (v. 6). |
| Divine initiative | ESTABLISHED IN THE RESTORATION PROMISE | The LORD gathers, brings, prospers, and circumcises the heart (vv. 3–6, 9). |
| Spiritual regeneration in the later technical sense | NOT SETTLED BY THIS PASSAGE ALONE | Heart-circumcision is explicit, but the passage is framed in Israel's covenant restoration and land life. Later canonical connections require separate examination. |
| Human will as the originating cause of spiritual birth | NOT ESTABLISHED | The text commands covenant choice but does not identify autonomous human will as the source of spiritual birth. |
| Salvation as a whole | NOT EXHAUSTIVELY DEFINED HERE | The immediate subject is covenant restoration, obedience, blessing, curse, and life in the promised land. |

### G. GUA-W Triple Constraint disposition

- **Biblical compliance — PASS, within scope.** The command to choose life must be acknowledged. So must the chapter's preceding promise that the LORD will circumcise the heart. Neither may be removed.
- **Scientific coherence — PASS, within scope.** The Hebrew observations distinguish the command to choose from the divine action upon the heart. The covenant and land context is retained. No philosophical conclusion is smuggled into the verb “choose.”
- **GUA-W consistency — PASS, within scope.** The passage presents human covenant response and responsibility while attributing the promised heart-circumcision to the LORD. It does not make the derivative human response the Original Cause of God's work.

**Entry finding:** Deuteronomy 30:19 undeniably commands Israel to choose life. It establishes covenant choice and responsibility. It does not, by that command alone, establish that fallen man's independent will is a God-given autonomous power that originates spiritual birth or salvation. The immediate chapter also declares that the LORD will circumcise Israel's heart so that Israel will love Him (v. 6). The command and the divine heart-work must be read together.

**Status:** PASS for the narrow finding that the passage commands covenant choice; FAIL for the claim that the command itself proves autonomous human will as the originating cause of spiritual birth or salvation; PARTIAL for broader questions concerning ability and the canonical relation between covenant heart-circumcision and regeneration.

## 9. Entry — HW-003: Joshua 24:14–28

### A. Exact KJV passage

> **Joshua 24:14–28**
>
> 14 Now therefore fear the Lord, and serve him in sincerity and in truth: and put away the gods which your fathers served on the other side of the flood, and in Egypt; and serve ye the Lord.
>
> 15 And if it seem evil unto you to serve the Lord, choose you this day whom ye will serve; whether the gods which your fathers served that were on the other side of the flood, or the gods of the Amorites, in whose land ye dwell: but as for me and my house, we will serve the Lord.
>
> 16 And the people answered and said, God forbid that we should forsake the Lord, to serve other gods;
>
> 17 For the Lord our God, he it is that brought us up and our fathers out of the land of Egypt, from the house of bondage, and which did those great signs in our sight, and preserved us in all the way wherein we went, and among all the people through whom we passed:
>
> 18 And the Lord drave out from before us all the people, even the Amorites which dwelt in the land: therefore will we also serve the Lord; for he is our God.
>
> 19 And Joshua said unto the people, Ye cannot serve the Lord: for he is an holy God; he is a jealous God; he will not forgive your transgressions nor your sins.
>
> 20 If ye forsake the Lord, and serve strange gods, then he will turn and do you hurt, and consume you, after that he hath done you good.
>
> 21 And the people said unto Joshua, Nay; but we will serve the Lord.
>
> 22 And Joshua said unto the people, Ye are witnesses against yourselves that ye have chosen you the Lord, to serve him. And they said, We are witnesses.
>
> 23 Now therefore put away, said he, the strange gods which are among you, and incline your heart unto the Lord God of Israel.
>
> 24 And the people said unto Joshua, The Lord our God will we serve, and his voice will we obey.
>
> 25 So Joshua made a covenant with the people that day, and set them a statute and an ordinance in Shechem.
>
> 26 And Joshua wrote these words in the book of the law of God, and took a great stone, and set it up there under an oak, that was by the sanctuary of the Lord.
>
> 27 And Joshua said unto all the people, Behold, this stone shall be a witness unto us; for it hath heard all the words of the Lord which he spake unto us: it shall be therefore a witness unto you, lest ye deny your God.
>
> 28 So Joshua let the people depart, every man unto his inheritance.

### B. Argument commonly made

The common argument takes Joshua 24:15—“choose you this day whom ye will serve”—as proof that God has given every person an independent Free Will by which he can choose God and thereby originate his own salvation. The people's declaration in verses 16–18 and Joshua's statement in verse 22 that they have chosen the LORD are then used as confirmation.

The passage plainly records a real covenant decision and holds the people accountable for it. But the question is whether the choice between covenant service and idolatry establishes autonomous spiritual ability, or whether Joshua's own warning in verse 19 and the historical/covenantal setting qualify that inference.

### C. Immediate biblical context

Joshua 24 is a covenant-renewal address at Shechem. The chapter opens with Joshua gathering Israel's tribes and recounting the LORD's acts in their history (vv. 1–13): the LORD called Abraham, multiplied his seed, delivered Israel from Egypt, brought them through the wilderness, and gave them the land. The people are not presented with a choice in a historical vacuum. The LORD's prior acts are the foundation of Joshua's appeal.

In verses 14–15, Israel is commanded to fear and serve the LORD, put away other gods, and choose whom they will serve. The alternatives are covenantal allegiance to the LORD or service to the gods associated with their ancestors and the Amorites. Joshua declares his own household's allegiance.

The people answer that they will not forsake the LORD, citing His deliverance and protection (vv. 16–18). Joshua then says, “Ye cannot serve the Lord” (v. 19), explaining that He is holy and jealous and will not bear their transgressions and sins in the manner they appear to assume. He warns that forsaking the LORD for strange gods will bring judgment (v. 20). The people renew their declaration (v. 21). Joshua identifies them as witnesses against themselves that they have chosen the LORD (v. 22), commands them to put away the strange gods among them and incline their hearts to the LORD (v. 23), and records the covenant with a stone as witness (vv. 25–27).

The chapter therefore contains both an explicit choice and an explicit warning about their capacity/fitness to serve the holy and jealous LORD. It is a covenant summons grounded in God's prior historical deliverance, not a direct discourse defining the origin of individual regeneration.

### D. Counter-texts and contextual qualifications

**Joshua 24:19** is the immediate qualification and cannot be passed over. Joshua says, “Ye cannot serve the Lord.” The KJV wording is categorical. Its explanation is covenantal and moral: the LORD is holy and jealous, and He will not bear their transgressions and sins. The next verse warns of judgment if they forsake Him.

The exact force of “cannot” in this warning requires care. The Hebrew uses a form of יָכֹל (*yākōl*), “be able / be capable,” with “serve” as its infinitive. This is genuine ability language. However, the immediate explanation concerns God's holiness, jealousy, and the danger of covenant unfaithfulness. The text does not explicitly define a universal metaphysical inability of every fallen person to perform every act of service, nor does it explain the mechanism of regeneration. Neither may the warning be neutralized as though Joshua did not say “cannot.”

**Joshua 24:23** further qualifies the people's verbal declaration: Joshua commands them to put away the strange gods already among them and incline their heart to the LORD. Their spoken pledge is not treated as sufficient evidence that the covenant problem has disappeared. The presence of foreign gods among them is a concrete issue in the scene.

**Joshua 24:2–13** supplies the controlling prior-action context. The LORD's call, deliverance, preservation, and gift of the land precede this covenant response in the narrative. Israel's choice is a response to the LORD's historical acts; the passage does not portray Israel as the originating cause of its own deliverance.

Deuteronomy 30:6 remains a relevant canonical comparison: the LORD promises to circumcise Israel's heart so that they will love Him. That text must be read in its own restoration context, but it prevents a simplistic claim that the covenant Scriptures speak only of human choice and never of God's action upon the heart.

### E. Original Hebrew findings

- **בָּחַר (*bāḥar*)**, “choose” (vv. 15, 22): the verb describes choosing/selecting. In verse 15, Israel is summoned to choose whom it will serve; in verse 22, Joshua says the people have chosen the LORD for service. The verb establishes choice in the covenant scene. It does not by itself define autonomous spiritual power or the source of regeneration.
- **עָבַד (*ʿāḇaḏ*)**, “serve” (vv. 14–19, 21–24): the repeated verb concerns service/allegiance. The choice is specifically whom Israel will serve. It is not an abstract choice detached from covenant loyalty and worship.
- **לֹא תוּכְלוּ (*lōʾ tūḵəlū*)**, “ye cannot” (v. 19): לֹא is the negative particle; תוּכְלוּ is Qal imperfect, second-person masculine plural, from יָכֹל (*yākōl*), “be able, be capable.” The following infinitive לַעֲבֹד (*laʿăḇōḏ*) means “to serve.” The grammar supports the KJV's direct ability wording. Context is required to determine the precise scope and force of the warning.
- **קְדֹשִׁים (*qədōšîm*)**, “holy” (v. 19): the adjective describes the LORD's holiness; the verse grounds Joshua's warning in who the LORD is.
- **קַנּוֹא (*qannōʾ*)**, “jealous” (v. 19): describes the LORD's covenant jealousy/exclusive claim. In this covenant context it underscores that Israel cannot combine service to the LORD with rival gods.
- Verse 23's “incline your heart” is a command addressed to the people. It must be read alongside Joshua's warning in verse 19, not used to erase it.

The Hebrew therefore gives no basis for pretending that the word “choose” is absent or that the word “cannot” is merely an English embellishment. Both are present in the same covenant exchange. Their relationship must be interpreted from the whole scene.

### F. What the passage establishes

| Category | Finding | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| Choice/response | ESTABLISHED | Israel is told to choose whom it will serve, and Joshua says they have chosen the LORD (vv. 15, 22). |
| Covenant responsibility | ESTABLISHED | The people are commanded to put away idols, incline their hearts, and remain faithful; a covenant and witness-stone formalize accountability (vv. 23–27). |
| Ability | EXPLICITLY QUALIFIED / SCOPE REQUIRES CARE | Joshua says “Ye cannot serve the Lord” (v. 19); the precise scope of this warning must be read in its covenant context. |
| Willingness | PRESENT IN THE PEOPLE'S DECLARATIONS | They repeatedly say they will serve the LORD (vv. 16–18, 21, 24); Joshua nevertheless confronts the idols among them. |
| Divine initiative | ESTABLISHED IN THE HISTORICAL NARRATIVE | The LORD called, delivered, preserved, and gave the land before the covenant response (vv. 2–13). |
| Spiritual regeneration | NOT ESTABLISHED AS A TECHNICAL SUBJECT | The passage addresses covenant allegiance and idolatry, not the mechanics or order of the new birth. |
| Human will as the originating cause of salvation | NOT ESTABLISHED | The people choose covenant service, but the text does not identify their choice as the originating cause of deliverance, regeneration, or salvation. |
| Witness/accountability | ESTABLISHED | The people are witnesses against themselves; the stone is set as a covenant witness (vv. 22, 26–27). |

### G. GUA-W Triple Constraint disposition

- **Biblical compliance — PASS, within scope.** The real command to choose and the people's declared choice are preserved. So is Joshua's warning that they cannot serve the LORD, the command to put away idols, and the LORD's prior acts in Israel's history.
- **Scientific coherence — PASS, within scope.** The Hebrew forms support both the choice language and the ability-negation in verse 19. The entry does not extend either verb beyond the covenant scene without further evidence.
- **GUA-W consistency — PASS, within scope.** The LORD's prior acts are narrated as the ground of Israel's covenant response. Human choice is a real witness-response and responsibility; it is not presented as the Original Cause of God's deliverance.

**Entry finding:** Joshua 24:15 establishes that Israel is summoned to make a real covenant choice concerning whom it will serve. Verse 22 confirms that the people have chosen the LORD and are witnesses against themselves. But Joshua 24:19 immediately warns, “Ye cannot serve the Lord,” and verses 23–27 expose the continuing demand to put away idols and formalize covenant accountability. The chapter is not a proof that autonomous human Free Will originates salvation. It is a covenant-renewal summons grounded in the LORD's prior deliverance, and it contains a direct warning about the people's capacity and fidelity in serving the holy God.

**Status:** PASS for the existence of covenant choice and responsibility; PARTIAL for the precise scope of “Ye cannot serve the Lord”; FAIL for using this passage alone as proof that independent human will originates spiritual birth or salvation.

## 10. Entry — HW-004: Genesis 3:1–7

### A. Exact KJV passage

> **Genesis 3:1–7**
>
> 1 Now the serpent was more subtil than any beast of the field which the LORD God had made. And he said unto the woman, Yea, hath God said, Ye shall not eat of every tree of the garden?
>
> 2 And the woman said unto the serpent, We may eat of the fruit of the trees of the garden:
>
> 3 But of the fruit of the tree which is in the midst of the garden, God hath said, Ye shall not eat of it, neither shall ye touch it, lest ye die.
>
> 4 And the serpent said unto the woman, Ye shall not surely die:
>
> 5 For God doth know that in the day ye eat thereof, then your eyes shall be opened, and ye shall be as gods, knowing good and evil.
>
> 6 And when the woman saw that the tree was good for food, and that it was pleasant to the eyes, and a tree to be desired to make one wise, she took of the fruit thereof, and did eat, and gave also unto her husband with her; and he did eat.
>
> 7 And the eyes of them both were opened, and they knew that they were naked; and they sewed fig leaves together, and made themselves aprons.

### B. Argument commonly made

This passage is sometimes used to argue that the first humans possessed an independent Free Will that enabled them to choose either obedience or disobedience, and that the Fall therefore originated in an autonomous human decision. Others use Eve's evaluation of the tree and her act of taking and eating to argue that she exercised the authority to determine good and evil for herself.

The passage does show deliberate human action: the woman sees, evaluates, takes, and eats; the man also eats. It does not describe them as physically compelled. But the text must be examined carefully before turning those actions into a complete philosophical account of Free Will, the origin of sin, or the source of salvation.

A second question is whether the decisive change occurred simply when the humans exercised the capacity to choose, or when the serpent challenged God's word and the woman accepted a rival account of what was true, desirable, and to be gained by disobedience. The sequence is explicit; a full theory of the human will is not.

### C. Immediate biblical context

Genesis 3:1–7 follows directly after Genesis 2:15–25. God placed the man in the garden to dress and keep it (2:15), gave him the command concerning the tree of the knowledge of good and evil (2:16–17), formed the woman and brought her to the man (2:18–25), and the man and woman were naked and not ashamed (2:25).

The command in Genesis 2:16–17 is important to the comparison:

> **Genesis 2:16–17**
>
> 16 And the LORD God commanded the man, saying, Of every tree of the garden thou mayest freely eat:
>
> 17 But of the tree of the knowledge of good and evil, thou shalt not eat of it: for in the day that thou eatest thereof thou shalt surely die.

The command contains both generous permission and a specific prohibition. The serpent's opening question in Genesis 3:1 distorts the emphasis by asking whether God had prohibited eating from every tree. The woman answers that the fruit of the trees may be eaten, but says of the central tree, “Ye shall not eat of it, neither shall ye touch it, lest ye die” (3:2–3). The prohibition against touching is not present in the command as recorded in Genesis 2:16–17. The text does not tell us whether this wording was supplied by the woman, learned from another instruction, or reflects some unrecorded detail; it only records the difference between the two statements.

The serpent then contradicts God's warning: “Ye shall not surely die” (3:4). He offers a rival explanation of God's motive and promises opened eyes and godlike knowledge (3:5). Verse 6 narrates the woman's evaluation and action in three descriptions: the tree was good for food, pleasant to the eyes, and desirable to make one wise. She takes and eats, gives to her husband who is with her, and he eats.

Verse 7 records the immediate result: their eyes are opened, they know they are naked, and they make coverings. The promised opening of eyes occurs, but the result narrated is not the uncomplicated elevation promised by the serpent. Shame and concealment enter the account. The larger consequences continue in Genesis 3:8 onward and must be examined there before claiming that verses 1–7 contain the whole account of the Fall.

### D. Counter-texts and contextual qualifications

**Genesis 2:16–17** is the controlling prior command. The serpent's question and denial must be tested against what God actually said. The text records permission to eat freely from every tree except the specified tree, and a clear death warning concerning disobedience.

**Genesis 3:4–5** records the serpent's direct contradiction and his attribution of motive to God. The claim that God is withholding godlike knowledge is the serpent's speech, not an inspired narrator's confirmation that God acted from jealousy or deception. The narrator records the temptation; readers must not adopt the serpent's accusation as fact.

**Genesis 3:7–13** shows the immediate aftermath in greater detail: opened eyes, awareness of nakedness, hiding, fear, blame, and interrogation. These developments qualify any claim that the act simply produced the promised wisdom in an unambiguously beneficial sense.

**1 Timothy 2:13–14** later states that Adam was not deceived, but the woman being deceived was in the transgression. **2 Corinthians 11:3** refers to the serpent beguiling Eve through subtilty. These later texts add canonical testimony about deception and the distinct roles of Eve and Adam. They do not erase the Genesis narrative's statement that Adam also ate, nor do they make him a passive nonparticipant.

**Romans 5:12–19** places the entrance of sin and death in the world through one man and develops the Adam/Christ contrast. That passage must be examined in its own argument; it prevents reducing the biblical account of the Fall to Eve's act alone.

### E. Original Hebrew findings

- **עָרוּם (*ʿārūm*)**, “subtil” (3:1): the adjective describes the serpent as shrewd/crafty. Genesis 2:25 uses the related-sounding **עֲרוּמִּים (*ʿărummîm*)**, “naked.” The sound-play links the couple's unashamed nakedness at the close of chapter 2 with the serpent's craftiness at the opening of chapter 3. The shared sound does not mean the words have the same meaning.
- **אַף כִּי (*ʾap kî*)**, introducing the serpent's question (3:1): the expression can carry a questioning or challenging force in context. The serpent's formulation places God's generosity under suspicion by framing the command as a broad restriction.
- **לֹא תֹאכְלוּ (*lōʾ tōʾḵəlū*)**, “ye shall not eat” (3:3): the woman's report contains the prohibition. Her additional phrase **וְלֹא תִגְּעוּ (*wəlōʾ tigəʿū*)**, “neither shall ye touch,” is not in the wording of Genesis 2:16–17. The difference is textually observable; the reason for the difference is not stated.
- **לֹא מוֹת תְּמֻתוּן (*lōʾ mōṯ təmutūn*)**, “Ye shall not surely die” (3:4): the serpent directly negates the death warning. The Hebrew uses a negative followed by the infinitive-absolute construction with the finite verb, echoing the emphatic death formulation in Genesis 2:17 while reversing its polarity.
- **יֹדֵעַ (*yōḏēaʿ*)**, “doth know” (3:5): the serpent asserts that God knows what will happen. This is part of the serpent's accusation and promise, not a narrator-certified explanation of God's motive.
- **וַתֵּרֶא (*wattērēʾ*)**, “and [the woman] saw” (3:6): a wayyiqtol form narrating the next event in the sequence. What follows is her assessment of the tree.
- **טוֹב (*ṭōḇ*)**, “good” (3:6): the tree is assessed as good for food. The word “good” here describes the tree's perceived suitability for eating; it does not by itself mean that the woman formally claimed God's authority to define moral good and evil.
- **תַּאֲוָה (*taʾăwāh*)**, “desire/delight” (3:6): the tree is described as a delight to the eyes. This names attraction or desire, not an independent philosophical faculty.
- **נֶחְמָד (*neḥmāḏ*)**, “to be desired” (3:6): a Niphal participial form from חָמַד (*ḥāmaḏ*), describing the tree as desirable. The text presents desire as part of the woman's evaluation.
- **לְהַשְׂכִּיל (*ləhaśkîl*)**, “to make one wise” (3:6): a Hiphil infinitive from שָׂכַל (*śāḵal*), expressing the prospect of gaining insight or becoming wise. The phrase connects the tree's desirability to the serpent's promise of knowledge in verse 5.
- **וַתִּקַּח (*wattiqqaḥ*)**, “and she took”; **וַתֹּאכַל (*wattōʾḵal*)**, “and she ate”; **וַתִּתֵּן (*wattittēn*)**, “and she gave”; **וַיֹּאכַל (*wayyōʾḵal*)**, “and he ate” (3:6): the narrative chain of wayyiqtol verbs advances the actions in sequence. It records deliberate acts but does not, by grammatical form alone, explain the metaphysical origin of the will or the complete causal process behind the transgression.
- **עִמָּהּ (*ʿimmāh*)**, “with her” (3:6): the phrase says her husband was with her when she gave him the fruit. It supports his presence in the scene, but the verse does not narrate every preceding moment or specify how long he had been there.

The Hebrew supports the narrative sequence of perception, evaluation/desire, taking, eating, giving, and the man's eating. It also preserves the distinction between what God commanded, what the woman reports, and what the serpent says. The grammar does not by itself establish a comprehensive doctrine of autonomous Free Will.

### F. What the passage establishes

| Category | Finding | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| Human choice/action | ESTABLISHED | The woman takes and eats; the man also eats (v. 6). |
| Responsibility | ESTABLISHED IN THE NARRATIVE, WITH FULL JUDGMENT DEVELOPED LATER | The humans act contrary to the command; the following verses record God's interrogation and consequences. |
| Ability to choose between actions | ACTION IS RECORDED; GENERAL CAPACITY THEORY NOT DEFINED | They perform the prohibited act; the passage does not offer a philosophical account of the will's capacities. |
| Desire/willingness | ESTABLISHED AS PART OF THE WOMAN'S EVALUATION | The tree is perceived as good, attractive, and desirable for wisdom (v. 6). |
| Deception/competing testimony | ESTABLISHED | The serpent questions, contradicts, and promises; later Scripture explicitly describes Eve as deceived. |
| Authority to define good and evil independently | NOT EXPLICITLY FORMULATED IN THESE VERSES | The tree concerns knowledge of good and evil, but the text does not say in these verses that the woman formally claimed divine authority to define moral categories. |
| Divine initiative | GOD'S PRIOR COMMAND IS ESTABLISHED | The prohibition precedes the temptation (Gen. 2:16–17). |
| Spiritual regeneration/salvation | NOT THE SUBJECT OF THIS PASSAGE | Genesis 3:1–7 narrates temptation and transgression, not the origin of saving faith or the new birth. |
| Human will as the originating cause of salvation | NOT ADDRESSED / NOT ESTABLISHED | The passage concerns the Fall, not the source of salvation. |

### G. GUA-W Triple Constraint disposition

- **Biblical compliance — PASS, within scope.** The record preserves God's original command, the serpent's distortion and contradiction, the woman's recorded evaluation, both humans' eating, and the immediate result. It does not treat the serpent's account of God's motive as truth.
- **Scientific coherence — PASS, within scope.** Hebrew observations distinguish the words and narrative forms without deriving a complete psychology or metaphysics from lexical glosses. The difference between Genesis 2:16–17 and the woman's quotation in 3:3 is noted without inventing an explanation for it.
- **GUA-W consistency — PASS, within scope.** God's command precedes the human response. The humans are witnesses/agents who act within an order they did not author. Their choice is real, but the passage does not grant them authorship of the command or authority to revise what God said.

**Entry finding:** Genesis 3:1–7 records a progression from the serpent's challenge to God's word, through a competing account of God's command and motive, to the woman's evaluation and desire, her taking and eating, and the man's eating. The text establishes human action, desire, deception, and transgression. It does not say that the mere possession of choice is itself the Fall, nor does it provide a complete philosophical definition of Free Will. The distinction between choosing an action and claiming authority to redefine God's word is a supported line of inquiry, but the stronger claim that the woman formally assumed divine authority to define good and evil is not explicitly stated in these seven verses. The tree's promised knowledge, the serpent's lie, and the human evaluation must be examined in the wider Genesis 2–3 context.

**Status:** PASS for the narrated sequence of temptation, desire, choice, and transgression; PARTIAL for the precise relationship between deception, desire, and volition; NOT ESTABLISHED for a comprehensive philosophical doctrine of autonomous Free Will; NOT THE SUBJECT for the origin of regeneration or salvation.

## 11. Entry — HW-005: John 3:1–8

### A. Exact KJV passage

> **John 3:1–8**
>
> 1 There was a man of the Pharisees, named Nicodemus, a ruler of the Jews:
>
> 2 The same came to Jesus by night, and said unto him, Rabbi, we know that thou art a teacher come from God: for no man can do these miracles that thou doest, except God be with him.
>
> 3 Jesus answered and said unto him, Verily, verily, I say unto thee, Except a man be born again, he cannot see the kingdom of God.
>
> 4 Nicodemus saith unto him, How can a man be born when he is old? can he enter the second time into his mother's womb, and be born?
>
> 5 Jesus answered, Verily, verily, I say unto thee, Except a man be born of water and of the Spirit, he cannot enter into the kingdom of God.
>
> 6 That which is born of the flesh is flesh; and that which is born of the Spirit is spirit.
>
> 7 Marvel not that I said unto thee, Ye must be born again.
>
> 8 The wind bloweth where it listeth, and thou hearest the sound thereof, but canst not tell whence it cometh, and whither it goeth: so is every one that is born of the Spirit.

### B. Argument commonly made

This passage is commonly cited to establish that the new birth is necessary for seeing and entering God's kingdom. It is also used in arguments about Free Will in two opposing ways.

One argument says that a person must choose to be born again, and that the command or invitation to believe presupposes an independent ability to originate the new birth. Another points to Jesus' repeated passive birth language and His comparison with the wind to argue that the birth is not self-produced and that the Spirit acts sovereignly.

The second argument has substantial textual support concerning the source and agency of the birth. But we must not claim more than the passage says: John 3:1–8 does not here give a complete account of every relation between regeneration, faith, repentance, and the full order of salvation. Nor does the wind analogy mean that a human being has no response or responsibility; those questions require the wider discourse and other passages.

### C. Immediate biblical context

Nicodemus is a Pharisee, a ruler of the Jews, and a teacher in Israel (vv. 1, 10). He comes to Jesus at night and acknowledges that Jesus is a teacher come from God, citing the signs Jesus performs (v. 2). Jesus does not begin by discussing Nicodemus's decision-making capacity. He declares the necessity of birth from above: without it, a person cannot see the kingdom of God (v. 3).

The Greek word translated “again” in verse 3, ἄνωθεν (*anōthen*), can mean “again” or “from above.” Nicodemus understands Jesus in the sense of a second physical birth and asks how an old man could enter his mother's womb again (v. 4). Jesus answers by distinguishing birth from flesh and birth from Spirit (vv. 5–6), and repeats the necessity: “Ye must be born again” (v. 7).

Verse 8 uses the wind as an analogy. The wind's movement is perceptible by its sound, but its origin and destination are not under the hearer's control or fully observable. Jesus then says, “so is every one that is born of the Spirit.” The analogy emphasizes the Spirit's mysterious and uncommanded operation, while the final clause explicitly identifies the person under discussion as one born of the Spirit.

The discourse continues beyond verse 8. In verses 9–10 Jesus challenges Nicodemus, the teacher of Israel, for not understanding these things. Verses 11–15 move to testimony concerning heavenly things and the Son of man; verses 16–21 speak of God's love, the giving of the Son, belief, judgment, and the response to light. Those later verses matter to the relationship between new birth and faith and must not be silently imported as though they were all contained in verses 1–8.

### D. Counter-texts and contextual qualifications

**Ezekiel 36:24–28** is a major Old Testament background for Jesus' words to a teacher of Israel. God promises to gather His people, sprinkle clean water upon them, cleanse them from uncleanness and idols, give them a new heart and a new spirit, remove the stony heart, give a heart of flesh, and put His Spirit within them, causing them to walk in His statutes. The repeated subject of the promised actions is the LORD. This makes covenant cleansing and Spirit-given renewal a strong contextual lens for “water and Spirit” in John 3:5–6. It is a supported intertextual connection, not a license to claim that every interpretive question about “water” is uncontested.

Interpretations of “water and the Spirit” in John 3:5 include (a) the one cleansing-and-renewing work promised in Ezekiel 36, (b) water baptism together with the Spirit, and (c) other proposals such as physical birth followed by spiritual birth. The immediate discourse and Old Testament background must adjudicate these options. The text explicitly joins “water and Spirit”; it does not explicitly say “physical birth and then spiritual birth.” The Ezekiel connection is especially relevant because Jesus asks how Nicodemus, “a master of Israel,” does not know these things (v. 10). The precise relation of water to the Spirit should nevertheless remain identified as an interpretive question rather than presented as a settled lexical fact.

**John 1:12–13** is an important comparison within the same Gospel. Receiving and believing are named, but the birth is said not to be of the will of the flesh or the will of man, but of God. John 3 adds Jesus' direct teaching that birth is “of the Spirit.” The two passages converge on divine source without making the act of believing disappear.

**John 3:14–21** must be read as the continuation of this very conversation. It explicitly speaks of believing in the lifted-up Son and of the condemnation/light contrast. It is relevant to faith's relation to life, but it does not reverse Jesus' statement that birth from above is necessary. A complete causal sequence should not be inferred from one clause while ignoring the discourse as a whole.

**John 6:63–65**, **James 1:18**, and **1 Peter 1:3, 23** are further passages for later full entries because they speak of the Spirit's life-giving work or God's begetting. They are not substitutes for the present context.

### E. Original Greek findings

- **ἄνωθεν (*anōthen*)**, “again” (v. 3; repeated in v. 7): the adverb can mean “from above” as well as “again/anew.” Nicodemus hears the idea of a second birth in verse 4, while Jesus' later language “born of the Spirit” gives the birth its heavenly/divine source. The double sense is important in John's discourse; the translation “again” should not make the “from above” dimension disappear.
- **γεννηθῇ (*gennēthē*)**, “be born” (vv. 3, 5): aorist passive subjunctive, third-person singular, from γεννάω (*gennaō*). In the construction “unless one be born,” the person is grammatically the recipient of the birth, not the expressed agent who gives birth to himself. The passive form supports the distinction between receiving birth and originating it.
- **οὐ δύναται (*ou dynatai*)**, “cannot” (vv. 3, 5): the negated verb δύναμαι (*dynamai*) expresses inability/capacity. Jesus says that without this birth a person is not able to see or enter the kingdom. The inability is directly tied to the absence of the birth; the text does not present new birth as an optional enhancement.
- **ἐξ ὕδατος καὶ πνεύματος (*ex hydatos kai pneumatos*)**, “of water and of the Spirit” (v. 5): the preposition ἐκ (*ek*) expresses source/origin. The phrase joins water and Spirit in the one birth statement. The precise referent of “water” remains a contextual interpretive question; Ezekiel 36:25–27 is a significant background.
- **τὸ γεγεννημένον (*to gegennēmenon*)**, “that which is born” (v. 6): perfect passive participle, neuter nominative singular, from γεννάω. The perfect form presents the one born as standing in the resulting state of that birth. The parallel clauses distinguish what is born of flesh from what is born of Spirit.
- **ἐκ τῆς σαρκὸς (*ek tēs sarkos*) / ἐκ τοῦ πνεύματος (*ek tou pneumatos*)**, “of the flesh” / “of the Spirit” (v. 6): the repeated source construction sets two distinct origins and corresponding kinds of birth in contrast. Flesh produces flesh; the Spirit produces what is spirit.
- **δεῖ (*dei*)**, “must” (v. 7): an impersonal verb expressing necessity. Jesus says, “Ye must be born again.” The necessity is not phrased as a self-appointed human project; it is the requirement Jesus declares.
- **πνεῦμα (*pneuma*)** and **πνεῖ (*pnei*)** (v. 8): πνεῦμα can mean “wind” or “spirit,” and πνεῖ is the verb “blows.” The verse exploits the relation between the terms. KJV renders the first occurrence “wind” and the final occurrence “Spirit.” This is a genuine wordplay/ambiguity in the Greek, not a reason to flatten the verse into only one English sense.
- **θέλει (*thelei*)**, “listeth” (v. 8): present active indicative, third-person singular, from θέλω (*thelō*), “will/wish.” In the wind image, it describes where the wind blows as it wills. The verse does not say that the person being born chooses the Spirit's origin or directs the Spirit's movement.
- **γεγεννημένος (*gegennēmenos*)**, “that is born” (v. 8): perfect middle/passive participle, nominative masculine singular, from γεννάω. In context it is passive in sense: the person is one who has been born of the Spirit.

The Greek grammar supports the distinction between the Spirit as source/agent of birth and the human as its recipient. It does not, by itself, settle every later theological dispute about the order of regeneration and faith. The argument here rests on Jesus' explicit source language, passive birth constructions, and stated necessity—not on a claim that one grammatical form independently answers every question.

### F. What the passage establishes

| Category | Finding | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| Necessity of new birth | ESTABLISHED | Without birth from above, a person cannot see or enter God's kingdom (vv. 3, 5). |
| Source of spiritual birth | EXPLICITLY THE SPIRIT | Jesus contrasts flesh-birth with birth “of the Spirit” (v. 6), and says the person is born of the Spirit (v. 8). |
| Human self-origination of the new birth | NOT SUPPORTED; PASSIVE BIRTH LANGUAGE POINTS OTHERWISE | The person is described as being born; the Spirit is identified as source. |
| Human choice/response | NOT THE FOCUS OF vv. 1–8 | Nicodemus comes and speaks, but Jesus' teaching in these verses concerns the necessity and source of birth. |
| Human responsibility | NOT EXHAUSTIVELY TREATED IN vv. 1–8 | The wider discourse addresses belief, light, and judgment (vv. 16–21). |
| Human ability apart from new birth | EXPLICITLY LIMITED | Without birth from above, one cannot see or enter the kingdom (vv. 3, 5). |
| Divine initiative | ESTABLISHED | Birth is of the Spirit; the wind analogy emphasizes the Spirit's operation beyond human control (vv. 6, 8). |
| Faith's relation to regeneration | NOT FULLY SETTLED BY vv. 1–8 ALONE | Faith appears in the continuation (vv. 14–21); the full relation requires examining the complete discourse and other texts. |
| Salvation as a whole | NOT EXHAUSTIVELY DEFINED | The passage establishes the necessity and divine source of birth, not every aspect of the order and application of salvation. |

### G. GUA-W Triple Constraint disposition

- **Biblical compliance — PASS, within scope.** Jesus' words establish the necessity of new birth, the contrast between flesh and Spirit, and birth from the Spirit. The entry preserves Nicodemus's response, the wind/Spirit wordplay, and the unresolved interpretive question surrounding “water.”
- **Scientific coherence — PASS, within scope.** The Greek morphology and syntax are identified with limits. The entry does not treat a passive verb as a complete systematic theology, and it distinguishes lexical ambiguity from contextual interpretation.
- **GUA-W consistency — PASS, within scope.** The Spirit is identified as the source of spiritual birth. The human is the one who must be born, not the originating author of that birth. This preserves God as Original Cause while leaving the relation of faith and regeneration for further textual investigation.

**Entry finding:** Jesus declares that a person must be born from above to see and enter the kingdom of God. He distinguishes flesh-born from Spirit-born life, identifies the Spirit as the source of the latter, and describes the person as one who is born—not as the one who originates his own birth. The wind analogy underscores the Spirit's operation, which cannot be directed or mastered by the human observer. John 3:1–8 therefore does not support the claim that independent human will originates spiritual birth. It establishes the necessity and divine source of the new birth. The relation of this birth to believing is further addressed in John 3:14–21 and must be examined without imposing a chronology not established by these eight verses alone.

**Status:** PASS for the necessity and Spirit-source of new birth; PARTIAL for the exact interpretation of “water and Spirit” and the relation of faith to regeneration; FAIL for using John 3:1–8 to claim that independent human will originates spiritual birth.

## 12. Entry — HW-006: John 6:35–45, 63–65

### A. Exact KJV passage

> 40 And this is the will of him that sent me, that every one which seeth the Son, and believeth on him, may have everlasting life: and I will raise him up at the last day.
>
> 41 The Jews then murmured at him, because he said, I am the bread which came down from heaven.
>
> 42 And they said, Is not this Jesus, the son of Joseph, whose father and mother we know? how is it then that he saith, I came down from heaven?
>
> 43 Jesus therefore answered and said unto them, Murmur not among yourselves.
>
> 44 No man can come to me, except the Father which hath sent me draw him: and I will raise him up at the last day.
>
> 45 It is written in the prophets, And they shall be all taught of God. Every man therefore that hath heard, and hath learned of the Father, cometh unto me.

> **John 6:63–65**
>
> 63 It is the spirit that quickeneth; the flesh profiteth nothing: the words that I speak unto you, they are spirit, and they are life.
>
> 64 But there are some of you that believe not. For Jesus knew from the beginning who they were that believed not, and who should betray him.
>
> 65 And he said, Therefore said I unto you, that no man can come unto me, except it were given unto him of my Father.

### B. Argument commonly made

This passage is central to the question of whether fallen man's independent will originates his coming to Christ, faith, or salvation.

One argument emphasizes verse 40: “every one which seeth the Son, and believeth on him” has everlasting life. It concludes that believing is the decisive human condition and that the individual must therefore possess an independent ability to initiate faith. Another emphasizes verses 37 and 44–45, arguing that those who come are given by the Father, that no one can come unless drawn by the Father, and that the one who has heard and learned from the Father comes. Verses 63 and 65 add the Spirit's quickening and the Father's giving.

The text must not be reduced to either slogan. Jesus explicitly speaks of coming and believing, and He explicitly speaks of the Father's giving, drawing, teaching, and granting. The question is how Jesus relates these realities in the same discourse.

### C. Immediate biblical context

John 6 begins with the feeding of the five thousand (vv. 1–14), followed by Jesus' walking on the sea (vv. 16–21). The crowd seeks Jesus after the sign, and Jesus challenges them because they sought Him for bread rather than because they understood the sign (vv. 22–27). He directs them to the food that endureth unto everlasting life and identifies Himself as the bread of life (vv. 27–35).

In verses 35–40 Jesus joins coming and believing to everlasting life, while identifying the Father's giving and will as decisive in the Son's mission. Verse 36 is an immediate warning against assuming that seeing Jesus or witnessing signs automatically produces faith: Jesus says some have seen Him and yet do not believe. Verse 37 states that all the Father gives to the Son shall come to Him, and the Son promises not to cast out the one who comes. Verses 38–40 ground this in the Son's obedience to the Father's will and the preservation and resurrection of those given to Him.

In verses 41–42 the hearers murmur at Jesus' claim to have come down from heaven. Jesus answers that no one can come unless the Father draws him (v. 44), and then cites the prophets: “they shall be all taught of God.” He explains, “Every man therefore that hath heard, and hath learned of the Father, cometh unto me” (v. 45). The “therefore” connects the Father's drawing with hearing and learning from the Father and coming to Christ. The text does not portray drawing as a human self-originated act.

The omitted verses 46–62 are part of the same discourse and must remain in view. Jesus speaks of seeing the Father, eating the living bread, His flesh given for the life of the world, and the necessity of eating His flesh and drinking His blood. His hearers dispute and many find the saying hard (vv. 52, 60–61). This discourse continues the conflict over Jesus' identity and the reception of His words; verses 63–65 cannot be detached from it.

In verses 63–65 Jesus says the Spirit quickens, the flesh profits nothing, and His words are spirit and life. He acknowledges that some hearers do not believe (v. 64), and then repeats that no one can come unless it has been given to him by the Father (v. 65). Verse 64 prevents us from treating outward proximity, hearing, or seeing as equivalent to believing. Verse 65 restates the inability of verse 44 and identifies the Father's grant as necessary.

### D. Counter-texts and contextual qualifications

**John 6:40** must be retained in full. Jesus says that everyone who sees the Son and believes on Him may have everlasting life. Believing is not erased or made unreal by the Father's giving and drawing. The question is not whether faith occurs, but whether the passage identifies autonomous human will as faith's originating cause.

**John 6:36 and 64** show that some see Jesus and hear His words but do not believe. The discourse therefore distinguishes exposure to Jesus' signs and teaching from believing response. It does not permit the inference that mere human observation automatically produces faith.

**John 12:32** uses the same Greek verb for “draw” when Jesus says that, if lifted up from the earth, He will draw all men unto Him. This must be considered when assessing the meaning and scope of “draw” in John 6:44. It warns against asserting that the verb by itself always means an irresistible action applied only to a restricted group. John 12:32 has its own context, including the Greeks who seek Jesus (12:20–33), and the scope of “all” has been interpreted in more than one way. The lexical overlap does not by itself settle the relation between the two passages.

**John 6:45** is Jesus' own explanatory continuation of verse 44. The Father draws; the one who has heard and learned from the Father comes to Christ. This makes the Father's teaching relevant to the drawing. We must not detach verse 44 from the explanation immediately supplied in verse 45.

**John 6:63** says, “It is the spirit that quickeneth; the flesh profiteth nothing.” The statement establishes the Spirit's life-giving role and the insufficiency of “the flesh” in the discourse. The precise scope of “flesh” should be read in context: Jesus contrasts flesh with Spirit and identifies His words as spirit and life. The verse should not be converted into the claim that human bodily existence or every ordinary human action has no value whatsoever.

**John 6:65** repeats the inability statement and says coming must be given by the Father. The Greek perfect passive form translated “it were given” presents the grant as something given, not self-originated by the person coming. The verse does not by itself define every stage or chronology of salvation.

**Ephesians 2:1–10**, **Romans 8:5–11**, and **1 Corinthians 2:10–16** are relevant later entries because they address spiritual death, the Spirit, and the natural person's relation to the things of God. They should be examined separately rather than imported as substitutes for John's own argument.

### E. Original Greek findings

- **ὁ ἐρχόμενος (*ho erchomenos*)**, “he that cometh” (v. 35): present middle/deponent participle, nominative masculine singular, from ἔρχομαι (*erchomai*), “come.” Jesus genuinely describes the person as coming to Him. The participle identifies the person who comes; it does not identify that person as the originating cause of the Father's giving or drawing.
- **ὁ πιστεύων (*ho pisteuōn*)**, “he that believeth” (v. 35); **πιστεύων (*pisteuōn*)** (v. 40): present active participle from πιστεύω (*pisteuō*), “believe.” Faith is explicitly named as the human response associated with everlasting life. The active voice identifies the believer as the one believing, but grammatical voice alone does not settle the ultimate source or enabling cause of faith.
- **πᾶν ὃ δίδωσίν μοι ὁ πατήρ (*pan ho didōsin moi ho patēr*)**, “All that the Father giveth me” (v. 37): the neuter singular expression refers collectively to all that the Father gives to the Son. **δίδωσιν (*didōsin*)** is present active indicative, third-person singular, from δίδωμι (*didōmi*), “give.” The Father is the grammatical subject and the Son the recipient.
- **ἥξει (*hēxei*)**, “shall come” (v. 37): future active indicative, third-person singular, from ἥκω (*hēkō*), “come/arrive.” Jesus states the coming of those given by the Father as a certainty in this declaration. The assurance is part of the sentence's force; it must not be weakened into a mere possibility.
- **οὐδεὶς δύναται ἐλθεῖν (*oudeis dynatai elthein*)**, “No man can come” (v. 44): οὐδείς means “no one”; δύναται is present middle/deponent indicative, third-person singular, from δύναμαι (*dynamai*), “be able”; ἐλθεῖν is an aorist active infinitive from ἔρχομαι. Jesus states inability, not merely that coming is difficult or uncommon.
- **ἐὰν μὴ ὁ πατὴρ ... ἑλκύσῃ αὐτόν (*ean mē ho patēr ... helkysē auton*)**, “except the Father ... draw him” (v. 44): ἑλκύσῃ is aorist active subjunctive, third-person singular, from ἑλκύω (*helkyō*), “draw/drag.” The Father is the subject and the person is the object. The verb's lexical range does not by itself prove that every use means irresistible force; John 12:32 is a necessary comparison. In John 6:44, however, the sentence explicitly makes the Father's drawing necessary for a person's coming.
- **διδακτοὶ θεοῦ (*didaktoi theou*)**, “taught of God” (v. 45): διδακτοί is a nominative plural adjective/participle meaning “taught,” with θεοῦ (“of God”) identifying the source or agent of the teaching. Jesus cites prophetic promise and applies it to hearing and learning from the Father.
- **ἀκούσας (*akousas*)**, “hath heard,” and **μαθὼν (*mathōn*)**, “hath learned” (v. 45): aorist active participles from ἀκούω (*akouō*), “hear,” and μανθάνω (*manthanō*), “learn.” Jesus connects hearing and learning from the Father with coming to Him. These are not detached human achievements in the sentence; they occur within the Father's promised teaching.
- **ζῳοποιεῖ (*zōopoiei*)**, “quickeneth” (v. 63): present active indicative, third-person singular, from ζωοποιέω (*zōopoieō*), “make alive/give life.” “The Spirit” is the grammatical subject. The text directly attributes life-giving to the Spirit.
- **ἡ σὰρξ οὐκ ὠφελεῖ οὐδέν (*hē sarx ouk ōphelei ouden*)**, “the flesh profiteth nothing” (v. 63): the flesh is the subject of the negated statement; the wording is emphatic. Its scope is governed by the immediate contrast with the Spirit who gives life and by Jesus' words, which are spirit and life.
- **οὐδεὶς δύναται ἐλθεῖν ... ἐὰν μὴ ᾖ δεδομένον αὐτῷ ἐκ τοῦ πατρός μου (*oudeis dynatai elthein ... ean mē ē dedomenon autō ek tou patros mou*)**, “no man can come ... except it were given unto him of my Father” (v. 65): the inability construction repeats. **δεδομένον (*dedomenon*)** is a perfect passive participle, neuter singular, from δίδωμι, used impersonally with ᾖ (“may be/is”) in the condition. The passive form presents the necessary grant as given to the person, with the Father named as its source.

The Greek therefore establishes the Father as giver and drawer, the Spirit as life-giver, and the human as the one who comes and believes. It does not permit us to erase the human response; neither does it permit us to make the human will the source of the Father's gift or the Spirit's quickening.

### G. GUA-W Triple Constraint disposition

- **Biblical compliance — PASS, within scope.** The entry preserves coming, believing, unbelief, the Father's giving and drawing, the Father's teaching, and the Spirit's quickening. It does not isolate verse 40 from verses 37 and 44–45, nor use verse 44 to erase the explicit call to believe.
- **Scientific coherence — PASS, within scope.** Greek syntax and morphology are used to identify grammatical subjects, objects, voice, and verbal forms. The lexical meaning of ἑλκύω is not exaggerated into a universal doctrine of irresistible force; John 12:32 is retained as a canonical control.
- **GUA-W consistency — PASS, within scope.** The Father gives and draws; the Spirit quickens; the person comes and believes. The human response is real but derivative within the passage's stated order of agency. Nothing here identifies the human will as Original Cause of spiritual life.

**Entry finding:** John 6:35–45, 63–65 explicitly presents coming and believing as real responses, while identifying the Father as the giver and drawer and the Spirit as the one who quickens. Jesus states that no one can come unless the Father draws him, explains the drawing in relation to hearing and learning from the Father, and repeats that coming must be given by the Father. The passage therefore directly contradicts the claim that a person's independent ability is the originating source of coming to Christ. At the same time, the lexical range of “draw,” the comparison with John 12:32, and the precise chronology of regeneration and faith require careful treatment. The text establishes divine necessity and initiative; it does not invite us to invent an order beyond what it states.

**Status:** PASS for the necessity of the Father's drawing/grant and the Spirit's life-giving work; PASS for the reality of coming and believing; PARTIAL for the precise scope/manner of drawing and the complete chronology of regeneration and faith; FAIL for using this passage to establish autonomous human will as the originating cause of coming, faith, or salvation.

### B. Argument commonly made

Romans 9:16 is often cited as a direct denial that human willing or effort is the originating cause of God's mercy: “it is not of him that willeth, nor of him that runneth, but of God that sheweth mercy.” Verses 11 and 18 are also central to arguments about election and divine hardening.

A common counterargument says Paul is discussing only historical or corporate roles—Israel's place in salvation history, the selection of peoples, or service in God's plan—and not the eternal salvation of individuals. Another argument treats Paul's potter-and-clay language as a statement about God's sovereign authority while insisting that the passage does not address human faith or regeneration.

The investigation must test both. Paul's immediate question concerns God's word and Israel, and his examples include historical peoples and rulers. Yet the passage also speaks of children of God, promise, mercy, wrath, destruction, vessels of mercy, glory, and those whom God calls. It cannot responsibly be reduced to a discussion of mere earthly usefulness. Conversely, it must not be made to answer every question about individual election, regeneration, or the order of salvation without reading Romans 9–11 as a whole.

### C. Immediate biblical context

Romans 9 begins with Paul's grief over his kinsmen according to the flesh (vv. 1–5). He lists Israel's privileges: adoption, glory, covenants, law, service of God, promises, fathers, and the coming of Christ according to the flesh. The question raised by this grief is whether God's word and promises have failed because many Israelites do not believe.

Verse 6 answers: God's word has not failed, because “they are not all Israel, which are of Israel.” Paul then uses the patriarchal history to distinguish physical descent from the children identified with promise. Isaac, not Ishmael, is named in the promise line (vv. 7–9). Paul next turns to Rebecca's twin sons, Jacob and Esau. Before they were born and before either had done good or evil, God's purpose according to election was declared to stand “not of works, but of him that calleth” (vv. 10–13).

Paul anticipates the objection that this makes God unrighteous (v. 14), rejects it, and cites God's declaration to Moses concerning mercy (v. 15; Exodus 33:19). Verse 16 draws the conclusion: mercy does not originate in the willing or running person, but in God who shows mercy.

Paul then cites Pharaoh and the purpose of God's power and name being declared (v. 17; Exodus 9:16). Verse 18 summarizes: God has mercy on whom He will and hardens whom He will. Paul anticipates a further objection: if God's will cannot be resisted, why does He still find fault? (v. 19). He answers with the creature/Creator distinction and the potter/clay image (vv. 20–21), then speaks of God's longsuffering toward vessels of wrath and His making known the riches of glory on vessels of mercy prepared beforehand for glory (vv. 22–23). Verse 24 identifies the called as including both Jews and Gentiles.

Romans 9 does not end at verse 24. Verses 25–33 continue with Hosea, Isaiah, the remnant, and Israel's stumbling over Christ. Romans 10 then speaks extensively of Israel's zeal, ignorance of God's righteousness, Christ, the word of faith, believing, confessing, hearing, and preaching. Romans 11 addresses the remnant, the hardening of Israel in part, the inclusion of Gentiles, and God's purpose toward Israel. Any account that quotes Romans 9 while ignoring Paul's continuation through chapters 10 and 11 is incomplete.

### D. Counter-texts and contextual qualifications

**Romans 10:9–17** explicitly speaks of confessing Jesus as Lord, believing that God raised Him from the dead, calling upon the Lord, hearing, and preaching. These are not to be erased by Romans 9. But Romans 10 does not state that autonomous human will is the originating cause of faith. Paul asks how people can believe in Him of whom they have not heard and how they can hear without a preacher (10:14), and then says faith comes by hearing, and hearing by the word of God (10:17). Romans 9 and 10 must be read together rather than pitted against one another.

**Romans 11:5–7** continues the remnant/election discussion and says the election obtained what Israel sought, while the rest were blinded. **Romans 11:25–32** speaks of partial hardening, Gentile inclusion, God's mercy, and disobedience. This broader argument confirms that Romans 9 is not an isolated philosophical excursus; Paul is explaining God's faithfulness and purpose in the history of Israel and the nations.

**Genesis 25:23** is the oracle concerning the two nations in Rebecca's womb: “The elder shall serve the younger.” Its original setting concerns two peoples as well as the twins who represent them. Romans 9 applies the oracle within Paul's argument about God's purpose according to election. The corporate/national dimension must be acknowledged; it does not by itself prove that Paul's argument has no bearing on the individuals named.

**Malachi 1:2–5** is the source of “Jacob have I loved, but Esau have I hated” (Romans 9:13). Malachi's immediate context contrasts Israel/Jacob with Edom/Esau and speaks of their historical destinies. Paul's use of the text belongs to his argument about election and promise. The language must not be detached from either Malachi's setting or Paul's application.

**Exodus 33:19** supplies the declaration about God's freedom to show mercy; **Exodus 9:16** supplies the Pharaoh citation. Exodus narrates both Pharaoh's hardening and the LORD's hardening of Pharaoh, with the narrative sequence and agency described in multiple ways. Romans 9:17–18 expressly attributes the hardening to God's sovereign action, but the full account should also be read alongside the Exodus narrative rather than flattened into a single isolated sentence.

**Romans 9:22–23** contains an asymmetry that must be preserved. Verse 23 says God “afore prepared” the vessels of mercy unto glory, using an active verb with God as subject. Verse 22 describes vessels of wrath as “fitted to destruction” with a perfect passive participle and does not name an agent in that clause. It is not sound exegesis to silently make the two clauses grammatically identical or to claim that the Greek explicitly says God prepared the vessels of wrath in precisely the same way He prepared the vessels of mercy. The passage does affirm God's endurance, wrath, power, and authority; the exact force and agency of “fitted” require care.

**Ephesians 2:1–10**, **John 6:35–45**, and **John 3:1–8** address spiritual death, divine quickening, drawing, and new birth directly. They are relevant canonical comparisons, but Romans 9 must first be allowed to make its own argument about promise, calling, mercy, election, and hardening.

### E. Original Greek findings

- **οὐχ οἷον δὲ ὅτι ἐκπέπτωκεν ὁ λόγος τοῦ θεοῦ (*ouch hoion de hoti ekpeptōken ho logos tou theou*)**, “Not as though the word of God hath taken none effect” (v. 6): Paul opens by denying that God's word has failed or fallen away. This states the problem he is answering; the passage is not introduced as an abstract treatise on free choice.
- **οὐ γὰρ πάντες οἱ ἐξ Ἰσραήλ, οὗτοι Ἰσραήλ (*ou gar pantes hoi ex Israēl, houtoi Israēl*)**, “they are not all Israel, which are of Israel” (v. 6): Paul distinguishes physical descent from the Israel defined in the promise argument. The precise relation between ethnic Israel and the people of promise is developed across Romans 9–11.
- **ἐπαγγελίας (*epangelias*)**, “promise” (vv. 8–9): Paul identifies the children of promise, not mere physical descent, as counted for the seed. The promise to Sarah concerning Isaac is the example.
- **κατ’ ἐκλογὴν πρόθεσις τοῦ θεοῦ (*kat’ eklogēn prothesis tou theou*)**, “the purpose of God according to election” (v. 11): ἐκλογή (*eklogē*) means election/choice; πρόθεσις (*prothesis*) means purpose/plan. The purpose is explicitly God's, and Paul says it stands before the twins have done good or evil, “not of works, but of him that calleth.”
- **τοῦ καλοῦντος (*tou kalountos*)**, “of him that calleth” (v. 11): present active participle, genitive masculine singular, from καλέω (*kaleō*). The caller is the one identified as the source in the contrast. Paul does not say the purpose stands because of the twins' prior willing or performance.
- **οὐκ ἐξ ἔργων (*ouk ex ergōn*)**, “not of works” (v. 11): the preposition ἐκ (*ek*) marks source or basis. In this immediate context the distinction is God's purpose according to election, not the works of the unborn children.
- **ἐλεήσω ὃν ἂν ἐλεῶ (*eleēsō hon an eleō*)**, “I will have mercy on whom I will have mercy” (v. 15): Paul cites God's own declaration to Moses. The repeated mercy language places the decision to show mercy with God.
- **τοῦ θέλοντος (*tou thelontos*)**, “of him that willeth” (v. 16): present active participle, genitive masculine singular, from θέλω (*thelō*), “will/wish/desire.” It represents the willing person in the source/basis contrast.
- **τοῦ τρέχοντος (*tou trechontos*)**, “of him that runneth” (v. 16): present active participle, genitive masculine singular, from τρέχω (*trechō*), “run.” It represents exertion or striving. The exact rhetorical range may include human effort generally; the contrast is with God's showing mercy.
- **τοῦ ἐλεῶντος θεοῦ (*tou eleōntos theou*)**, “of God that sheweth mercy” (v. 16): present active participle, genitive masculine singular, from ἐλεέω (*eleeō*), “show mercy.” God is the grammatical subject of the mercy action. Paul's sentence contrasts the willing and running person with God as the one who shows mercy.
- **σκληρύνει (*sklērynei*)**, “he hardeneth” (v. 18): present active indicative, third-person singular, from σκληρύνω (*sklērynō*), “harden.” God is the subject in Paul's statement. The text explicitly attributes hardening to God's action; the wider Exodus narrative must still be read for the full account of Pharaoh's own acts and the progressive hardening narrative.
- **τίς ἀνθέστηκεν τῷ βουλήματι αὐτοῦ (*tis anthestēken tō boulēmati autou*)**, “who hath resisted his will?” (v. 19): ἀνθέστηκεν is perfect active indicative from ἀνθίστημι (*anthistēmi*), “stand against/resist”; βούλημα (*boulēma*) denotes will, purpose, or intention. The objection is voiced by Paul's interlocutor; it must not be mistaken for Paul's unqualified endorsement of every premise in the objection.
- **πλάσμα (*plasma*) / πλάσαντι (*plasanti*)**, “thing formed” / “him that formed it” (v. 20): the vocabulary presents the Creator/creature relation. The formed thing does not occupy the Creator's position to call Him to account as though it possessed His authority.
- **ἐξουσίαν (*exousian*)**, “power” (v. 21): authority/right. The potter has authority over the clay to make vessels for differing uses. The image stresses the Creator's authority; its exact application must be read in the argument that follows.
- **κατηρτισμένα (*katērtismena*)**, “fitted” (v. 22): perfect passive participle, accusative neuter plural, from καταρτίζω (*katartizō*), “prepare, fit, make ready.” The grammatical form is passive and no agent is expressed in the clause. It should not be silently converted into the active statement “God prepared the vessels of wrath” as though that were the exact wording.
- **προητοίμασεν (*proētoimasen*)**, “he had afore prepared” (v. 23): aorist active indicative, third-person singular, from προετοιμάζω (*proetoimazō*). God is the expressed subject who beforehand prepares the vessels of mercy for glory. The difference in grammatical construction between verses 22 and 23 is material and must be preserved.
- **οὓς καὶ ἐκάλεσεν (*hous kai ekalesen*)**, “even us, whom he hath called” (v. 24): ἐκάλεσεν is aorist active indicative, third-person singular, from καλέω. Paul identifies the called as including people from Jews and Gentiles. This is part of the answer to the question of God's promise and the identity of His people.

The Greek strongly supports Paul's explicit contrast: the purpose according to election stands not on the works of the unborn children but on God who calls; mercy is not sourced in the willing or running person but in God who shows mercy. The grammar also requires restraint in verse 22: the passive participle “fitted” does not name its agent, unlike the active “he had afore prepared” in verse 23.

- **GUA-W consistency — PASS, within scope.** God is the one who calls and shows mercy; the creature is not the Author or originating source of the divine purpose. ## 14. Entry — HW-008: Ephesians 2:1–10 (2026-11-18)

### A. Exact KJV passage

> **Ephesians 2:1–10**
>
> 1 And you hath he quickened, who were dead in trespasses and sins;
>
> 2 Wherein in time past ye walked according to the course of this world, according to the prince of the power of the air, the spirit that now worketh in the children of disobedience:
>
> 3 Among whom also we all had our conversation in times past in the lusts of our flesh, fulfilling the desires of the flesh and of the mind; and were by nature the children of wrath, even as others.
>
> 4 But God, who is rich in mercy, for his great love wherewith he loved us,
>
> 5 Even when we were dead in sins, hath quickened us together with Christ, (by grace ye are saved;)
>
> 6 And hath raised us up together, and made us sit together in heavenly places in Christ Jesus:
>
> 7 That in the ages to come he might shew the exceeding riches of his grace in his kindness toward us through Christ Jesus.
>
> 8 For by grace are ye saved through faith; and that not of yourselves: it is the gift of God:
>
> 9 Not of works, lest any man should boast.
>
> 10 For we are his workmanship, created in Christ Jesus unto good works, which God hath before ordained that we should walk in them.

### B. Argument commonly made

This passage is used in more than one direction in arguments about the human will.

One argument emphasizes that people are “dead in trespasses and sins” (vv. 1, 5). It reasons that spiritual death entails inability to originate spiritual life, and that God's quickening must therefore precede any saving response. The passage certainly places the former condition and God's life-giving action in direct contrast; the further question of the precise relation and chronology of faith and quickening must still be examined from Paul's wording and the wider canon.

A second argument emphasizes “through faith” (v. 8) and says that faith is a human response; it then concludes that the individual independently originates the decisive act by which salvation occurs. The first observation is true: Paul names faith as the means through which salvation is received. The second conclusion does not follow automatically. The verse immediately says that salvation is “not of yourselves” and is “the gift of God,” while verse 9 excludes works as its source and grounds for boasting.

A third argument says that the words “and that not of yourselves: it is the gift of God” prove specifically that faith itself is the gift, while another says that the gift refers only to salvation and excludes faith. The Greek demonstrative and its antecedent must be examined before either conclusion is asserted.

This entry must preserve all the elements Paul actually states: the dead condition, real human conduct and desire, God's intervention, grace, faith, the exclusion of works, and the creation of a people in Christ for good works.

### C. Immediate biblical context

**Ephesians 1:15–23** supplies the preceding argument. Paul prays that believers may know the hope of God's calling, the riches of His inheritance, and the exceeding greatness of His power toward those who believe. He describes that power in relation to God's raising Christ from the dead, seating Him at His right hand, and placing all things under His feet. Ephesians 2 continues this resurrection and power language by describing the believers' being made alive, raised, and seated together with Christ. The connection is important: the life-giving action in chapter 2 belongs to the power and work of God already described in chapter 1.

**Ephesians 2:1–3** describes the former condition. The KJV's “hath he quickened” in verse 1 is supplied in italics; the Greek text begins “And you, being dead...” and does not express the finite verb “made alive” until verse 5. The object “you” is held in suspense through the description of the former condition, and the sentence reaches its main action in “God ... hath quickened us together with Christ” (v. 5). The KJV supply makes the connection understandable, but the original construction should not be overlooked.

Paul describes the former life as a walk according to the age/course of this world and according to the ruler of the authority of the air (v. 2). He then includes “we all” in the former conduct of lusts and desires (v. 3). The movement from “you” to “we all” prevents the writer and his wider audience from treating the former condition as a problem belonging only to some other group. The later context in verses 11–22 identifies the “you” in relation to Gentiles and describes Christ's reconciliation of both groups into one new humanity. The exact rhetorical scope of “we all” should be read in that Jew/Gentile context rather than used to erase the passage's movement between “you” and “we.”

**Ephesians 2:4–7** gives the decisive contrast: “But God.” God is rich in mercy; His great love is the stated ground of His action. Even while those addressed were dead in sins, God made them alive together with Christ, raised them together, and seated them together in heavenly places in Christ. The repeated “together with” language locates the believers' life and exalted status in union with Christ. The purpose stated in verse 7 is the display of the exceeding riches of God's grace in coming ages.

**Ephesians 2:8–10** explains salvation by grace through faith, excludes boasting and works as its source, and then identifies believers as God's workmanship, created in Christ Jesus for good works which God prepared beforehand. Works are not the originating ground of salvation; they are the stated purpose and walk of those created in Christ.

**Ephesians 2:11–22** continues the chapter by describing Gentiles formerly alienated from the commonwealth of Israel and strangers from the covenants of promise, brought near by the blood of Christ. Christ makes peace, reconciles both unto God in one body by the cross, and builds them together as a habitation of God through the Spirit. The salvation described in verses 1–10 is therefore not an isolated private transaction detached from Paul's wider account of reconciliation and the one people created in Christ.

### D. Counter-texts and contextual qualifications

**Ephesians 1:19–23** is the closest preceding context. The power toward believers is compared with the divine power exercised in raising Christ and seating Him above all principality and power. Chapter 2's made-alive, raised, and seated language continues that Christ-centered account. The text does not present spiritual life as an achievement independent of God's power.

**Ephesians 2:11–22** qualifies any reading that reduces the passage to a purely individual account. Paul describes the reconciliation of Jew and Gentile, the removal of enmity, and the creation of one new man in Christ. The individual believer's salvation belongs within this corporate reconciliation.

**Colossians 2:12–13** is a close Pauline comparison. Paul speaks of being buried with Christ in baptism, raised with Him through faith in the operation of God, and being quickened together with Him, having forgiven trespasses. This supports the connection between divine operation, union with Christ, faith, and life. It does not by itself settle every chronological question about regeneration and faith.

**Romans 6:1–11** describes believers as united with Christ in His death and resurrection and as walking in newness of life. It confirms that the new life is grounded in Christ's death and Resurrection and has consequences for conduct. It should not be used to erase Paul's own wording in Ephesians.

**Titus 3:3–7** describes the former condition as foolishness, disobedience, deception, and serving diverse lusts; then says that salvation is not by works of righteousness which we have done, but according to God's mercy, by the washing of regeneration and renewing of the Holy Ghost, whom God shed abundantly through Jesus Christ. This is a significant parallel for divine mercy and life-giving renewal. It requires its own full entry before its details are treated as settled.

**John 6:35–45, 63–65** remains relevant because Jesus speaks of the Father's giving, drawing, and teaching, the Spirit's quickening, and the inability of anyone to come unless it is given by the Father. HW-006 has already recorded those statements while leaving the complete order of regeneration and faith open.

**Philippians 1:29** says that unto believers it is given on behalf of Christ not only to believe on Him but also to suffer for His sake. This is relevant to whether faith may be spoken of elsewhere as granted by God. It should be examined in its own context and not used to make Ephesians 2:8 say more than its syntax establishes.

**Romans 10:9–17** explicitly speaks of believing, confessing, hearing, and preaching. These human responses remain real in Paul's account. Their presence does not, by itself, identify autonomous human will as their originating cause.

### E. Original Greek findings

- **ὄντας (*ontas*)**, “being” (v. 1): present active participle, accusative masculine plural, from εἰμί (*eimi*), “be.” It describes the state of those addressed. The finite verb “made alive” is not present in verse 1's Greek clause; the KJV supplies “hath he quickened” from the main verb in verse 5. The participial construction continues through the description of the former condition.
- **νεκρούς (*nekrous*)**, “dead” (vv. 1, 5): accusative masculine plural adjective, “dead.” The context identifies the sphere or condition as trespasses and sins. The word is used of spiritual death here, not physical cessation of bodily life. It must not be made to mean that the people described had no desires, actions, choices, or conduct: Paul expressly describes their walking, living, and fulfilling desires in verses 2–3.
- **παραπτώμασιν (*paraptōmasin*)**, “trespasses” (vv. 1, 5): dative plural of παράπτωμα (*paraptōma*), a false step, trespass, or transgression. **ἁμαρτίαις (*hamartiais*)**, “sins” (v. 1): dative plural of ἁμαρτία (*hamartia*), sin. The paired terms describe the moral/spiritual condition; no rigid distinction between two separate classes of sin should be imposed solely from the two nouns.
- **περιεπατήσατε (*periepatēsate*)**, “ye walked” (v. 2): aorist active indicative, second person plural, from περιπατέω (*peripateō*). In this context “walk” describes the former course or manner of life, not literal physical locomotion. The people are described as active participants in a pattern of conduct.
- **αἰῶνα (*aiōna*)**, “course” (v. 2): accusative singular of αἰών (*aiōn*), commonly “age.” The phrase describes the age/order of this world. The translation “course” communicates the prevailing order or pattern, but the underlying word should not be confused with κόσμος (*kosmos*), “world.”
- **ἄρχοντα (*archonta*)** and **ἐξουσίας (*exousias*)** (v. 2): “prince/ruler” and “authority/power.” Paul describes a hostile spiritual ruler and the sphere of authority associated with the air. The identity and scope of this ruler belong to the wider biblical teaching about Satan; the phrase must not be expanded into an independent power equal to God.
- **ἐνεργοῦντος (*energountos*)**, “worketh” (v. 2): present active participle, genitive masculine singular, from ἐνεργέω (*energeō*), “work/operate.” Paul describes an active operation in the children of disobedience. The phrase does not erase the human conduct also described in the passage.
- **ἀνεστράφημεν (*anestraphēmen*)**, “we had our conversation” (v. 3): aorist passive-form verb used intransitively, first person plural, from ἀναστρέφω (*anastrephō*), describing conduct or manner of life. **ἐπιθυμίαις (*epithymiais*)**, “lusts/desires,” identifies desires in which that former life was lived. **ποιοῦντες (*poiountes*)**, “fulfilling,” is a present active participle, nominative masculine plural, from ποιέω (*poieō*). The text expressly attributes active doing to the people described.
- **θελήματα (*thelēmata*)**, “desires” (v. 3): accusative plural of θέλημα (*thelēma*), will, desire, or what is willed. It is qualified by “of the flesh and of the mind.” This is not a technical definition of the whole human will, nor does the noun by itself establish autonomous authority or originating power. It does show that the former conduct involved the enactment of desires.
- **ἤμεθα (*ēmetha*)**, “we were” (v. 3): imperfect form of εἰμί, first person plural. **τέκνα φύσει ὀργῆς (*tekna physei orgēs*)**, “children of wrath by nature,” describes the former standing/condition. φύσει (*physei*) means “by nature” or “by disposition/constitution” according to context. The phrase is weighty, but this verse alone does not provide a complete philosophical account of inherited sin or every aspect of human nature.
- **ὁ δὲ θεός (*ho de theos*)**, “But God” (v. 4): the explicit subject of the contrast and the following saving actions. The contrast is not between human self-repair and divine assistance; it is between the former condition and God's mercy and love.
- **συνεζωοποίησεν (*synezōopoiēsen*)**, “hath quickened ... together with” (v. 5): aorist active indicative, third person singular, from συνζωοποιέω (*syzōopoieō*), “make alive together with.” God is the grammatical subject; the people are the recipients of the action. The σύν (*syn*) element joins the life-giving action to Christ. The aorist presents the action as a whole; it does not, by itself, supply a complete chronology of every element of salvation.
- **χάριτί (*chariti*)**, “by grace” (vv. 5, 8): dative singular of χάρις (*charis*), grace or favor. The phrase identifies grace as the basis or means in Paul's description of salvation, not a reward earned by the recipients.
- **ἐστε σεσῳσμένοι (*este sesōsmenoi*)**, “are ye saved / you have been saved” (vv. 5, 8): present indicative of εἰμί with a perfect passive participle of σῴζω (*sōzō*), “save/rescue/deliver.” The construction presents a completed saving action with a continuing resulting state. The passive form describes the recipients as those who have been saved; it does not name them as the originating savior.
- **συνήγειρεν (*synēgeiren*)** and **συνεκάθισεν (*synekathisen*)**, “raised us up together” and “made us sit together” (v. 6): aorist active indicative verbs with σύν (*syn*), “with/together.” God remains the subject. The believers' raised and seated status is “in Christ Jesus”; it is not an independent human achievement.
- **διὰ πίστεως (*dia pisteōs*)**, “through faith” (v. 8): διά (*dia*) with the genitive marks means or mediation; πίστις (*pistis*) means faith, trust, or believing. Faith is expressly named as the means through which salvation is received. The phrase must not be deleted or made unreal. Its presence does not, by itself, identify autonomous human will as the originating source of faith.
- **τοῦτο (*touto*)**, “that/this” (v. 8): nominative neuter singular demonstrative pronoun. χάρις (“grace”) and πίστις (“faith”) are feminine nouns. The neuter demonstrative is commonly understood as pointing to the preceding thought or event as a whole—salvation by grace through faith—rather than simply agreeing with the feminine noun “faith.” Grammatical gender is an important caution against the simplistic claim that the pronoun must refer exclusively to faith. It is not, by itself, sufficient to settle every theological question about whether faith is also given by God; that question requires the wider canonical evidence. The clause clearly denies that salvation is “of yourselves” and identifies it as God's gift.
- **οὐκ ἐξ ὑμῶν (*ouk ex hymōn*)**, “not of yourselves” (v. 8): the preposition ἐκ (*ek*) marks source or origin. Paul denies that the saving reality under discussion originates from the recipients themselves.
- **ἔργων (*ergōn*)**, “works” (v. 9): genitive plural of ἔργον (*ergon*), work or deed. The phrase “not of works” excludes works as the source or ground of salvation and excludes boasting. It does not say that good works have no place in the believer's life; verse 10 immediately states their place.
- **ποίημα (*poiēma*)**, “workmanship” (v. 10): nominative neuter singular of ποιέω (*poieō*), something made or created, workmanship. Paul identifies believers as God's handiwork.
- **κτισθέντες (*ktisthentes*)**, “created” (v. 10): aorist passive participle, nominative masculine plural, from κτίζω (*ktizō*), “create.” The passive form identifies believers as those created in Christ Jesus; God is the implied creator in the context.
- **προητοίμασεν (*proētoimasen*)**, “hath before ordained/prepared” (v. 10): aorist active indicative, third person singular, from προετοιμάζω (*proetoimazō*), “prepare beforehand.” God is the expressed subject who prepared the good works beforehand. The purpose is that believers should walk in them. This is not a statement that good works earn the creation in Christ; it places them as the intended walk of those created in Him.

- **Biblical compliance — PASS, within scope.** The entry preserves both the former human conduct and God's life-giving action. It does not erase faith, desire, works, or responsibility; it also does not make them the source of salvation. It reads verses 1–10 within Ephesians 1 and 2:11–22.

### H. Entry finding and limits

Ephesians 2:1–10 presents a former human condition of spiritual death in trespasses and sins, accompanied by real human conduct, disobedience, desire, and action. Paul does not portray the people as inactive objects; he describes what they walked in and what they did. Yet their activity is not identified as the source of spiritual life.

It also does not support the opposite overstatement that fallen people have no will, desire, action, or responsibility. The former condition includes active conduct; the new life is God's work in Christ.

## 14. Entry — HW-009: James 1:16–18 (2026-11-18)

### A. Exact KJV passage

> **James 1:16–18**
>
> 16 Do not err, my beloved brethren.
>
> 17 Every good gift and every perfect gift is from above, and cometh down from the Father of lights, with whom is no variableness, neither shadow of turning.
>
> 18 Of his own will begat he us with the word of truth, that we should be a kind of firstfruits of his creatures.

The immediate context must include James 1:13–15, where James denies that God tempts anyone with evil and describes the progression from a person's own lust, through enticement and conception, to sin and death. Verses 16–18 then direct the reader away from error and identify every good and perfect gift as coming from the Father of lights. Verse 18 names God's own will in the begetting of those addressed.

### B. Argument commonly made

James 1:18 is cited to establish that new birth originates in God's will rather than in an independent human decision. The wording directly supports God's will as the stated source or ground of the begetting: “Of his own will begat he us.” God is the grammatical subject of the active verb; “us” are those brought forth.

The verse also names “the word of truth” in connection with the begetting. Interpretations differ on whether ἐν (*en*) here is best rendered instrumentally (“by/through the word”), or describes the sphere or means in which the begetting occurs. The KJV's “with” should be preserved while the grammatical range is noted. The verse does not present God's will and the Word of Truth as competing explanations.

Some readings extend the verse to assert a complete chronology of regeneration before faith. This verse does not mention faith or state a detailed sequence between believing and begetting. It establishes God's own will as the source of the begetting and identifies the Word of Truth in connection with it. Further chronology must be tested against other passages rather than inserted into this clause.

Other readings treat the human reception or response to the Word as the originating cause of new birth. James 1:18 does not say that. It identifies God as the one who begat “us” and God's will as the stated ground. The verse does not, however, provide an exhaustive account of every human response involved in hearing or receiving the Word.

### C. Immediate context and argument

**James 1:13–15 — temptation and the origin of sin.** James expressly denies that God is the source of evil temptation. He describes each person as tempted when drawn away and enticed by “his own lust.” Lust conceives and brings forth sin; completed sin brings forth death. This is a moral progression in which human desire and conduct are expressly involved. It must not be converted into a claim that God authors sin.

**James 1:16 — warning against error.** “Do not err” links the warning to the preceding explanation and introduces the positive account of God's character and gifts. The reader must not attribute to God what James has just denied of Him.

**James 1:17 — the Father of lights.** Every good gift and every perfect gift comes from above, from the Father of lights. Unlike created lights that vary or cast changing shadows, God has no variableness or shadow of turning. The description grounds the contrast in God's consistent goodness. The verse does not describe God as the source of temptation; it identifies Him as the source of good and perfect gifts.

**James 1:18 — God's will and the Word of Truth.** “Of his own will” precedes and qualifies “begat he us.” God is the acting subject. The verb is active; those addressed are the recipients of the begetting. “With the word of truth” identifies the Word in connection with this act. The stated purpose or result is that the believers should be “a kind of firstfruits of his creatures.”

**James 1:19–27 — the response that follows.** The following context commands hearing, speaking, anger, receiving with meekness the engrafted word, and being doers of the word rather than hearers only. James therefore does not erase human response or obedience. He distinguishes the divine begetting in verse 18 from the conduct required of those who hear the Word in the following verses. The command to receive the Word in meekness (v. 21) should be read in its own place; it does not change the grammatical subject of “begat he us” in verse 18.

### D. Canonical comparisons and counter-texts

**John 1:12–13.** Those who receive Christ and believe on His name are given the right to become the sons of God (v. 12); verse 13 describes their birth as not of blood, the will of the flesh, or the will of man, but of God. This is a close comparison because both passages identify God as the source of birth. John 1 also expressly mentions believing, whereas James 1:18 does not. Their distinct statements must be preserved.

**Ephesians 2:1–10.** Paul describes those dead in trespasses as made alive together with Christ by God, saved by grace through faith, not from themselves or works, and created in Christ for good works prepared beforehand. This complements James's attribution of begetting to God's own will. Ephesians explicitly names faith; James 1:18 names the Word of Truth. Neither passage should be made to say the other's exact wording.

**1 Peter 1:3, 23–25.** Peter blesses God for having begotten believers again according to His abundant mercy through the resurrection of Jesus Christ (v. 3), and later says they are born again not of corruptible seed but incorruptible, by the Word of God which lives and abides forever (vv. 23–25). This is a strong comparison for divine mercy, new birth, and the enduring Word. The exact relation between the Word and the divine act should be examined in Peter's own construction.

**1 John 5:1.** “Whosoever believeth that Jesus is the Christ is born of God.” The perfect-form birth language and present believing require careful examination. This text is directly relevant to the relation between believing and having been born of God. It is retained for its own dedicated entry; it must not be settled by importing a chronology into James 1:18.

**Philippians 1:29.** Paul says that it is given to believers on behalf of Christ not only to believe on Him but also to suffer for His sake. This is relevant to the divine granting of believing, but the verse's context and the scope of “it is given” require separate examination. It does not add the word “faith” to James 1:18.

**John 3:1–8.** Jesus teaches that birth of the Spirit is necessary to see and enter the kingdom and that what is born of the Spirit is spirit. This provides a distinct teaching about the Spirit as the source of spiritual birth. James 1:18 identifies God's will and the Word of Truth; the passages may illuminate one another without being collapsed.

### E. Original-language findings

- **βουληθεὶς (*boulētheis*)**, “of his own will” (v. 18): aorist passive-form participle, nominative masculine singular, from βούλομαι (*boulomai*), “will, intend, wish, purpose.” The verb is deponent in this usage; the participle has active meaning, “having willed” or “because/when He willed,” according to how the participial relation is expressed in translation. It agrees with the implied subject, God. The phrase identifies God's willing; it does not name a human will as the source of the begetting.
- **ἀπεκύησεν (*apekyēsen*)**, “begat” (v. 18): aorist active indicative, third person singular, from ἀποκυέω (*apokyeō*), “bring forth” or “give birth.” God is the grammatical subject. The verb is used in James 1:15 of lust bringing forth sin and in 1:18 of God bringing forth “us.” The shared birth imagery creates a literary contrast between the progression from lust to sin/death and God's begetting of believers. It does not make the two sources morally or causally equivalent.
- **ἡμᾶς (*hēmas*)**, “us” (v. 18): accusative first-person plural pronoun, the object of the active verb. Those addressed are recipients of God's begetting.
- **ἐν λόγῳ ἀληθείας (*en logō alētheias*)**, “with the word of truth” (v. 18): ἐν (*en*) with the dative may express sphere, means, or instrument, depending on context. λόγῳ (*logō*) is dative singular of λόγος (*logos*), “word/message”; ἀληθείας (*alētheias*) is genitive singular of ἀλήθεια (*alētheia*), “truth.” The Word of Truth is expressly connected with the begetting. The precise nuance of ἐν should not be overstated; “with” is the KJV rendering, while “by/through” is a possible instrumental interpretation.
- **εἰς τὸ εἶναι (*eis to einai*)**, “that we should be” (v. 18): εἰς with the articular infinitive expresses purpose or intended result. The phrase introduces the stated end of the begetting.
- **ἀπαρχήν (*aparchēn*)**, “firstfruits” (v. 18): accusative singular of ἀπαρχή (*aparchē*), first portion or firstfruits, often carrying the idea of an initial portion set apart and representative of a larger whole. “A kind of” reflects the wording of the KJV and should not be dropped when discussing the phrase.
- **κτισμάτων (*ktismatōn*)**, “creatures” (v. 18): genitive plural of κτίσμα (*ktisma*), that which is created. The phrase places the recipients in relation to God's created order. The exact scope of “his creatures” and the force of “firstfruits” should be read in the wider argument rather than assigned an unsupported timetable.
- **πατρὸς τῶν φώτων (*patros tōn phōtōn*)**, “Father of lights” (v. 17): the genitive plural “of lights” is associated with the Father. The image is commonly understood in relation to the heavenly lights, but the central point in this context is God's unchanging goodness in contrast with variation and shadow. Do not make the metaphor carry claims that James does not state.
- **παραλλαγή (*parallagē*)** and **τροπῆς ἀποσκίασμα (*tropēs aposkiasma*)** (v. 17): terms for variation/change and a shadow associated with turning or shifting. James contrasts the changing appearance of lights with God's constancy. The verse is a character statement about God, not a technical account of astronomy.

### G. Triple Constraint disposition

- **Biblical compliance — PASS, within scope.** The reading preserves James 1:13–18 as a connected argument: God is not the source of evil temptation; human lust is involved in the progression to sin; good gifts come from the Father; and God begets believers of His own will through/in connection with the Word of Truth. The commands and human response in verses 19–27 remain real.
- **Scientific coherence — PASS, within scope.** The grammatical observations distinguish the participle βουληθείς from the active finite verb ἀπεκύησεν, identify the grammatical subject and object, and preserve the range of ἐν. No chronology is inferred from tense alone.
- **GUA-W consistency — PASS, within scope.** God is identified as the source and acting subject of the begetting. The Word of Truth is connected with His action. The recipients do not author their own birth. No new architecture is proposed.

### H. Entry finding and limits

James 1:13–18 presents a clear contrast. The progression toward sin and death is associated with a person's own lust and the actions that follow. God, by contrast, is the unchanging Father of lights and the source of every good and perfect gift. James then states that God, “of his own will,” begat believers with the Word of Truth, with the intended result that they should be a kind of firstfruits of His creatures.

The text explicitly identifies God's will as the ground of the begetting and God as its acting subject. It connects the Word of Truth with that begetting. It does not mention faith in verse 18, define every aspect of human willing, or give a complete chronological account of regeneration and faith. The commands to hear, receive, and do the Word in the following verses demonstrate that divine begetting does not erase human response and obedience.

**Finding:** James 1:18 does not identify autonomous human will as the originating cause of new birth. It identifies God's own will as the stated source of the begetting and the Word of Truth as connected with that act. The precise instrumental nuance of ἐν and the full canonical chronology of faith and regeneration remain open.

**Status:** PASS for God's own will as the stated ground of begetting; PASS for God's agency and the connection with the Word of Truth; PARTIAL for the exact nuance of “with/by” and the scope of “firstfruits”; UNRESOLVED for the full chronology of new birth and faith and for an exhaustive definition of the human will.

### I. Related GUA-W recognition and cross-references

This passage witnesses a distinction between the source of an act and the means or word associated with it. God's own will is named; God performs the begetting; the Word of Truth is connected with the act; the recipients are brought forth and designated as firstfruits. This is a textual recognition only, not a proposal to create or modify GUA-W architecture.
