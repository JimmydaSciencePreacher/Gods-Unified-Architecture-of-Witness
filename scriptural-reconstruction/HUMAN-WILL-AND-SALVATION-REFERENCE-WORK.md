# The Biblical Examination of the Human Will

**GUA-W Scripture Reference Work**  
**Status:** ACTIVE — first entry recorded; full corpus not yet audited  
**Governing question:** Does Scripture ever identify fallen man's independent will as the originating cause of his spiritual birth, faith, or salvation?

## 1. Scriptural starting point and governing posture

**This study begins at Revelation 22:18 (KJV).** The starting text is the warning against adding to the words of the prophecy of this book. This governs how the investigation handles Scripture: we must not add meanings, premises, or conclusions that the text does not give.

> **Revelation 22:18**
>
> 18 For I testify unto every man that heareth the words of the prophecy of this book, If any man shall add unto these things, God shall add unto him the plagues that are written in this book:

The immediate context must be preserved. Revelation 22:18–19 gives the paired warning concerning adding to and taking away from the words of the book of this prophecy. The scope of “this book” must be handled carefully in its literary setting; the verse must not be casually expanded into a claim that it directly settles every interpretive dispute or every question about the canon. Its force here is methodological: the study must neither add to Scripture nor take from it.

This warning governs every entry that follows. We will not insert “Free Will,” autonomous spiritual ability, or any other theological premise into a passage unless the passage and its context establish it. Nor will we remove the words that challenge a preferred doctrine. Claims must be tested by the whole relevant testimony of Scripture, with distinctions kept clear and conclusions limited to what the evidence warrants.

**Governing posture:** This is a direct, unsparing examination of claims made in the name of Scripture. No doctrine receives protection because it is popular, traditional, comforting, or preached with confidence. Equally, no passage may be exaggerated to make a stronger claim than its words and context support. The force of the conclusion must come from the Word itself.

## 2. Purpose and scope


This is a canonical, text-first reference work examining passages used to claim that human Free Will is a gift from God, especially where the claim moves from ordinary human choice or responsibility to an assertion that fallen man's independent will originates spiritual birth, faith, or salvation.

The investigation is not a collection of isolated proof-texts. Each passage must be read in its immediate literary, historical, grammatical, and canonical context. Arguments made from a passage are to be represented fairly before they are tested. Counter-texts must likewise be read in context, not deployed as competing proof-texts.

The work does not assume that every use of will, choose, desire, receive, believe, obey, or come has the same meaning. Nor does it assume that demonstrating human choice establishes autonomous spiritual ability, or that responsibility by itself proves ability.

## 3. Order of distinction before scriptural interpretation

Before quoting or interpreting a passage about the human will, the categories involved must be distinguished. These are not interchangeable terms, and one must not be silently substituted for another:

1. **Choice** — selecting, deciding, or responding among alternatives.
2. **Responsibility** — being morally or covenantally accountable for an action or omission.
3. **Ability** — possessing the capacity to perform the particular action under examination.
4. **Willingness** — desire, intention, readiness, or volition concerning that action.
5. **Divine initiative** — what God originates or performs, including calling, giving, drawing, opening, quickening, and working.
6. **Regeneration / spiritual birth** — the giving or origin of spiritual life.
7. **Faith** — believing or trusting; distinguish the act, its object, its source, and its relation to spiritual birth.
8. **Salvation** — God's saving purpose and accomplishment, and the distinct matters of reception, application, perseverance, and final completion.

### The order-of-distinction safeguard

**Logical distinction is not necessarily chronological sequence.** Listing or defining these categories in an analytical order does not claim that they occur in that temporal or causal order. For example, distinguishing faith from regeneration does not, by itself, establish which precedes the other. That question must be investigated from the relevant passages, their grammar, and their canonical context.

Likewise:
- Choice does not, by itself, establish ability.
- Responsibility does not, by itself, establish ability.
- Willingness does not, by itself, establish independent originating power.
- The presence of faith does not, by itself, identify faith's source.
- A command does not, by itself, settle the question of the hearer's capacity.
- A divine act and a human response must not be collapsed into one category.

These distinctions are an interpretive control, not a conclusion imposed upon the text. Each passage may establish, qualify, or challenge a category. The investigation must report what it actually says and leave unresolved what it does not settle.

## 4. Mandatory record for every passage entry

Every full entry must preserve these fields:

1. **Exact KJV passage** — quote the complete passage under examination, not merely a phrase detached from its sentence.
2. **Claim made from the passage** — state the strongest fair version of the argument used to support Free Will, including the inference being drawn.
3. **Immediate biblical context** — examine the surrounding verses, speaker, audience, literary unit, occasion, and argument.
4. **Counter-texts and their contexts** — identify relevant passages that qualify, challenge, or clarify the claim; read each in its own context.
5. **Original-language findings** — Hebrew, Aramaic, or Greek only where relevant; include lemma, morphology/syntax where material, semantic range, and limits. A Strong's gloss alone is not an argument.
6. **What the passage establishes** — classify the evidence separately for choice, responsibility, ability, willingness, divine initiative, regeneration/spiritual birth, faith, salvation, or another clearly named category.
7. **GUA-W Triple Constraint disposition** — separately assess Biblical compliance, Scientific coherence (using the appropriate methods of theological, linguistic, historical, or other relevant inquiry), and GUA-W consistency.
8. **Finding and limits** — distinguish explicit text, supported inference, interpretation, analogy, and unresolved matters. Record a status: PASS, PARTIAL, FAIL, UNRESOLVED, INTERPRETATION, or NOT YET INVESTIGATED.

No passage may be made to answer a question it does not address. A passage that establishes choice must not automatically be cited as proof of autonomous spiritual ability. A passage that establishes responsibility must not automatically be treated as proof of ability. A passage that describes belief must not automatically be treated as identifying the originating cause of regeneration.

## 5. Governing distinctions

- **Choice:** selection, decision, or response among alternatives.
- **Responsibility:** moral or covenantal accountability for an action or omission.
- **Ability:** capacity to perform the action in question; the domain and conditions of that capacity must be specified.
- **Willingness:** desire, intention, readiness, or volition.
- **Divine initiative:** an act, call, gift, drawing, giving, opening, quickening, or work attributed to God.
- **Spiritual birth/regeneration:** the origin of spiritual life, distinguished from its signs, effects, and accompanying responses.
- **Faith:** believing/trusting; distinguish its presence, object, expression, and source.
- **Salvation:** distinguish God's saving purpose and accomplishment from reception, application, perseverance, and final completion.

The term **independent will** is used here in a carefully limited sense: a human will alleged to originate spiritual birth, faith, or salvation independently of God's prior saving initiative. It does not mean that a person has no will, makes no choices, or bears no responsibility.

## 6. Triple Constraint application

Each entry is tested under all three constraints:

1. **Biblical compliance:** Does the claim preserve the exact KJV wording, the passage's context, and the testimony of Scripture as a whole, including the project's Genesis 1–2 foundation?
2. **Scientific coherence:** Are linguistic, grammatical, historical, and theological claims supported by appropriate evidence and methods? Are lexical possibilities distinguished from contextual meaning? Are claims no stronger than the evidence?
3. **GUA-W consistency:** Does the interpretation preserve Author → Word → Creation → Witness, God alone as Original Cause, and the distinction between divine initiative and derivative human response?

Failure under any one constraint prevents admission of a claim as an accepted GUA-W conclusion. A disputed reading may remain recorded as a competing interpretation or unresolved question.

## 7. Initial entry — HW-001: John 1:12–13

### A. Exact KJV passage

> **John 1:12–13**
>
> 12 But as many as received him, to them gave he power to become the sons of God, even to them that believe on his name:
>
> 13 Which were born, not of blood, nor of the will of the flesh, nor of the will of man, but of God.

### B. Argument commonly made

Verse 12 says that people received Christ and believed on His name. A common argument reasons that these are voluntary human acts; therefore, an independently originating human Free Will is the decisive cause of becoming God's child.

This argument must be represented carefully: the text does place receiving and believing in the description of those given authority/power to become the sons of God. The question is whether that response is thereby identified as the originating cause of the birth.

### C. Immediate context

John 1:1–14 opens with the Word's pre-existence, relation to God, and divine identity; all things are made by Him (vv. 1–3). John then introduces the witness of John the Baptist (vv. 6–8), the true Light coming into the world (vv. 9–11), the rejection by His own, and the reception by some (vv. 11–13). Verse 14 identifies the Word made flesh.

Verses 12 and 13 must be read together. Verse 12 describes receiving and believing and the authority/power given to those who believe. Verse 13 then describes the birth of those people and expressly identifies what that birth is not from and what it is from.

### D. Counter-text within the same immediate unit

John 1:13 is not a remote counter-text. It is the next sentence in the same unit. The KJV says those born were not born:
- of blood;
- of the will of the flesh;
- of the will of man;
- but of God.

The wording directly prevents the reader from assigning the originating source of this birth to human will. It does not erase the receiving and believing in verse 12. It distinguishes the response described in verse 12 from the source of the birth described in verse 13.

Further canonical counter-texts and corroborating passages to be examined in their own contexts include John 3:3–8; John 6:35–45, 63–65; Romans 9:6–24; Ephesians 2:1–10; James 1:16–18; 1 Peter 1:3, 23; and 1 John 5:1. These references are a research queue, not conclusions imported into this entry.

### E. Original Greek findings

Key expressions in the Greek text:

- **ἔλαβον (*elabon*)**, “received” — the verb used in John 1:12.
- **πιστεύουσιν (*pisteuousin*)**, “believe” — present active participle, describing those believing in His name.
- **ἐξουσία (*exousia*)**, “authority/right” — the noun translated “power” in the KJV in verse 12; it should not be confused automatically with inherent autonomous ability.
- **ἐγεννήθησαν (*egennēthēsan*)**, “were born” — aorist passive indicative, third person plural, in verse 13.
- **θελήματος (*thelēmatos*)**, “will/desire/intention” — genitive singular of θέλημα, occurring in the phrases “will of the flesh” and “will of man.”
- **ἐκ θεοῦ (*ek theou*)**, “of/from God” — identifies the source in the final clause of verse 13.

The passive form “were born” and the contrastive source phrase “but of God” are material. The syntax does not say that human beings cause themselves to be born of God. The verse explicitly negates human-will origin and affirms God as source.

Lexical caution: θέλημα can denote will, desire, or intention according to context. The conclusion here does not rest on an overextended dictionary definition; it rests on the sentence's explicit contrast and source construction.

### F. What the passage establishes

| Category | Finding | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| Choice/response | ESTABLISHED | Receiving Christ and believing on His name are stated in v. 12. |
| Responsibility | NOT SETTLED BY THIS TEXT ALONE | The passage is not a full treatment of moral accountability. |
| Human ability | NOT ESTABLISHED AS AUTONOMOUS SPIRITUAL ABILITY | Receiving/believing are described, but the text does not call independent human ability the source of birth. |
| Willingness | PRESENT BY IMPLICATION OF THE RESPONSE, NOT DEFINED AS ORIGIN | The text does not give a theory of human volition. |
| Divine initiative/source | ESTABLISHED FOR THE BIRTH | Those born are born “of God” (v. 13). |
| Spiritual birth | ESTABLISHED | The passage explicitly speaks of being born. |
| Human will as originating cause of spiritual birth | EXCLUDED BY THE TEXT'S WORDING | “Not ... of the will of the flesh, nor of the will of man, but of God.” |
| Faith's originating cause | NOT FULLY SETTLED BY THIS PASSAGE ALONE | Faith is named in v. 12; the precise causal relation between faith and regeneration must be examined across the canon. |
| Salvation as a whole | NOT EXHAUSTIVELY DEFINED HERE | The passage contributes to the question but does not alone describe every aspect of salvation. |

### G. GUA-W Triple Constraint disposition

- **Biblical compliance — PASS, within scope.** The claim that verse 12 describes receiving and believing is true. The stronger claim that human will originates the birth conflicts with verse 13.
- **Scientific coherence — PASS, within scope.** The grammatical and lexical observations above are limited to what the Greek construction supports. The passive birth verb and explicit source contrast are not being made to prove more than the sentence says.
- **GUA-W consistency — PASS, within scope.** The passage identifies God as the source of the birth and does not assign Original Cause to a derivative human response.

**Entry finding:** John 1:12–13 establishes receiving and believing as responses associated with those given authority to become God's children, while expressly identifying God—not the will of the flesh or the will of man—as the source of their birth. It does not establish fallen man's independent will as the originating cause of spiritual birth. It does not, by itself, settle every question concerning the order or causal relation of faith and regeneration.

**Status:** PASS for the narrow finding concerning the source of birth; PARTIAL for the wider question concerning the origin of faith and the complete order of salvation.

## 8. Entry — HW-002: Deuteronomy 30:11–20

### A. Exact KJV passage

> **Deuteronomy 30:11–20**
>
> 11 For this commandment which I command thee this day, it is not hidden from thee, neither is it far off.
>
> 12 It is not in heaven, that thou shouldest say, Who shall go up for us to heaven, and bring it unto us, that we may hear it, and do it?
>
> 13 Neither is it beyond the sea, that thou shouldest say, Who shall go over the sea for us, and bring it unto us, that we may hear it, and do it?
>
> 14 But the word is very nigh unto thee, in thy mouth, and in thy heart, that thou mayest do it.
>
> 15 See, I have set before thee this day life and good, and death and evil;
>
> 16 In that I command thee this day to love the LORD thy God, to walk in his ways, and to keep his commandments and his statutes and his judgments, that thou mayest live and multiply: and the LORD thy God shall bless thee in the land whither thou goest to possess it.
>
> 17 But if thine heart turn away, so that thou wilt not hear, but shalt be drawn away, and worship other gods, and serve them;
>
> 18 I denounce unto you this day, that ye shall surely perish, and that ye shall not prolong your days upon the land, whither thou passest over Jordan to go to possess it.
>
> 19 I call heaven and earth to record this day against you, that I have set before you life and death, blessing and cursing: therefore choose life, that both thou and thy seed may live:
>
> 20 That thou mayest love the LORD thy God, and that thou mayest obey his voice, and that thou mayest cleave unto him: for he is thy life, and the length of thy days: that thou mayest dwell in the land which the LORD sware unto thy fathers, to Abraham, to Isaac, and to Jacob, to give them.

### B. Argument commonly made

The argument is that Moses commands Israel to choose life (v. 19); therefore, human beings possess Free Will as a God-given, independent power to choose spiritual life and salvation. The command is sometimes treated as proof that the hearer has an autonomous ability to originate the spiritual response God requires.

The first part of the argument is accurate: Israel is commanded to choose. The further inference must be tested. The passage must identify the kind of life being set before Israel, the covenant setting of the command, and what the surrounding text says about the heart and God's own work.

### C. Immediate biblical context

Deuteronomy 30 does not begin at verse 11. Verses 1–10 describe Israel's future after the blessings and curses have come upon them and they return unto the LORD. The LORD promises to gather the dispersed people, bring them back to the land, and do them good. Crucially, verse 6 states:

> **Deuteronomy 30:6**
>
> And the LORD thy God will circumcise thine heart, and the heart of thy seed, to love the LORD thy God with all thine heart, and with all thy soul, that thou mayest live.

The text assigns the heart-circumcising action to the LORD. Its stated purpose is that Israel love the LORD with the whole heart and soul, unto life. Verse 8 then says, “And thou shalt return and obey the voice of the LORD, and do all his commandments.” The passage therefore places God's promised action toward the heart in the same restoration context as Israel's return and obedience.

Verses 11–14 explain that the commandment is not inaccessible or hidden. It is near, in Israel's mouth and heart, so that it may be done. Verses 15–20 set out the covenant alternatives: life and good, or death and evil; love, obedience, and cleaving to the LORD, or a heart turning away, refusal to hear, and idolatry. The stated consequence is explicitly tied to Israel's life in the land promised to the fathers (vv. 16, 18, 20).

The chapter's immediate subject is Israel's covenant relationship with the LORD, including restoration, obedience, blessing, curse, and life in the land. The word “life” must first be read in this covenant setting. The passage cannot simply be detached from that setting and treated as a direct technical definition of the origin of an individual's regeneration or eternal salvation.

### D. Counter-texts and contextual qualifications

**Deuteronomy 30:6** is the primary qualification within the same chapter. Israel is commanded to love and obey, but the LORD says that He will circumcise the heart so that Israel will love Him with all the heart and soul. This is an explicit divine action directed toward the heart and ordered toward the love commanded in verses 16 and 20.

This does not make the command to choose in verse 19 unreal, nor does it say that Israel does not make choices or bear covenant responsibility. It does prevent verse 19 from being isolated as though the chapter contained only an unaided human choice and no divine work upon the heart.

**Deuteronomy 10:12–16** supplies earlier covenant context: Israel is called to fear, walk, love, serve, and keep the LORD's commandments; verse 16 commands circumcision of the heart. Read with Deuteronomy 30:6, the heart is not treated as a merely external decision-point.

**Romans 10:6–10** quotes and applies language from Deuteronomy 30:12–14 to the word of faith preached concerning Christ. This is an important canonical development, but Paul's use must be examined in its own argument. It does not authorize silently replacing the original covenant setting of Deuteronomy 30:11–20 with a later doctrinal formula. Romans 10 deserves its own full entry before conclusions are drawn from the relationship.

Other relevant texts for later comparison include Jeremiah 31:31–34 and Ezekiel 36:24–28, where covenant renewal, a new heart, and God's action are described. They are corroborating research references, not substitutes for reading Deuteronomy in its own context.

### E. Original Hebrew findings

- **הַמִּצְוָה (*hammiṣwāh*)**, “the commandment” (v. 11): the definite noun identifies the command/instruction under discussion. The following clause says it is not hidden or far away.
- **נִפְלֵאת (*niphlēṯ*)**, from פלא (*p-l-ʾ*) (v. 11): a Niphal form describing something extraordinary, difficult, or beyond reach. In this context the point is that the command is not inaccessible or beyond Israel's reach; it is not a declaration that every inward spiritual capacity question has been settled.
- **בָּחַר (*bāḥar*)**, “choose” (v. 19): the verb is used in the exhortation commonly rendered “choose life.” It establishes a real covenant summons to choose between the alternatives Moses has set before Israel. The verb itself does not encode a philosophical doctrine of autonomous Free Will, nor does its occurrence alone define the source of spiritual regeneration.
- **לֵבָב (*lēbāb*)**, “heart” (vv. 6, 14, 17): the heart is the inward center of thought, disposition, and will in Hebrew usage. Verse 6 makes it the direct object of the LORD's promised circumcising action; verse 17 describes the heart turning away.
- **וּמָל (*ūmāl*)**, from מול (*m-w-l*) (v. 6): “and [He] will circumcise.” The subject is “the LORD thy God”; the heart is the object. The clause continues with **לְאַהֲבָה (*ləʾahăḇāh*)**, “to love,” expressing the purpose/result of this heart-circumcision. The grammatical subject of the circumcising action is God, not Israel.

The Hebrew findings support the plain contrast in the chapter: Israel is addressed with a genuine command and responsibility, while the LORD is identified as the one who will circumcise the heart in the restoration promise. Lexical data alone does not settle every theological question about the relationship between command, ability, divine action, and response.

### F. What the passage establishes

| Category | Finding | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| Choice/response | ESTABLISHED | Israel is commanded to choose life (v. 19). |
| Covenant responsibility | ESTABLISHED | Israel is warned of the consequences of turning away, refusing to hear, and serving other gods (vv. 17–18). |
| Human ability | PARTIAL / NOT FULLY DEFINED | The command is described as near and not inaccessible (vv. 11–14); this does not, by itself, define autonomous ability to regenerate or save oneself. |
| Willingness/disposition | ADDRESSED | The heart may turn away (v. 17); the LORD promises to circumcise the heart so that Israel will love Him (v. 6). |
| Divine initiative | ESTABLISHED IN THE RESTORATION PROMISE | The LORD gathers, brings, prospers, and circumcises the heart (vv. 3–6, 9). |
| Spiritual regeneration in the later technical sense | NOT SETTLED BY THIS PASSAGE ALONE | Heart-circumcision is explicit, but the passage is framed in Israel's covenant restoration and land life. Later canonical connections require separate examination. |
| Human will as the originating cause of spiritual birth | NOT ESTABLISHED | The text commands covenant choice but does not identify autonomous human will as the source of spiritual birth. |
| Salvation as a whole | NOT EXHAUSTIVELY DEFINED HERE | The immediate subject is covenant restoration, obedience, blessing, curse, and life in the promised land. |

### G. GUA-W Triple Constraint disposition

- **Biblical compliance — PASS, within scope.** The command to choose life must be acknowledged. So must the chapter's preceding promise that the LORD will circumcise the heart. Neither may be removed.
- **Scientific coherence — PASS, within scope.** The Hebrew observations distinguish the command to choose from the divine action upon the heart. The covenant and land context is retained. No philosophical conclusion is smuggled into the verb “choose.”
- **GUA-W consistency — PASS, within scope.** The passage presents human covenant response and responsibility while attributing the promised heart-circumcision to the LORD. It does not make the derivative human response the Original Cause of God's work.

**Entry finding:** Deuteronomy 30:19 undeniably commands Israel to choose life. It establishes covenant choice and responsibility. It does not, by that command alone, establish that fallen man's independent will is a God-given autonomous power that originates spiritual birth or salvation. The immediate chapter also declares that the LORD will circumcise Israel's heart so that Israel will love Him (v. 6). The command and the divine heart-work must be read together.

**Status:** PASS for the narrow finding that the passage commands covenant choice; FAIL for the claim that the command itself proves autonomous human will as the originating cause of spiritual birth or salvation; PARTIAL for broader questions concerning ability and the canonical relation between covenant heart-circumcision and regeneration.
