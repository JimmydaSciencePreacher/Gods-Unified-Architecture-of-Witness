

## 32. Foundational synthesis — Genesis 1–3 and the human will (U-006) — 2026-11-18

### A. Return to the Beginning

**The Answer precedes the question. The question exists to find the Answer already given in the Beginning.** This study therefore returns first to Genesis 1–2, with particular attention to Genesis 2:1–5, before asking what Genesis 3 reveals about human willing. The question is derivative; it is not the starting authority.

Genesis 2:1–3 records the finished heavens, earth, and host; God's ending and rest; and the blessing and sanctification of the seventh day. Genesis 2:4–6 introduces the generations of heaven and earth and the stated field-growth, rain, tilling, and watering conditions. These verses establish the Creator's prior work and the conditions preceding man's formation. They must not be made to say that no vegetation of any kind existed anywhere.

The direct human command and boundary are stated in Genesis 2:16–17: broad permission is given, one tree is prohibited, and a consequence is declared. Genesis 2:7 attributes man's formation and life to God; Genesis 2:15 gives man an appointed task within the Garden God planted and into which God placed him. Thus the human will is examined within an order, life, purpose, and command that precede the human response.

### B. The will in operation in Genesis 3

Genesis 3 does not offer an abstract philosophical definition of “will.” It does provide a narrated account of human willing in operation. The text distinguishes:
- receiving and responding to competing communication (3:1–5);
- reporting the prior command, with a difference between the woman's report and the recorded command (3:2–3; compare 2:16–17);
- seeing and perceiving (3:6);
- evaluating the tree as good for food, pleasant to the eyes, and desirable for wisdom (3:6);
- desire/desirability (3:6);
- taking, eating, giving, and the man's eating (3:6);
- heeding another's voice, expressly identified in Adam's judgment (3:17);
- changed awareness, covering, hiding, fear, and answers to God's questioning (3:7–13).

These operations must remain distinguishable. Perception is not evaluation; evaluation is not desire; desire is not action; hearing is not necessarily heeding; and action is not authority.

### C. Capacity is not authorship or authority

The humans' actions are real and accountable; the narrative does not portray them as physically compelled. Yet the text does not make the human will the author of Creation, the source of human life, the giver of the command, or the authority that establishes the governing standard. Genesis 3:22 acknowledges changed knowledge of good and evil; it does not state that God's authority was transferred. This agrees with the already-resolved U-007 distinction between knowledge/evaluation and authority to establish the standard.

### D. What the beginning account attributes to God

God creates (Genesis 1:1); completes, rests, blesses, and sanctifies (2:1–3); forms man and gives the breath of life (2:7); plants and places (2:8, 15); appoints man's work (2:15); grants permission and gives the prohibition (2:16–17); questions and judges (3:9–19); and guards access to the tree of life (3:22–24). The human will operates within a reality it did not originate.

### E. U-006 disposition

**Revised status: NARROWED — FOUNDATIONAL ANSWER ESTABLISHED IN GENESIS 2–3; NO EXHAUSTIVE ABSTRACT DEFINITION CLAIMED.**

The beginning account establishes the principal operations of human willing in the Fall narrative: reception/heeding, perception, evaluation, desire, choice/action, and response. It establishes the distinction between creaturely capacity and the Author's originating work and authority. The question is no longer properly described as wholly unanswered. What remains is a bounded question about whether later canonical passages add further distinctions or clarify specific terms—not whether the Answer must be invented from a later philosophical framework.

The existing Genesis 3 reconstruction and HW-004 are the authoritative records for this passage. No duplicate Genesis investigation or new HW number is created. Later Scripture is to be brought in as witness and canonical clarification after the beginning has been examined, not as a replacement starting point.

**Methodological guardrail:** An unresolved label describes the current state of the written examination; it must never imply that the Answer was absent from Scripture. Return to Genesis 1–2 before retaining such a label, then examine the relevant beginning-account event, and only afterward compare later canonical testimony.


## 33. Genesis 3:8–24 — the human response and God's governing action

The Genesis 3 examination is extended through the chapter's conclusion. Verses 8–13 show the man and woman hearing God, hiding, fearing, answering, and attributing responsibility. Adam refers to the woman and to God's giving; the woman refers to the serpent's beguiling. Neither answer changes the fact of the recorded eating. The narrative preserves the reality of deception and the reality of human participation without collapsing either into the other.

Verses 14–19 record differentiated divine judgments: the serpent is cursed and enmity declared; the woman is addressed concerning sorrow/conception, childbirth, and her relation to her husband; Adam is addressed concerning his hearkening and eating, the cursed ground, labor, and return to dust. These are God's judgments, not consequences whose standard is authored by the human will. The passage must not be used to claim that every later instance of suffering or conflict maps directly to a specific personal sin.

Verses 20–21 record Adam naming his wife Eve and the LORD God making and providing coats of skins. The text does not explicitly call the clothing event a sacrifice or supply a ritual explanation; do not state either as though Genesis 3:21 says it.

Verses 22–24 record God's stated concern about the man taking from the tree of life and living forever, God's sending the man from the Garden to till the ground, and God's guarding the way to the tree of life with cherubims and a flaming sword. The human does not secure access by independent will; God governs the boundary.

**Integrated result:** Genesis 3:1–24 presents human willing as real, multi-faceted, and accountable, but not sovereign or originating. It includes reception of speech, perception, evaluation, desire, heeding, action, fear, concealment, response, and attribution. It presents God as the One who commands, calls, questions, judges, provides, expels, and guards. The Creator/creature distinction remains intact through the chapter's final verse.

**U-006 refinement:** The beginning account gives a substantial narrative answer about the operations and limits of human willing. It does not give an exhaustive abstract taxonomy of every faculty or settle every later doctrinal question concerning saving faith. Those questions must return to the Beginning first, then be compared with their specific canonical witnesses. Genesis 3 must not be treated as silent merely because it is narrative rather than a formal definition.


## 34. Genesis 3:15 and the canonical witness to the promised victory

The Genesis 3 account's declaration in verse 15 must be read before later theological labels are applied. God says, “I will put enmity” between the serpent and the woman and between their respective seed/offspring. The verse announces divine initiative, conflict, and the bruising of the serpent's head and the woman's seed's heel. It does not name Jesus or narrate the later victory in detail.

The canonical witnesses clarify the larger promise:
- Revelation 12:9 identifies the dragon as the old serpent, the Devil, and Satan; Revelation 12:1–17 portrays the woman, her male child, and her remaining offspring in conflict with the dragon.
- Romans 16:20 declares that “the God of peace shall bruise Satan under your feet shortly,” strongly echoing Genesis 3:15 while keeping God as the acting subject.
- Hebrews 2:14–15 says Christ took part in flesh and blood and through death defeated the devil's power and delivered those held in bondage through fear of death.
- 1 John 3:8 says the Son of God was manifested to destroy the works of the devil.
- Colossians 2:14–15 places Christ's triumph over hostile powers in the Cross.
- Romans 5:12–21 and 1 Corinthians 15:21–22, 45–49 establish the Adam/Christ contrast and Christ as the last Adam.
- Galatians 3:16 identifies Christ as the Seed in the Abrahamic promise. It must not be represented as a direct grammatical exposition of Genesis 3:15.

**Finding:** The whole New Testament gives a Christ-centered account of God's victory over Satan through the Son's coming, death, and resurrection. Christian canonical theology therefore rightly reads Genesis 3:15 as the first announcement of the conflict culminating in Christ's victory. The precise scope of *zeraʿ* in Genesis 3:15 and the symbolism of Revelation 12's woman remain interpretive matters; they do not erase the explicit canonical identification of the dragon as Satan or the explicit New Testament testimony concerning Christ's victory.
