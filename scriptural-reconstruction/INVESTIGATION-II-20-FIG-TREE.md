# Investigation II.20 — The Fig Tree
**Primary texts:** Matthew 21:18–22; Mark 11:12–14, 20–25 (KJV)  
**Related texts:** Jeremiah 8:13; Hosea 9:10, 16; Micah 7:1; Luke 13:6–9; John 15:1–8  
**Status:** Comparative textual study recorded; symbolic interpretation must be distinguished from explicit narrative detail.

## Question
What do Matthew and Mark report when Jesus speaks to the fig tree, and how does Mark's placement of the event around the temple cleansing affect interpretation?

## Narrative sequence
- Matthew records Jesus returning to the city in the morning and becoming hungry.
- He sees a fig tree by the way, comes to it, and finds nothing but leaves.
- Jesus says, “Let no fruit grow on thee henceforward for ever,” and the tree withers away.
- Mark narrates the event in two stages: Jesus approaches a leafy tree and finds no fruit, “for the time of figs was not yet.” He speaks to it; the disciples hear.
- Mark then places Jesus' action in the temple between the cursing and the disciples' later observation that the tree is dried up from the roots.
- Peter remembers Jesus' words. Jesus teaches about faith, prayer, and forgiveness.

## Differences to preserve
Matthew presents the withering in a compressed sequence; Mark separates the spoken judgment from the disciples' later observation and uses the event as a narrative frame around the temple action. Mark explicitly notes that it was not the season for figs. That detail must not be omitted or explained away. The relation between leaves, fruit, season, and prophetic symbolism requires careful treatment.

## What the text states
Jesus speaks to the tree; it withers. Mark says the disciples see it dried from the roots. Jesus uses their response to teach about faith, prayer, and forgiveness. The event is situated near the temple cleansing in Mark.

## Witness and agency
Jesus sees, speaks, and acts with authority. The disciples hear and later observe the result; Peter points it out. Jesus teaches them. The tree is the object of the sign, not a human witness.

## Canonical context
The prophets use fruitless trees and failed harvest imagery in judgment contexts (for example, Jeremiah 8:13; Hosea 9:10, 16; Micah 7:1). These are possible canonical backgrounds, not proof that every detail of the Gospel event is a one-to-one allegory. Mark's placement around the temple cleansing invites investigation of a judgment theme, but the text's symbolic force should be argued from the narrative and wider Scripture rather than assumed. Luke 13:6–9 is a separate parable of a fruitless fig tree and must not be confused with this event.

## What the text does not state
The accounts do not give botanical measurements or a physiological mechanism. They do not say the tree was diseased. The apparent tension between the tree's fruitlessness and Mark's note that it was not the season for figs must remain an explicit interpretive question rather than be suppressed.

## Triple Constraint
- **Biblical compliance — PASS for the recorded sequence; interpretation PARTIAL:** the season note and Mark's temple-frame are preserved; the full symbolic reading remains open.
- **Scientific coherence — PASS for restraint; botanical mechanism UNRESOLVED:** no botanical data are supplied. Avoid invented plant physiology.
- **GUA-W consistency — PASS for governing hierarchy; symbolic placement PARTIAL:** Jesus' authority is preserved; any architectural analogy remains subordinate and unconfirmed.

## Finding
The fig tree account is not simply another healing or provision narrative. It is a judgment sign with a distinctive narrative placement, especially in Mark. The season note is an interpretive control: any explanation must account for it rather than ignore it. Jesus then directs the disciples to faith, prayer, and forgiveness.

**Jesus speaks to the tree. The disciples witness its withering. The sign is placed beside a confrontation in the temple.**
