# Genesis 4:6–7 — Text-First Reconstruction, Unit 9

**Status:** Text-first investigation; the difficult interpretation of Genesis 4:7 remains disputed; observed relationships are considered within the completed GUA-W  
**Date:** 2026-11-16  
**Scope:** KJV Genesis 4:6–7, read in continuity with Genesis 4:1–8 and Genesis 3:16–17  
**Questions:** What does God identify in Cain's condition? What is the relation among sin, the door, desire, and rule in Genesis 4:7? What does the passage establish about Cain's responsibility and the nature of human will?  
**Method:** Preserve the KJV as the base text. Examine Hebrew wording and morphology where material. Distinguish the Masoretic wording, grammatical observations, translation decisions, interpretations, and proposed architectural implications. Do not import a modern philosophical definition of will.

## 1. Prior work and source discipline

This unit follows Unit 8, Genesis 3:1–24, and examines U-006 (human will, still unresolved) and revisits the resolved U-007 distinction (knowledge/evaluation does not transfer authority to establish the standard).

Genesis 4:6–7 must not be detached from its immediate narrative:
- Genesis 4:4–5 describes the LORD's regard for Abel and his offering, but not for Cain and his offering.
- Cain becomes very angry and his face falls (4:5).
- God addresses Cain (4:6–7).
- Cain then speaks to Abel, and later kills him (4:8).

The KJV is the project's base translation. The Masoretic Hebrew text is consulted for lexical and grammatical observations. The NET notes, Sefaria text/commentary, and interlinear/morphological resources are secondary aids. Their interpretive conclusions are not treated as the Hebrew text itself.

## 2. Genesis 4:6 — God addresses Cain's condition

> And the LORD said unto Cain, Why art thou wroth? and why is thy countenance fallen?

### Hebrew text

וַיֹּאמֶר יְהוָה אֶל־קָיִן לָמָּה חָרָה לָךְ וְלָמָּה נָפְלוּ פָנֶיךָ׃

### Word-level observations

- וַיֹּאמֶר (*wayyōʾmer*) — “and [He] said”; Qal wayyiqtol, third-person masculine singular, from אמר (*ʾāmar*), say.
- יְהוָה (*YHWH*) — the LORD; subject of the verb “said.”
- אֶל־קָיִן (*ʾel-Qāyin*) — to Cain.
- לָמָּה (*lāmāh*) — why? The interrogative occurs twice, giving the verse a parallel two-question structure.
- חָרָה (*ḥārāh*) — Qal perfect, third-person masculine singular, from חרָה (*ḥārāh*), burn/be kindled. In this construction, “Why has it burned/been kindled for you?” is idiomatically rendered “Why are you angry?” or “Why are you distressed?”
- לָךְ (*lāḵ*) — to/for you; second-person suffix.
- נָפְלוּ (*nāp̄əlû*) — Qal perfect, third-person plural, from נפל (*nāphal*), fall.
- פָנֶיךָ (*p̄āneḵā*) — your face/countenance. *Pānîm* is grammatically plural in form, hence the plural verb “have fallen,” although it refers to Cain's one countenance.

The first clause uses an idiom of burning/kindling; the Hebrew verb itself does not contain a separate noun meaning “anger.” English “wroth/angry” communicates the contextual sense. Ancient versions differ in how explicitly they render the emotion: the Septuagint uses a grief/sorrow expression, while the Vulgate uses anger. The Hebrew image of the face falling is retained across several renderings.

### What the text states

1. The LORD speaks directly to Cain.
2. God asks why Cain's anger/distress has arisen.
3. God asks why Cain's countenance has fallen.
4. The questions address Cain's observable condition; they do not state that God lacks knowledge of it.

The questions themselves do not yet tell us the full internal process by which Cain reached this condition. The immediate context identifies the occasion: Cain's offering was not regarded as Abel's was, and Cain became very angry (4:4–5).

**Observation:** God addresses Cain before the murder narrated in 4:8. The narrative therefore places divine speech and warning between Cain's anger and his later action.

## 3. Genesis 4:7 — Conditional response, sin at the entrance, desire, and rule

> If thou doest well, shalt thou not be accepted? and if thou doest not well, sin lieth at the door. And unto thee shall be his desire, and thou shalt rule over him.

### Masoretic Hebrew text

הֲלֹא אִם־תֵּיטִיב שְׂאֵת וְאִם־לֹא תֵיטִיב לַפֶּתַח חַטָּאת רֹבֵץ וְאֵלֶיךָ תְּשׁוּקָתוֹ וְאַתָּה תִּמְשָׁל־בּוֹ׃

### Word-level observations

- הֲלֹא (*halōʾ*) — interrogative particle with לֹא; commonly rendered “is it not…?” or as a rhetorical question.
- אִם (*ʾim*) — if.
- תֵּיטִיב (*têṭîḇ*) — Hiphil imperfect, second-person masculine singular, from יָטַב (*yāṭaḇ*): do well, act rightly, do good.
- שְׂאֵת (*śəʾēt*) — a difficult form, generally analyzed as an infinitive construct associated with נשא (*nāśāʾ*), lift/carry. The compact Hebrew clause is elliptical. “Acceptance,” “being lifted up,” or the lifting of Cain's fallen face are interpretive renderings; the precise force is not settled by the one word alone.
- וְאִם־לֹא תֵּיטִיב (*wəʾim-lōʾ têṭîḇ*) — and/ but if you do not do well.
- לַפֶּתַח (*lappetaḥ*) — at/to the door or entrance; פֶּתַח (*petaḥ*) is a masculine singular noun.
- חַטָּאת (*ḥaṭṭāʾt*) — sin; feminine singular noun. The same form can also mean a sin offering in other contexts, but “sin” is the natural sense in this clause in the KJV and common translations.
- רֹבֵץ (*rōḇēṣ*) — Qal active participle, masculine singular, from רבץ (*rāḇaṣ*): crouching, lying down, lying in wait.
- וְאֵלֶיךָ (*wəʾēleḵā*) — and/toward you; second-person masculine singular suffix.
- תְּשׁוּקָתוֹ (*təšûqātō*) — its/his desire; noun תְּשׁוּקָה (*təšûqāh*) with a third-person masculine singular suffix.
- וְאַתָּה (*wəʾattāh*) — and/but you; independent second-person masculine singular pronoun, emphatic in the clause.
- תִּמְשָׁל (*timšāl*) — Qal imperfect, second-person masculine singular, from משל (*māšal*): rule, exercise dominion, have mastery.
- בּוֹ (*bō*) — in/over him or it; preposition בְּ with a third-person masculine singular suffix.

### 3.1 The two conditional clauses

The verse first sets two alternatives:
- If Cain does well: שְׂאֵת (*śəʾēt*), “lifting/acceptance” in the difficult first clause.
- If Cain does not do well: the following image of something crouching at the door.

Both clauses use the same verb תֵּיטִיב (*têṭîḇ*), “you do well,” with the second negated. This parallelism is explicit.

The opening phrase is textually difficult. The NET note reads the first clause as “If you do well, [there is] uplifting,” connecting it to Cain's fallen face. Other translations render the result as acceptance or being accepted. Sefaria's presentation also marks the verse's meaning as uncertain. Therefore, do not treat “accepted” as a mechanically certain one-word equivalent of שְׂאֵת.

**Direct observation:** God presents Cain with a positive and a negative alternative, both framed around whether Cain does well.

### 3.2 “Sin lieth at the door”

The phrase לַפֶּתַח חַטָּאת רֹבֵץ (*lappetaḥ ḥaṭṭāʾt rōḇēṣ*) places a crouching/lying figure at the entrance.

The imagery is vivid: something is at the threshold, crouching or lying in wait. The NET notes interpret the participle as active and understand the image as sin personified like an animal ready to pounce.

**Grammatical issue that must remain visible:** חַטָּאת (*ḥaṭṭāʾt*, “sin”) is feminine singular, while רֹבֵץ (*rōḇēṣ*, “crouching”) is masculine singular. The later suffixes in תְּשׁוּקָתוֹ (*its/his desire*) and בּוֹ (*over it/him*) are also masculine singular.

This mismatch has generated different explanations and translations. Some translations treat sin as personified and retain “it”; the KJV retains masculine “his/him.” Some interpreters take the masculine forms as referring to a masculine croucher/figure, rather than directly agreeing with the feminine noun “sin.” Other proposals supply an implied masculine noun or understand the clause differently. The morphology alone does not settle the referent.

Accordingly:
- It is accurate to report that the KJV personifies sin as lying at the door and uses “his/him” in the final clauses.
- It is not accurate to claim that the Hebrew suffixes grammatically agree in gender with the feminine noun חַטָּאת.
- It is not yet established solely by this mismatch that the subject must be a separate male being, demon, or person.
- The animal/threshold image is an interpretive account of the participle and context, not an independent Hebrew noun meaning “beast” in the verse.

### 3.3 “Unto thee shall be his desire”

תְּשׁוּקָה (*təšûqāh*) occurs in Genesis 3:16 and Genesis 4:7, and also in Song of Solomon 7:10 (English numbering often 7:11).

The shared word in Genesis 3:16 and 4:7 is a strong lexical link. It does not automatically prove that the relationship in the two passages is identical. The referents and contexts differ:
- Genesis 3:16 speaks of the woman's desire toward her husband, followed by his rule over her.
- Genesis 4:7 speaks of the desire of the crouching figure / “it” / “him” toward Cain, followed by Cain's rule over that figure.

Interpretations of *təšûqāh* include desire, longing, urge, or a desire involving control/dominance. The NET notes favor a controlling/dominating sense in both Genesis contexts. Other interpretations exist. The word should not be assigned one narrow definition without examining all its occurrences and context.

**Direct observation:** The verse describes a directional relation: “toward you” is the desire of the masculine-referenced figure/entity.

### 3.4 “And thou shalt rule over him”

תִּמְשָׁל (*timšāl*) is a Qal imperfect, second-person masculine singular, from משל (*māšal*), “rule/have dominion/master.”

The KJV renders it “thou shalt rule.” Other translations render it “you must master it” or “you can be its master.” The Hebrew imperfect form itself does not mechanically encode the English modal distinction between prediction, capacity, and obligation. The force must be assessed from syntax and context.

The independent pronoun וְאַתָּה (*wəʾattāh*, “and/but you”) gives Cain explicit prominence: “but you…” The statement is directed to Cain, not to an abstract humanity in general.

The suffix in בּוֹ (*bō*) is masculine singular. Its referent remains tied to the grammatical and interpretive issue described above.

**Direct observation:** God addresses Cain as the person who is to exercise rule/mastery over the masculine-referenced crouching/desiring figure or entity. The verse does not depict Cain as passive or without responsibility.

**Interpretive boundary:** The verse's wording supports a responsibility/expectation of mastery, but the precise force—command, obligation, assurance of capacity, or prediction—must not be claimed as settled by the imperfect form alone.

## 4. The Genesis 3:16 ↔ Genesis 4:7 correspondence

The two passages share a distinctive sequence:

| Genesis 3:16 | Genesis 4:7 |
|---|---|
| “Unto thy husband” | “Unto thee” |
| תְּשׁוּקָה (*təšûqāh*) — desire | תְּשׁוּקָה (*təšûqāh*) — desire |
| “He shall rule over thee” | “Thou shalt rule over him” |

The parallel is lexical and syntactic, but the roles are reversed:
- Genesis 3:16: the woman → husband; then husband → rule over woman.
- Genesis 4:7: the crouching figure/entity → Cain; then Cain → rule over it/him.

This may illuminate the use of *təšûqāh* as a relational urge or desire that is followed by rule. It does not, by itself, prove that every occurrence means “desire to dominate.” The interpretation must still account for Song of Solomon 7:10/11 and the distinct contexts.

The connection is sufficiently strong to warrant keeping Genesis 3:16 and 4:7 together in the unresolved investigation.

## 5. What Genesis 4:6–7 states about Cain's condition and responsibility

The passage records:
1. Cain is angry/distressed and his face has fallen (4:5–6).
2. God addresses him before the later murder (4:6).
3. God presents a conditional contrast: doing well versus not doing well (4:7).
4. The negative alternative is described with an image of a crouching figure/entity at the door (4:7).
5. That figure/entity has desire toward Cain (4:7).
6. Cain is addressed emphatically as the one to rule/master (4:7).

The passage does **not** explicitly state:
- a formal definition of human will;
- that Cain's capacity to choose is independent of God;
- that desire itself is identical to will;
- that the crouching figure/entity is a demon or a separate male being;
- that the masculine suffixes unambiguously refer to a distinct person;
- that Cain is guaranteed to master the figure/entity regardless of his response;
- that the imperfect verb alone proves either “must” or “can.”

## 6. Relevance to U-006 — Definition and components of human will

Genesis 4:6–7 adds evidence that must be distinguished rather than collapsed:
- **Affective condition:** anger/distress and fallen countenance.
- **Divine address:** God questions Cain and speaks to him before the later action.
- **Conditional conduct:** “if you do well / if you do not do well.”
- **External or personified opposition:** a crouching figure/entity at the door.
- **Desire:** a directional desire toward Cain.
- **Rule/mastery:** Cain is addressed as the one to rule/master.

This passage does not define will. It does, however, prevent a simple model in which a person is only a passive endpoint of desire or external influence. God addresses Cain's responsibility in the presence of a described opposing force.

**Provisional inference:** Human responsibility and the presence of desire/temptation are presented together. The text does not equate them.

## 7. Relevance to U-007 — Knowledge versus authority

Genesis 4:6–7 does not repeat the phrase “know good and evil.” It therefore does not directly settle what kind of knowledge Genesis 3:22 describes.

It does provide a related test:
- God speaks to Cain and sets the evaluative frame of “doing well.”
- Cain is not presented as the author of the standard by which God addresses his conduct.
- Cain is told about the danger at the door and his responsibility in relation to it.

**Provisional observation:** The passage distinguishes God's instruction from Cain's response. It does not describe Cain as having acquired authority to redefine what counts as doing well.

This supports keeping “capacity to evaluate” distinct from “authority to establish the standard,” but it does not by itself complete U-007.

## 9. Unresolved questions retained

1. What is the precise force of שְׂאֵת (*śəʾēt*) in the first clause: uplift, acceptance, lifting of the face, or another related sense?
2. How should the masculine רֹבֵץ (*rōḇēṣ*) and masculine suffixes תְּשׁוּקָתוֹ (*təšûqātō*) and בּוֹ (*bō*) be related to feminine חַטָּאת (*ḥaṭṭāʾt*)?
3. Is the crouching figure a personification of sin, an implied masculine figure, or a construction requiring another explanation?
4. What range of meaning does תְּשׁוּקָה (*təšûqāh*) carry across Genesis 3:16, Genesis 4:7, and Song of Solomon 7:10/11?
5. Does תִּמְשָׁל (*timšāl*) function here as obligation, command, prediction, or an assertion of capacity?
6. What does Genesis 4:7 contribute to a Scriptural definition of will, responsibility, desire, and mastery?
7. How does Cain's response in Genesis 4:8 test the warning and responsibility stated in 4:6–7?
