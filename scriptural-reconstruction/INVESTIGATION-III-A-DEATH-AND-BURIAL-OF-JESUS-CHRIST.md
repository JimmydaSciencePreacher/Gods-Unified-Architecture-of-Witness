# Investigation III.A — The Death and Burial of Jesus Christ

**Primary texts:** Matthew 27:32–66; Mark 15:21–47; Luke 23:26–56; John 19:16–42 (KJV)  
**Related texts:** Psalm 22; Psalm 34:20; Isaiah 52:13–53:12; Zechariah 12:10; 1 Corinthians 15:3–5; Romans 6:3–4; Acts 13:28–30  
**Status:** Comparative Scriptural examination written; historical and medical assessment remains bounded and open.

## 1. Investigative question

What do the four Gospel accounts record concerning the crucifixion, death, confirmation of death, release of Jesus' body, and burial—and what can historical, archaeological, and medical evidence responsibly contribute to examining that testimony?

This examination is foundational to the central Resurrection investigation. It does not attempt to prove the Resurrection by proving the death. It establishes and examines the preceding events as the Gospel accounts present them.

## 2. The Crucifixion: shared testimony and distinct details

All four Gospels record Jesus' crucifixion under Roman authority at the place called Golgotha / Calvary, with two others crucified alongside Him (Matthew 27:32–38; Mark 15:21–27; Luke 23:26–33; John 19:16–18).

Details to preserve:

- **Simon of Cyrene:** Matthew, Mark, and Luke record Simon being compelled to carry the cross. John 19:17 says Jesus went forth bearing His cross. These statements should be read in their own narrative contexts; one must not silently erase either report.
- **The place:** Matthew, Mark, and John use Golgotha; Luke gives Calvary. John explains Golgotha as the place of a skull.
- **The inscription:** All four record the charge/title associated with Jesus as King of the Jews, while the wording and presentation differ. John 19:19–22 gives the fullest account of Pilate's written title and the dispute about its wording.
- **The garments:** The Synoptics record the soldiers dividing Jesus' garments and casting lots; John 19:23–24 gives a more detailed description of the seamless coat and the soldiers' decision not to rend it.
- **The spectators and mockery:** Each account records mockery or derision, with differing speakers and details.
- **The two crucified men:** Matthew and Mark record both men reviling Jesus; Luke records one rebuking the other and addressing Jesus; John simply records two others crucified with Him. Preserve the narrative differences rather than harmonizing them by omission.

## 3. Jesus' words and the recorded death

The four accounts preserve different sayings and details from the cross:

- **Matthew 27:46–50:** Jesus cries, “Eli, Eli, lama sabachthani?” and then cries again with a loud voice before yielding up the ghost.
- **Mark 15:34–37:** Mark records the corresponding cry and says Jesus gave up the ghost.
- **Luke 23:34, 43, 46:** Luke records Jesus' words concerning forgiveness, the promise to the repentant malefactor, and His final entrusting of His spirit to the Father.
- **John 19:26–30:** John records Jesus addressing His mother and the beloved disciple, saying “I thirst,” and declaring, “It is finished,” before bowing His head and giving up the ghost.

**TEXT STATES:** Each Gospel presents Jesus as actually dying. John 19:30 expressly records that He gave up the ghost. Matthew 27:50 and Mark 15:37 record the same outcome. Luke 23:46 records His final words and death.

**CANONICAL OBSERVATION:** John 19:30's “It is finished” is a completion declaration in the Passion narrative. Its relation to the project's three completion witnesses (Genesis 2:1–5; John 19:30; Revelation 21:6) may be recognized in the established canonical comparison, while the passages remain distinct in wording, event, and immediate context. This death-and-burial study does not redefine or add to that established comparison.

## 4. Confirmation of death: the details that must not be overlooked

### Mark 15:42–45

Mark records Joseph of Arimathaea going boldly to Pilate and asking for the body of Jesus. Pilate marvels that Jesus is already dead, calls the centurion, and asks whether He has been any while dead. After receiving the centurion's confirmation, Pilate grants the body to Joseph.

This detail is significant within Mark's own account: the Roman governor does not simply release the body without inquiry; the narrative records a check with the centurion responsible for the execution.

### John 19:31–37

John records that the Jews sought to have the legs of the crucified men broken so that their bodies would not remain on the crosses during the sabbath. The soldiers break the legs of the two others. When they come to Jesus, they see that He is already dead and do not break His legs. One soldier pierces His side with a spear, and John records that blood and water came forth.

John then explicitly connects these details with Scripture:

- “A bone of him shall not be broken” (John 19:36; compare Exodus 12:46; Numbers 9:12; Psalm 34:20).
- “They shall look on him whom they pierced” (John 19:37; compare Zechariah 12:10).

**TEXT STATES:** Mark records Pilate's inquiry to the centurion. John records the soldiers' observation that Jesus was already dead, the decision not to break His legs, and the piercing of His side.

**TEXTUAL LIMIT:** John reports what came forth from Jesus' side but does not provide a modern clinical interpretation, laboratory analysis, or a diagnosis of the cause of death. Those must not be inserted into the text.

## 5. The burial: account-by-account comparison

### Matthew 27:57–61

- Joseph of Arimathaea is described as a rich man and a disciple of Jesus.
- He goes to Pilate and asks for Jesus' body.
- Pilate commands that the body be delivered.
- Joseph wraps the body in clean linen.
- He lays it in his own new tomb, hewn out of the rock.
- He rolls a great stone to the door and departs.
- Mary Magdalene and the other Mary are present, sitting over against the sepulchre.

Matthew 27:62–66 then separately records the chief priests and Pharisees approaching Pilate, recalling Jesus' prediction, and requesting that the sepulchre be secured. Pilate authorizes a guard; they seal the stone and set a watch.

### Mark 15:42–47

- The burial occurs when evening has come, on the preparation day before the sabbath.
- Joseph is described as an honourable counsellor, waiting for the kingdom of God.
- He goes boldly to Pilate and asks for Jesus' body.
- Pilate first checks with the centurion that Jesus is dead.
- Joseph buys fine linen, takes Jesus down, wraps Him, and lays Him in a sepulchre hewn out of a rock.
- He rolls a stone to the door.
- Mary Magdalene and Mary the mother of Joses observe where He is laid.

Mark uniquely records Pilate's surprise that Jesus had died so soon and the inquiry to the centurion.

### Luke 23:50–56

- Joseph is a counsellor, described as a good and just man.
- Luke says Joseph had not consented to the counsel and deed of the others and was waiting for the kingdom of God.
- He goes to Pilate, asks for Jesus' body, takes it down, and wraps it in linen.
- Jesus is laid in a sepulchre hewn in stone, “wherein never man before was laid.”
- The preparation day is ending and the sabbath draws on.
- The women who came with Jesus from Galilee follow, behold the sepulchre, and see how His body was laid.
- They return and prepare spices and ointments, then rest on the sabbath according to the commandment.

Luke uniquely states that Joseph did not consent to the council's decision and explicitly says the tomb had never before held a body. Luke also records the women's observation of the burial and their preparation of spices before resting on the sabbath.

### John 19:38–42

- Joseph of Arimathaea is described as a disciple of Jesus, but secretly for fear of the Jews.
- He asks Pilate for permission to take Jesus' body; Pilate gives permission.
- Nicodemus, previously having come to Jesus by night, brings a mixture of myrrh and aloes, about an hundred pound weight.
- They take Jesus' body and wind it in linen clothes with the spices, “as the manner of the Jews is to bury.”
- In the place where Jesus was crucified there is a garden, and in the garden a new sepulchre wherein was never man yet laid.
- Because the sepulchre was nigh at hand, they lay Jesus there on the preparation day.

John uniquely names Nicodemus, gives the quantity and kind of spices, describes the garden, and explicitly locates the new tomb near the place of crucifixion.

## 6. Differences and shared details to preserve

| Detail | Gospel testimony |
|---|---|
| Joseph requests the body | All four Gospels |
| Pilate authorizes release | All four Gospels; Mark adds the centurion inquiry |
| Joseph's description | Rich man and disciple (Matthew); honourable counsellor (Mark); good and just counsellor who dissented (Luke); secret disciple (John) |
| Nicodemus | John alone |
| Linen wrapping | All four |
| Tomb cut/hewn in rock or stone | All four |
| New / unused tomb | Matthew, Luke, John explicitly |
| Stone at entrance | Matthew, Mark, Luke, and the later tomb discovery in John |
| Women observe the burial/place | All four in differing details |
| Garden location | John |
| Spices prepared or used | Luke records women's preparation; John records Nicodemus' spices at burial |
| Guard and seal | Matthew alone |

**Important distinction:** The Gospel accounts are not identical in detail, but each records Joseph's involvement in receiving and burying Jesus' body. The differences must be investigated honestly. They do not authorize us to invent missing dialogue or an exact minute-by-minute sequence.

## 7. Historical and archaeological examination

Historical evidence is relevant here because the account describes a Roman execution and a Jewish burial in first-century Judea. It must be used carefully and must not be made to say more than it can.

### Roman crucifixion and disposal of bodies

John Granger Cook's peer-reviewed study, “Crucifixion and Burial” (*New Testament Studies*, 2011), examines Greco-Roman evidence and concludes that the treatment of crucified bodies was not governed by one universal outcome: some bodies were left exposed, while in other cases bodies were buried. Provincial officials had discretion in particular circumstances.

Mark D. Smith's historical analysis, “The Final Days of Jesus and the Realities of Roman Capital Punishment” (2018), likewise argues that Roman treatment of executed bodies was situational. His discussion of the Yehohanan find notes that a crucified man was buried in a customary Jewish family tomb. This establishes that burial of a crucified person was possible; it does not establish that every crucified person received burial or independently prove the details of Jesus' burial.

**Finding:** It is historically inaccurate to claim that Roman practice made burial of a crucified person categorically impossible. It is equally inaccurate to claim that every crucified person was routinely granted an honourable tomb burial. Context and discretion matter.

### Archaeological evidence: Yehohanan

The remains discovered at Giv'at ha-Mivtar in Jerusalem provide archaeological evidence of a man who had been crucified and was subsequently buried. An iron nail remained through the heel bone. The case demonstrates that at least one crucifixion victim received Jewish burial in a rock-cut tomb.

**Limit:** Yehohanan is not Jesus. The find does not establish Jesus' exact posture, wounds, method of attachment, duration on the cross, cause of death, or burial circumstances. It is contextual evidence for crucifixion and burial practices, not direct archaeological evidence for Jesus' body or tomb.

### Recent scholarship on the Gospel burial accounts

Tim Carter's 2026 article, “A Tomb Fit for a Prophet,” argues that an honourable rock-hewn tomb burial for a crucifixion victim is historically plausible, while challenging aspects of the traditional reconstruction of Joseph's motives and role. The article proposes a particular social-memory explanation for differences in the Gospel accounts.

That proposal is an interpretation, not an established fact. It should be compared with other scholarship and with the actual Gospel wording. In particular, the article's theory about Joseph's possible motives or a possible payment to Pilate is not stated in the KJV text and must not be promoted into a Scriptural finding.

## 8. Medical examination and its limits

Maslen and Mitchell's 2006 review, “Medical theories on the cause of death in crucifixion,” published in the *Journal of the Royal Society of Medicine*, surveys more than forty medical publications and identifies numerous competing proposed causes of death, including cardiovascular, respiratory, metabolic, and other hypotheses.

The authors observe that the wide range of proposed explanations reflects the lack of clear evidence for selecting one precise mechanism. They also caution that the details of crucifixion could vary by region, period, victim, and circumstance.

**Scientific finding:** The precise physiological mechanism of Jesus' death cannot responsibly be declared from the Gospel descriptions alone. A confident diagnosis such as asphyxiation, cardiac rupture, hypovolaemic shock, or another single cause goes beyond the available direct evidence.

**Boundary:** Uncertainty over the precise mechanism is not uncertainty about what the Gospel narratives explicitly report: Jesus died. Medical speculation must not overwrite the text, and the text must not be misrepresented as a modern medical report.

## 9. Witness, agency, and registration

The accounts distinguish several roles:

- **Jesus:** undergoes crucifixion and gives up His life; John 10:17–18 supplies His prior declaration concerning laying down His life.
- **Roman authority:** Pilate authorizes the execution and later the release of the body; Mark records the centurion's confirmation.
- **Soldiers:** execute the crucifixion, observe Jesus already dead, refrain from breaking His legs, and one pierces His side according to John.
- **Joseph of Arimathaea:** requests and receives the body and participates in the burial.
- **Nicodemus:** brings burial spices and assists in preparing the body according to John.
- **Women followers:** observe the burial place and, in Luke, prepare spices after seeing where and how the body is laid.
- **Gospel writers:** preserve testimony with distinct details and theological emphases.

**Witness distinction:** Those who observe, confirm, receive, prepare, and bury the body do not cause the death or author the event. Their actions and testimony are recorded as responses to what has occurred.

## 10. Canonical context

The Gospel writers themselves connect the Passion details with Scripture. John explicitly cites the unbroken bones and the piercing (John 19:36–37). The wider canonical record includes:

- Psalm 22 — suffering, mockery, and the division of garments in the Passion narratives.
- Psalm 34:20 — the preservation of bones, applied in John 19:36.
- Isaiah 52:13–53:12 — the suffering servant, whose death and burial are part of the passage's prophetic witness.
- Zechariah 12:10 — the one who is pierced, cited in John 19:37.
- 1 Corinthians 15:3–5 — Paul places Christ's death, burial, Resurrection, and appearances together in the received proclamation.
- Acts 13:28–30 — Paul recounts the condemnation, fulfilment of what was written, laying in a sepulchre, and God's raising Jesus from the dead.

These are canonical relationships explicitly made by the New Testament or arising from direct comparison of the cited passages. They are not a license to assign unrecorded physical mechanisms.

## 11. Relationship to the completed GUA-W

The completed GUA-W remains unchanged. The investigation recognizes the relationship already governed by:

**Author → Word → Creation → Witness**

The Passion account records the Word made flesh entering death in the history of Creation. The witnesses register the death, the release of the body, and the burial. The later Resurrection testimony reports what follows.

The distinction between event and witness is important: the centurion's confirmation, the soldiers' actions, Joseph's request, Nicodemus' preparation, and the women's observation are not the cause of Jesus' death. They are distinct acts and records surrounding the event.

No new architecture layer, runtime stage, or Gear is proposed by this comparison. The established architecture is not being built from these events. The investigation records the canonical relationships and preserves the exact limits of what the text says.

## 12. Triple Constraint Law

### Biblical compliance — PASS

All four Gospel accounts explicitly record the Crucifixion, death, and burial. Mark and John provide specific details concerning confirmation of death. The accounts' differences are preserved rather than concealed. The canonical cross-references are identified in context.

### Scientific coherence — PASS within stated limits; exact mechanism UNRESOLVED

Roman crucifixion and burial practices provide historical context. Archaeological evidence demonstrates that at least one crucified man in first-century Jerusalem received Jewish burial. Scholarship indicates that Roman disposal of bodies could vary by circumstance. Medical literature does not establish a single definitive mechanism for Jesus' death. No unsupported diagnosis is asserted.

### GUA-W consistency — PASS at the governing level

The record preserves God as Original Cause, Jesus Christ's unique identity, and the derivative role of witnesses. It does not make witnesses the cause of the event, reduce Christ to an architectural component, or propose a new stage in the completed GUA-W.

## 13. Findings

1. **The four Gospels record Jesus' crucifixion and death.** They differ in details but do not present the event as an apparent death or an escape before death.
2. **Mark records an additional confirmation step.** Pilate checks with the centurion before releasing the body.
3. **John records the soldiers' observation and actions.** They see Jesus is already dead, do not break His legs, and one pierces His side.
4. **All four Gospels record Joseph of Arimathaea's role in receiving and burying Jesus' body.**
5. **The burial accounts preserve meaningful distinct details.** Nicodemus, the spices, the unused tomb, the garden, the women's observation, and Matthew's guard account must remain attributed to the witnesses who record them.
6. **Historical context does not make burial impossible.** Roman practice varied; a crucified man buried in a Jewish tomb is archaeologically attested, though that find is not Jesus.
7. **The precise medical cause of death remains unresolved.** Competing medical hypotheses do not justify choosing one as established fact.
8. **The burial is a necessary part of the Resurrection proclamation.** Paul explicitly includes it in 1 Corinthians 15:3–5.

## 14. Unresolved questions

1. What can be established about the historical identity and role of Joseph of Arimathaea without importing speculative motives?
2. How should the differences in Joseph's description across the four Gospels be evaluated?
3. What can first-century Jewish burial archaeology establish about the kind of tomb described?
4. What is the best-supported account of Roman discretion concerning the bodies of crucifixion victims in Judea under Pilate?
5. What can the medical literature establish about the possible physiology of crucifixion, and what remains unknowable in this particular case?
6. What is the precise relationship between John's report of blood and water and the historical/medical questions raised about the piercing? The text's statement must be distinguished from later clinical interpretations.
7. How do the women’s observations of the burial place relate to their later visits to the tomb, account by account?

## 15. Provisional disposition

**Scriptural death and burial testimony:** WRITTEN.  
**Historical context:** PARTIAL; evidence supports the possibility of burial after crucifixion, with important qualifications.  
**Medical mechanism:** UNRESOLVED.  
**GUA-W consistency:** PASS at governing level.

This study establishes the preceding death and burial testimony for the central Resurrection investigation. It does not claim to have completed the historical case or to have explained the Resurrection.

**The text records the death. The body is released. The body is prepared and buried. The women observe where it is laid. The Resurrection investigation now proceeds from that recorded burial—not from an assumed or omitted one.**
